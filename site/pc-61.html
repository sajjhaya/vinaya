    Pc 61

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time Venerable Udāyī was skilled in archery. And because he disliked crows, he shot them. He cut off their heads and then set them out in a row impaled on stakes.1

    The monks asked him, "Who killed these crows?" — "I did. I don't like crows." The monks of few desires complained and criticised him: "How can Venerable Udāyī intentionally kill living beings?" They told the Buddha, who questioned Udāyī and rebuked him: "Foolish man, how can you do this? This will affect people's confidence." A monk who intentionally kills a living being commits an offence entailing confession.2

    Synopsis — Udāyī the archer shot crows because he disliked them, and displayed their heads in a row on stakes. The rule that follows makes the deliberate killing of any animal an offence.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk intentionally kills a living being, he commits an offense entailing confession."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu intentionally causes the death of a living being, he incurs a pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu intentionally deprive an animal of life, it is to be confessed."5

    Brahm

    "Deliberately depriving an animal of life is an offence of Pācittiya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should intentionally deprive a living being of life, [this is a case] involving expiation."7

    The renderings agree on what is forbidden. They differ in what they let the reader see. The Pāli is the opening clause of Pj 3's rule word for word with one noun changed: pāṇaṁ, "a living being", where Pj 3 has manussaviggahaṁ, "a human being".8 Brahmāli's recitation line keeps the parallel visible, since "intentionally causes the death of" is the wording his recitation gives both rules, and Ñāṇatusita keeps it too, referring the reader to his Pj 3 entry for the phrase.9​ 10 Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm write the word-analysis's definition, "an animal", into the rule itself.11​ 12​ 13 Neither choice changes the rule. The exclusion of human beings is the Vibhaṅga's own definition, and how small an animal falls within it is taken up below, under Object.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule protects a monk's harmlessness toward animals, and the confidence of the people who see how he treats them. Udāyī shot crows for no better reason than that he disliked them, and set their heads out in a row on stakes; the Buddha's rebuke was that this "will affect people's confidence".14 The rule's wording is the opening of Pj 3's with one noun changed, and it asks of a monk, toward any animal, the restraint that rule asks of him toward a human being.

    Thanissaro gives five factors: object, a living animal; perception, that one perceives it as one; intention, that one knowingly, consciously, deliberately and purposefully wants its death; effort, whatever one does to that end; and result, that it dies.15 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual lists four, none of them an effort or a result, which on my reading the rule's verb carries; the difference changes nothing.16 I follow Thanissaro's five.

    Object

    The Vibhaṅga says only that a living being here means an animal.17 The lower limit is the Commentary's smallest case, a bed bug's egg, as Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm both report it, and the Manual reads that as "any visible animal/insect".18​ 19​ 20 Thanissaro reaches the same limit from what the Canon allows: the medicines of the Khandhaka include substances that act on bacteria, so beings too small to see apparently are not within the rule.21 Ajahn Brahm reaches it from what the first monks could have known.22 A monk may take his medicines. Size makes no difference to the penalty, though it does to the act: Thanissaro notes from the Commentary that the animal's size is among the things that set the moral gravity of a killing.23

    Perception

    If it is a living being and he perceives it as one, the pācittiya; if he is unsure, an offence of wrong conduct; if he does not perceive it as a living being at all, no offence.24 Thanissaro gives the pair of cases the grid invites. Stepping with murderous intent on a spot of dirt one takes for a bed bug's egg is an offence of wrong conduct, and stepping on bed bug eggs one takes for dirt is no offence.25

    Intention

    This is the factor that decides the rule. "Intentionally" is defined as at Pj 3: knowing, perceiving, having intended and having decided, he transgresses.26 Thanissaro reads the four terms through the Commentary to that rule and concludes that the factor is fulfilled only by a clear and consciously made decision to end the animal's life.27 And so the rule does not reach the ordinary harm of monastic life. A monk who sweeps a path carefully and kills ants anyway commits no offence, even if he knew that some might die.28 Here this rule parts from Pc 62, whose monk need not aim at any death: it is enough that he knows the water he uses holds living beings. Motive, though, is not intention. To kill an animal to put it out of its misery fulfils the factor all the same, as Thanissaro and the Concise BMC both say.29​ 30 The Vibhaṅga defines the killing as cutting off the life faculty and destroying its continuity, and nothing in that asks whether the death is meant as a kindness.31

    Effort

    The Vibhaṅga does not list the ways of killing; Thanissaro gives the Commentary's direction to infer them from Pj 3, where they are one's own body, throwing, a stationary device such as a trap or poison, and commanding another, with magic and psychic power added by the Commentary to that rule.32 Getting someone else to kill is therefore within the rule, and the Concise BMC lists it as killing "by commanding or suggesting to someone".33 Ajahn Brahm thought the texts silent on the point and reached the same answer by analogy with Pj 3; his example is telling a layman to spray mosquitoes with insecticide.34 The Khandhaka gives a real case: a monk let a lay follower know he had a use for a calf's hide, the calf was killed, and the Buddha ruled that one "shouldn't make others kill."35 Thanissaro draws from it that there is no room here for an indirect hint: since the monk gave no command and was still brought under the rule, "Whatever one says in hopes of inciting someone else to kill an animal would fulfill this factor."36 The passage itself names no rule; it says only that he should be dealt with according to the rule, and the identification with this one is Thanissaro's.37

    Result

    The pācittiya falls only if the animal dies, and the Vibhaṅga sets no penalty for a failed attempt.38 Thanissaro fills the gap from Pj 3's case of a pit dug so that whatever falls in will die, where an animal's fall and its pain each carry an offence of wrong conduct, and its death the pācittiya. He generalises it, and says so: an offence of wrong conduct for the first effort that touches the animal, another if it suffers pain, and the full offence if it dies.39​ 40

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The first four clauses are four ways of not meeting the intention: the act is unintentional, or done without mindfulness, or without knowing, or without aiming at death.41 Thanissaro gives each a case — a dropped load that crushes a cat; absent-mindedly rubbing an arm while mosquitoes bite; stepping on an insect in a dark room; a dose of medicine a sick dog cannot bear.42 Ajahn Brahm marks where the plea stops, with the Thai fisherman who says he does not kill the fish, he only takes them out of the water and they die by themselves; that "is stretching 'unintentionally' a little too far."43 That the rule stops at the aim of death does not mean the care stops there. Thanissaro cites the Commentary's instruction that a monk who notices even bed bug eggs while cleaning a bed take care not to damage them, "out of compassion", and Ajahn Brahm asks that even a blameless killing be avoided "by more careful attention in future".44​ 45 The case a monk is most likely to meet is pests in a dwelling. Brahmāli's counsel in class was to keep them out, not kill them: a termite barrier is "no problem because it just stops them", and so is a chemical that only turns them back, while one that kills them is to be avoided wherever it possibly can be.46 Asking a lay supporter to do the killing is no way round the rule, for that is the command already met under Effort.47

    Every monk hears this rule, in substance, on the day he is ordained. Among the four things he is told never to do is this one: "A monk who's fully ordained shouldn't intentionally kill a living being, not even a small insect."48

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — killing a living being while unsure whether it is one.49
    • Dukkaṭa — killing what is not a living being while perceiving it as one, or being unsure.50
    • Dukkaṭa — on Thanissaro's generalisation of the Pj 3 pit, for the first effort that touches the animal, and another if it suffers pain.51​ 52
    • Pārājika, thullaccaya — beyond this rule: intentionally killing a human being, and killing a spirit, ghost or animal in human form.53

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if it is unintentional; if he is not mindful; if he does not know; if he is not aiming at death; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.54

    Related Offences

    • Pj 3 — intentionally killing a human being: the rule whose opening clause this one repeats with one noun changed, and to which the Commentary refers its whole analysis.55​ 56
    • Pc 20, 62 — pouring out, or using, water one knows contains living beings: the rules that do not require the aim of death.

    Related Suttas

    • Dhp 129–130 — every creature fears violence and death, as one does oneself: the ground of the harmlessness this rule asks toward any animal.57
    • Snp 1.8 — goodwill toward "whatever living beings there are", the large and the fine alike; the rule draws no line by size either, nor by whether the monk likes the creature, and Udāyī shot crows because he disliked them.58
    • AN 10.176 — one who abstains from killing "with the rod and weapon laid aside", where Udāyī was an archer who took his up against animals.59

The commentaries are known here only as Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm report them. The class is cited for one modern case, the pests in a dwelling; its other points are carried by Thanissaro.
