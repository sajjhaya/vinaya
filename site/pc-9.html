    Pc 9

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī, in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time Venerable Upananda the Sakyan was quarrelling with the monks from the group of six. In the course of this he committed an offence of intentionally emitting semen, and asked the Saṅgha for probation for it, which he received.

    Soon afterwards an association in Sāvatthī was offering a meal to the Saṅgha. Because Upananda was on probation, he sat on the last seat in the dining hall. The monks from the group of six saw an opportunity and told the lay followers there, "This Venerable Upananda, the esteemed associate of your families, eats the food given in faith with the same hand he uses to masturbate. After committing an offence of intentional emission of semen, he asked the Saṅgha for probation for that offence, which he received. And because he's on probation, he now sits on the last seat."

    The monks of few desires heard of this and complained: "How could the monks from the group of six tell a person who's not fully ordained about a monk's grave offence?" They told the Buddha, who asked the group of six whether it was true. They admitted it was. The Buddha rebuked them, and laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — After Venerable Upananda is placed on probation for a sexual offence and made to sit at the foot of the dining hall, the group of six monks explain why to the assembled laypeople, and are rebuked for disclosing a fellow monk's grave offence to the unordained.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk tells a person who is not fully ordained about a monk's grave offense, except if the monks have agreed, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu tells an unordained person about a bhikkhu's serious offence, except with the Saṅgha's agreement, he incurs a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu report (another) bhikkhu's serious offense to an unordained person — unless authorized by the bhikkhus — it is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Informing laypeople or novices of another monk's serious offence, except with the Sangha's permission, is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should declare the depraved offence of [another] bhikkhu to one who has not been fully admitted [into the bhikkhu-community], except with the authorisation of bhikkhus, [this is a case] involving expiation."6

    The five renderings agree, and differ only on the word for the offence, duṭṭhulla: "grave" in Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga, "serious" in his recitation and Manual and for Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm, "depraved" for Ñāṇatusita, who keeps closest to the word's sense of corruption. Nothing hangs on the choice, since the Vibhaṅga fixes the meaning by definition; the word itself is discussed at Sg 3.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A fellow monk's lapse belongs to the Saṅgha that can deal with it, and the rule keeps it there, so that lay people can go on trusting those they support. The group of six used Upananda's probation as a weapon: he sat in the last seat because he was making amends, and they told the lay supporters why, in the coarsest words they could find.7 Thanissaro reports the Vinaya-mukha as taking the rule's purpose to be keeping monks from advertising one another's faults among people outside the Saṅgha.8

    For Thanissaro the offence has two factors: object, a serious offence committed by another monk, and effort, reporting it to an unordained person without the Saṅgha's authorisation.9

    Object

    The Vibhaṅga defines a grave offence as "the four offenses entailing expulsion and the thirteen entailing suspension".10 Buddhaghosa, in Thanissaro's report, gives the ancient commentaries' narrower reading, saṅghādisesa only; Ajahn Brahm adds its reason, that one who has committed a pārājika is no longer a monk; Thanissaro finds that Buddhaghosa himself undoes his two arguments for it, and follows the Vibhaṅga.11​ 12 Ajahn Brahm suggests what the pārājika could still mean here: an ex-monk's pārājika, or an accusation of one the Saṅgha has not yet settled.13 To tell of a lesser offence is a dukkaṭa, and so is telling of a lay person's misconduct, grave or minor; Thanissaro marks that last dukkaṭa as easily overlooked, for it lets lay people seek a monk's advice without fear that he will talk.14​ 15

    Perception does not excuse, though it does not follow the pattern set out at Pc 4. A grave offence reported is the full offence whether he took it as grave, minor or uncertain; a minor one is a dukkaṭa however he took it.16​ 17

    Effort

    The person told is anyone but a fully ordained monk or nun, "a woman or a man, a householder or a monastic".18 To fulfil the factor he must tell both the action and the class of the offence. "Ven. Upananda committed a saṅghādisesa by masturbating" is the full offence; either half alone is no offence at all.19​ 20 Brahmāli finds the split too fine today, since with the Vinaya in print the action alone tells many lay people the class; he calls it "too finicky", "trying to split hairs".21​ 22 Ajahn Brahm adds that naming the class alone shames a monk, and would make it a pācittiya.23 Thanissaro takes the allowance as written, made apparently for the lay person who asks why a senior monk sits at the end of the line, and warns that a monk who uses it to discredit a fellow monk incurs a dukkaṭa under Pc 13.24

    The Saṅgha's agreement. The monks may agree to disclosure limited to certain offences, to certain families, to both, or to neither, and to go beyond the limit set is the full offence.25 The texts do not say how the agreement is given, and the Commentary, by Thanissaro's report, recommends an announcement made three times and agreed to unanimously.26 In his report the Vinaya-mukha reads it as meant for a monk who commits a serious offence and will not acknowledge it, his supporters informed to press him to submit to his penalty; Ajahn Brahm thinks of the act of information once made public about Devadatta, and judges the allowance unlikely to be used.27​ 28 Thanissaro warns that, used to press a monk who will not submit, it can backfire, the laity concluding only that the Saṅgha is jealous of the support they give.29 To tell a monk or nun of another's offence is no offence at all, and to conceal a serious one from them knowingly is itself an offence, under Pc 64.30

    What, then, does a monk say when a lay supporter asks why a monk is sitting at the end of the line? Brahmāli answers as the rule implies: it is an internal matter of the Saṅgha, the rehabilitation is not the laity's business, and that is all that needs to be said.31

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • A dukkaṭa for telling an unordained person of a monk's minor offence.32
    • A dukkaṭa for telling of an unordained person's misconduct, grave or minor.33
    • A dukkaṭa under Pc 13 for using the action-or-class allowance to discredit a fellow monk.34

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he tells the action that was the basis for the offence but not the class, or the class but not the action; if the monks have agreed, within the limit they set; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.35

    Related Offences

    • Sg 8, Sg 9 — falsely charging a monk with a pārājika: this rule governs disclosing a real offence, those a charge that is groundless.
    • Pc 8 — the neighbouring rule on telling the unordained: there one's own genuine attainment, here another's genuine offence.
    • Pc 13 — where using this rule's allowance to discredit a fellow monk is a dukkaṭa.36
    • Pc 64 — knowingly concealing a monk's serious offence from other monks, the obligation that runs the other way.37
    • Sg 3 — where duṭṭhulla, this rule's "grave", is discussed.

    Related Suttas

    • MN 103 — when a monk "might commit an offence or a transgression", the others "should not hurry to reprove him", but first see whether they can speak without hurt and help him out of the unwholesome: a lapse dealt with among the monks.38
