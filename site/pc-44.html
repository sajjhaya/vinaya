    Pc 44

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. Venerable Upananda the Sakyan went to the house of a friend and sat down in private on a concealed seat with his wife.

    The husband complained and criticised him, "How could Venerable Upananda sit down in private on a concealed seat with my wife?"

    The monks heard the complaints of that man. The monks of few desires complained and criticised Upananda, and the Buddha questioned him, established that it was true, rebuked him, and stated the rule.1​ 2

    Synopsis — Upananda visits a friend and sits with the man's wife somewhere screened from view. The husband finds them and complains; the rule that follows is the third of five consecutive rules with the same offender, and the first in a pair that between them divide the field of privacy in two.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk sits down in private on a concealed seat with a woman, he commits an offense entailing confession."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu, aiming at privacy, sits in private with one or more women in a secluded place, he incurs a pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu sit in private on a secluded seat with a woman, it is to be confessed."5

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Sitting or lying down in a concealed place alone with woman is an offence of Pācittiya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should take seat with a woman, privately, on a concealed seat, [this is a case] involving expiation."7

    None of the rule's words is contested. Ñāṇatusita refers every one of them to his Aniyata 1 entry and adds nothing here, and that is where this rule's vocabulary lives.8 What the renderings differ over is how much of the rule to put in the line. Brahmāli's recitation line adds two things the Pāli does not carry: "aiming at privacy", from the non-offence clauses, and "one or more women", from his Pātimokkha Manual's reading of the word-analysis, which Thanissaro shares.9​ 10​ 11 Both are true of the rule as applied. Ajahn Brahm's line says "alone with woman", which the rule does not require, and his own commentary says the opposite; the point is taken up under Object.12​ 13

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A monk seated with a woman where no one can see them has left his celibate life, and his good name among the laity, in the keeping of a screen, and this rule guards both by keeping him from the screened seat. The origin story is four sentences long: Upananda sat with his friend's wife on a concealed seat, and it was the husband who complained, "How could Venerable Upananda sit down in private on a concealed seat with my wife?"14 The rule was laid down once, with no amendment. It is the first of a pair: this rule covers a seat that is both private and concealed, and Pc 45 a place that is private but open to view.

    Thanissaro gives three factors: object, effort and intention.15 The Manual also counts three, the woman and two exceptions: another knowledgeable man present, and not aiming at privacy.16 Thanissaro's division is followed here, since the knowledgeable man belongs with the place he is watching, and his three factors are taken in his order.

    Object

    The object is a woman, and the definition is at its widest here: any human female, down to "a girl born on that very day".17 There is no threshold of age or understanding. The next rule defines the same word narrowly, as a woman able to tell decent speech from indecent, and the difference between the two definitions is much of the reason there are two rules; it is taken up there.18

    The singular does not mean one. Sitting with many women is no exemption, as Thanissaro and the Manual both say.19​ 20 Ajahn Brahm, whose rule line says "alone", agrees in his commentary, and offers a reason with a hedge: a second woman was presumably no guarantee against gossip.21 Here the rule parts from Pc 45, whose "one with one" makes any third person enough (at Aniyata 1 the same words are disputed), a difference taken up at Pc 45. A paṇḍaka, a female spirit, a female ghost and an animal in the form of a woman are grounds for an offence of wrong conduct. Perception mitigates nothing: where the person is a woman, every perception carries the full pācittiya, the pattern of Pc 4.22​ 23

    Effort

    The effort is sitting with her in a place that is both private and concealed, and these are two requirements. In private is defined twice over: private to the eye, where no one can see the two of them wink or nod, and private to the ear, where no one can hear ordinary speech. A concealed seat is one screened by a wall, a door, a cloth, a tree, a pillar or anything else.24 Thanissaro adds the test the aniyatas supply, a seat that would "afford them enough privacy to commit the sexual act". Of the two kinds of privacy, he reports the Commentary making sight the one that decides: a man within hearing but not within sight, sitting just outside the door, does not take the seat out of the rule.25

    Sitting includes lying down, and it makes no difference who arrives second: the monk may sit or lie beside the seated woman, she beside him, or both at once.26 Standing is outside the rule: the rule says "sits", and the non-offence clauses name standing as no offence.27

    What removes the offence is a man. The non-offence clause exempts a monk with a male companion who understands, one who knows what is and is not lewd.28​ 29 Thanissaro reports the Commentary making him a possible witness in the strict sense: he must be awake and neither blind nor deaf, though a distracted or drowsy man who meets those criteria is enough; the Concise BMC puts "within sight" at about six metres.30​ 31

    Intention

    Disputed — what it means to be "seeking privacy", which the non-offence clause names as a condition and the Vibhaṅga never defines.32

    Three readings are reported, and Thanissaro sets them side by side. His own takes the word from the Vibhaṅga's definition of private: aiming at privacy is not wanting anyone near enough to see or hear. The Commentary's, in his report, makes it any defilement connected with sex, and he asks whether that means intercourse alone or also the subtler bonds of sexuality listed in AN 7:47; if the latter, the factor would be met by wanting to joke with the woman, to look into her eyes or to enjoy her voice.33​ 34 The third, which he reports from the Vinaya-mukha and does not hold, says the monk who did not intend to be alone with her is not seeking privacy, as when he sits with a man and a woman and the man gets up and leaves before he can be stopped. Thanissaro judges the first probably correct and the third probably the safest, which separates what the rule means from what a monk should do about it.35 Ajahn Brahm reasons on the third pattern, the room the men leave.36 Brahmāli, in class, read the aim as wanting to be alone with the woman, and carried it to a modern case, a monk consulting a female doctor about something he would not want others to hear: "what you are aiming for is seeing your doctor … and getting your health things sorted out." On Thanissaro's own reading, the same consultation, kept out of others' hearing, could fall the other way.37 Thanissaro's counsel on the uncertain margin is to be no less strict than one's own Community, and the sources leave the question where he leaves it.38

    The rule is not the whole of the guidance. Thanissaro warns that a monk who sits alone with a woman, even with an innocent intention, is inviting the suspicions of others and leaving himself at her mercy, and he gives Visākhā's words from the origin story of Aniyata 1: "Even though the master may not be aiming at that act, cynical people are hard to convince."39

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — sitting in private on a concealed seat with a paṇḍaka, a female spirit, a female ghost, or a female animal in the form of a woman.40
    • Dukkaṭa — where the person is not a woman but he perceives her as one, or is unsure of it. Where the person is a woman, all three perceptions carry the full pācittiya.41

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he has a male companion who understands; if he stands and does not sit down; if he is not seeking privacy; if he sits down preoccupied with something else; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.42

    For the fourth, aññavihita, Brahmāli's endnote gives the Commentary's gloss, "thinking of something else". Thanissaro's example is a monk absorbed in his work or his meditation when a woman comes in and sits down.43​ 44 It overlaps the third without being the same: a monk may not be seeking privacy and still have noticed the situation, where this exemption covers the monk who has not noticed it at all.

    Related Offences

    • Pc 45 — sitting alone in private with a woman: the other half of the pair, where the seat is not concealed and another woman suffices as the companion.
    • Aniyata 1, 2 — the anchors for raho, the concealed seat and the open one, and the rules under which a trustworthy lay follower's report is enough to bring the matter before the Saṅgha.
    • Pc 43 — the rule immediately before, with the same offender, where the exemption is also a companion.
    • Pc 7 — teaching more than a few words to a woman with no knowledgeable man present: the same exemption by a man who understands.
    • Sg 2 — where mātugāma is defined in the same wide terms this rule uses.
    • Pc 30 — sitting alone in private with a nun: the nun's counterpart of Pc 45, which refers its question of "seeking privacy" here.
    • Pc 27, Pc 67 — travelling by arrangement with a nun and with a woman: the same pairing of a nun's rule and a woman's, for a journey, not a seat.

    Related Suttas

    • SN 35.127 — the reflection by which young monks keep the celibate life among women, regarding them as mothers, sisters and daughters: the guard a monk brings to any seat a woman shares.45
    • SN 47.6 — a monk's own resort is set against "the domain of others", "the five cords of sensual pleasure", toward which a screened seat with a woman can lead.46
    • AN 7.50 — the subtler bonds of sexuality, joking with women and gazing into their eyes, which Thanissaro asks whether the Commentary's reading of the rule's intention reaches (see Intention).47
