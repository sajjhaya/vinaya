    NP 2

    Story (Vatthu)

    At Sāvatthī, in the Jeta Grove, some monks stored a robe with other monks and set off to wander the country carrying only a sarong and an upper robe. The stored robes sat for a long time and grew mouldy, and the monks who had stayed behind put them out in the sun. Walking about the dwellings, Venerable Ānanda saw the robes being sunned and asked whose mouldy robes they were. When he was told, he complained: "How can those monks store a robe with other monks and then leave to wander the country in a sarong and an upper robe?" He told the Buddha, who questioned the monks, rebuked them — "How can those foolish men do this? This will affect people's confidence" — and laid down a first version of the rule: a monk who stays apart from his three robes even for a single day commits an offence entailing relinquishment and confession.1

    Soon afterwards a monk at Kosambī fell ill, and his relatives sent word: "Come, venerable, we'll nurse you." The monks urged him to go, but he said, "The Buddha has laid down a training rule that you can't be apart from your three robes. Now because I'm sick, I'm unable to travel with my three robes. So I can't go." They told the Buddha, who gave a teaching and allowed the Saṅgha to grant a sick monk permission to stay apart from his three robes — set out as a formal saṅgha-kamma, requested three times by the sick monk in person and then put to the Saṅgha by a competent monk.2 He then revised the rule to add the exception: except if the monks have agreed.3

    Synopsis — stored robes are left to rot while their owners travel light; the Buddha ties a monk to his three robes; and then, meeting the case of the sick monk who cannot carry them, he opens a formal exception through the Saṅgha.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "When his robe is finished and the robe season has ended, if a monk stays apart from his three robes even for a single day, except if the monks have agreed, he commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession."4

    Condensed recitation translation — "When a bhikkhu does not have robe-season privileges, if he stays apart from any of his three robes even for a single day, except with the Saṅgha's agreement, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."5

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "When a bhikkhu has finished his robe and the frame is dismantled (his kaṭhina privileges are ended): If he dwells apart from (any of) his three robes even for one night—unless authorized by the bhikkhus—it is to be forfeited and confessed."6

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Being separated from any of one's three robes at dawn without the Saṅgha's permission, except when the end of the rainy-season privileges or the Kaṭhina privileges still apply, is a nissaggiya pācittiya."7

    Ñāṇatusita

    "When the robe [-cloth] is finished by a bhikkhu, when the kaṭhina [-frame-privileges] have been withdrawn, if even for a single night a bhikkhu should stay apart from the three robes, except with the authorization of bhikkhus, [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture."8

    The opening clause is NP 1's and is discussed there. One difference here changes what the rule requires. The Pāli says ekarattam pi, "even for a single night", and Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita keep the night; Brahmāli's "for a single day" takes ratta as the dawn-to-dawn day of the Buddha's time, while Ajahn Brahm's rule line writes the Commentary's reading into the rule itself, "separated … at dawn". Whether the offence is a whole day apart or a single moment is the question below, under Effort.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A monk's care of the robes he has is the point of this rule. The monks of its Story left their outer robes with others and went off in the other two, and the robes went mouldy in other monks' keeping, so that the monks who stayed behind had to sun them; the rule was laid down, in Thanissaro's words, "so that bhikkhus would be responsible for looking after their own robes".9 It bent at once for a monk too sick to carry all three, but only through the Saṅgha, in a formal act.

    The rule shares NP 1's opening clause, its robe season and its relinquishment, all set out there.10 What is its own is the object, the timing of the offence, the zone a robe defines, and the exception. Thanissaro gives two factors, object and effort, with the exemptions inside effort; Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual lists five, which fall under the same two.11​ 12

    Object

    Any of the three robes a monk has determined as his set: the outer robe, the upper robe and the sarong.13 Nothing else is covered, not spare robes, not cloth determined as a requisite, not extra cloth, even when it is worn; a monk may be apart from any of these for as long as he likes.14​ 15 That is why the elders the Commentary records wanted the three robes themselves determined as requisite cloth, to escape this rule (NP 1). The non-offences follow from the object: a robe given away, lost, destroyed or taken on trust is no longer a determined robe of his, and there is nothing left for the rule to apply to.

    Effort

    Being outside the zone of one of the three robes when dawn rises, unless an exemption applies.16 The Vibhaṅga says only that the robe "becomes subject to relinquishment at dawn", and what that means is disputed.17

    Disputed — whether the offence is a single dawn apart from the robe, or a whole day.

    The Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī takes it that a monk must be with the robe at the moment of dawn itself, and that is how the rule is commonly kept; Thanissaro follows it, framing the effort as "greeting dawnrise" outside the zone, and Ajahn Brahm writes it into his rule line, holding that the Vibhaṅga itself defines the night as "separation at the time of dawn".18​ 19​ 20 Brahmāli calls this "an entirely artificial and unpersuasive interpretation. 'Night' and 'dawn' are just not the same thing." Since a day then began and ended at dawn, he reads the Vibhaṅga to mean that the offence falls at dawn only if the monk has been apart from the robe through the whole preceding twenty-four hours, and vippavasati, "stays apart from", suggests to him an extended time, not an instant; on his reading, to be with the robe even briefly in any day is enough. The narrow reading, he observes, "often leads to silly and impractical practices", monks carrying all three robes with them to urinate before dawn.21 Thanissaro argues elsewhere for when dawnrise falls, not for how long a monk must be apart, so he does not answer Brahmāli's case, though on the penance period he prefers, as "closer to the Canon", the reading of night as a full twenty-four hours.22 The question is open, and Brahmāli himself tells monks to know both readings, "because otherwise you might be in trouble when you go to other monasteries".23

    The texts do not define when dawn rises; the question is argued at Pc 37. Thailand's practical test is the moment the main lines of the palm, held at arm's length, can be seen by natural light, and Ajahn Brahm advises staying with the robe for the half hour before it.24​ 25

    The difficulty of the rule lies in the zone. The Vibhaṅga runs through the places a robe may be kept, an inhabited area, a house, a storehouse, a watchtower, a stilt house, a boat, a caravan, a field, a threshing floor, a monastery, a dwelling, the foot of a tree and the open, and gives each a zone decided by two questions: whether the place is enclosed, and whether it belongs to one clan or to many.26 Enclosed means bounded by a wall, a fence or a moat.27 The clan, the Commentary explains, is for a village or town its ruler, one ruler or a council, and for a building, a vehicle or a piece of land its owning family.28​ 29 An enclosed place of one clan is a zone as a whole; places of many clans, and places unenclosed, shrink the zone to the room or dwelling where the robe is kept, an entrance, or arm's reach of the robe.30 Where the Vibhaṅga allows a place "or not go beyond arm's reach", the Commentary measures the arm's reach from the place and not the robe, so that a monk may step outside to relieve himself without taking his robes along; Thanissaro sets out why this is the better reading.31

    The Manual reduces all of this to a pair of rules that cannot fail: a robe kept indoors, stay in the same room; a robe kept outdoors, stay within arm's reach. It is stricter than the Vibhaṅga, as Brahmāli says, and simple to remember; a monk who wants the latitude the text allows must learn the cases.32

    Disputed — what makes a monastery a place of one clan.

    It matters because an enclosed monastery of one clan is a zone as a whole, so that a monk with his robe in his hut may sit in the hall at dawn. Thanissaro gives the Sub-commentary's answer: a monastery is one clan if those who founded it belong to one.33 Ajahn Brahm argues that a monastery is always one clan: a village under one headman is one clan, so a monastery under one abbot is too; the Vibhaṅga gives a monastic dwelling the same zone whoever owns it; and monks are "sons of the Sakyan".34 And Thai practice, which Thanissaro traces to the Vinayamukha's having missed the Sub-commentary, treats a dwelling of monks from different families as many clans, so that each monk must keep to the room of his robes or within arm's reach of them; "there is no basis in the Canon or commentaries for this practice", but Thanissaro advises keeping it beside a Thai monk, to avoid useless controversy.35 The sources set the founders' reading beside the abbot's and none weighs the two against each other; the question stays open. In a monastery with one abbot and a clear boundary, which is the common case, Ajahn Brahm holds that the whole monastery is the zone;36 a monk unsure how his monastery was founded has the room of his robes, which is safe on every reading.

    Travel takes the Vibhaṅga's boat and caravan. A vehicle is a boat: in a private one it is enough that monk and robes are both aboard, while in a train, a bus or an aeroplane he must be in the compartment where the robes are or within arm's reach of it, which is why Thanissaro tells a monk flying overnight to keep his robes in his cabin baggage.37 A caravan, which the Sub-commentary extends to a party on foot, gives a zone some 80 metres before and behind and 11 to either side on Brahmāli's conversion, 98 and 14 on Thanissaro's; and in the wilderness away from villages the zone is a circle of the same radius around the robe.38​ 39

    Perception and intention do not excuse. If a monk has been outside the zone, he has the full offence whether he knows it, doubts it or thinks he has not, and Thanissaro adds that it stands "if he means to be in the same zone when circumstances prevent him".40​ 41 The way out the texts give is the robe's own status: there is no offence if the robe has been relinquished before dawn, and the Commentary advises a monk who cannot get back in time to rescind the robe's determination before dawn and re-determine it after.42​ 43 A robe so rescinded is extra cloth under NP 1 and must be re-determined within ten days; for a longer absence, assignment keeps it safely.

    The robe season. The rule does not apply while a monk has the robe-making ceremony's privileges, which are NP 1's to describe; going without taking all three robes is one of the ceremony's five allowances.44​ 45 Whether it also lapses in the last month of the rains for a monk who has not taken part in the ceremony is disputed.

    Disputed — whether the rule is lifted in the month after the rains for a monk without the robe-making ceremony.

    Thanissaro holds that it is not. The texts lift this rule only among the ceremony's privileges, and "unlike NP 1 & 3" it "is not automatically rescinded during this month"; it might seem reasonable to treat it as rescinded with the others, "but neither the Canon nor the commentaries support this idea", the Khandhaka allowing a monk to enter a village without his full set only if he has spread a kaṭhina.46 Ajahn Brahm and Brahmāli hold that it is. Ajahn Brahm lists it among the six rules that cease to apply in that month for a monk who completed the rains residence, and the Manual gives this rule NP 1's first factor, that the monk "does not have end of rainy season privileges nor kaṭhina privileges", and says of NP 29 that it "places a constraint on the exemption given in NP2. This constraint is effective during the last month of the rainy season."47​ 48 Thanissaro argues from where the texts lift the rule and the Manual from the factor it shares with NP 1, while Ajahn Brahm states the exemption without arguing it. Neither answers the other's ground, and the question stays open. The question decides how NP 29 is read, its six nights a relief on one reading and a limit on the other, though what the monk there must do is the same on both; and a monk without the ceremony who keeps all three robes by him at dawn in that month is safe on either.

    The Saṅgha's agreement. The sick monk asks the Saṅgha three times, in person, and a competent monk puts the request to it with a motion and one announcement.49 By the Commentary the permission lasts as long as the illness: once recovered he should rejoin his robes as soon as he reasonably can, and the permission lapses without any act to end it, reviving if the illness returns.50 The Story speaks only of sickness, but Brahmāli observes that neither the Story nor the rule says it cannot be given in other circumstances, and concludes that the Saṅgha may give it when it sees fit.51

    A zone declared for the robes. The Saṅgha may also designate its monastery zone, the sīmā it has established for its observance days, as an area where monks may stay apart from their three robes, so that a robe anywhere inside it keeps its owner safe anywhere else inside it. The allowance came after Venerable Mahākassapa's robes were soaked crossing a river on his way to the observance day, and when monks then left their robes in villages and lost them to fire and rats, inhabited areas were excluded.52 The early zones could be large, up to 40 kilometres across on Brahmāli's figures and 48 on Thanissaro's, and took in many monasteries; most now cover a fraction of one, and the device helps less than it did.53​ 54​ 55 The Canon gives the allowance for an established zone; the Commentary, Thanissaro reports, lets other territories serve too, against that wording.56

    None of the latitude the zones and exemptions give lifts the duty the Story set. Ajahn Brahm reminds a monk that he "should not neglect his responsibility to take good care of the few requisites, in particular the three robes, that he has", and that "one of the most effective ways to look after one's three robes is to wear them all regularly!"57

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — using a robe that should be relinquished before relinquishing it.58
    • Dukkaṭa — thinking one has stayed apart, or being unsure, when one has not; Thanissaro takes it, from NP 1, to be for using the robe.59​ 60

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if before dawn the robe has been relinquished, given away, lost, destroyed, burned, stolen or taken on trust; if he has the permission of the monks; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.61 The rule does not apply while the robe-making ceremony's privileges are in force.

    Related Offences

    • NP 1 — the opening clause, the robe season and the relinquishment; a robe whose determination is rescinded to escape this rule falls under its ten days.
    • NP 29 — six nights apart from one robe left in a village by a wilderness monk, a rule whose reading depends on the month after the rains.
    • Pc 37 — where the meaning of dawn is argued.
    • Pc 59 — assignment, the way to store a robe through a long absence.
    • Entering a village without the three robes (Khandhaka) — a dukkaṭa except on five occasions, among them illness, rain, a crossing, a locked store and the ceremony's privileges.62
