    Pc 88

    Story (Vatthu)

    At one time the Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. The monks from the group of six had beds and benches made upholstered with cotton down.1

    People walking about the dwellings saw this, and they complained and criticised those monks: "How can the Sakyan monastics have beds and benches made upholstered with cotton down? They are just like householders who indulge in worldly pleasures!"

    The monks heard the complaints of those people, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised those monks: "How could the monks from the group of six do this?"

    The Buddha asked those monks whether it was true, and they said that it was. He rebuked them: "Foolish men, how could you do this? This will affect people's confidence." After rebuking them, he laid down the rule.2

    Synopsis — the group of six have their beds and benches upholstered with cotton down, the neighbours say they look like householders, and the rule that follows requires the stuffing to be pulled out.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk has a bed or a bench made upholstered with cotton down, it is to be stripped, and he commits an offense entailing confession."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu makes a bed or bench with luxurious upholstery, or has one made, the upholstery should be removed and he incurs a pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu have a bed or bench upholstered with cotton down, it (the upholstery) is to be torn off and confessed."5

    Brahm

    "Having a bed or chair upholstered with cotton is an offence of Pācittiya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should have a bed or seat covered with cotton made, [this is a case] involving expiation with tearing off [the cotton]."7

    One word in the condensed recitation changes what the rule requires, and it is the word naming the object. Four of the five renderings name the material, cotton or cotton down; the recitation names neither, giving "luxurious upholstery", which is not a compression of tūlonaddhaṁ but a substitution of the rule's purpose for the rule's object.8 A monk reciting the condensed line alone would take the rule to reach any luxurious seat, which is a reading two of the book's teachers divide over; it is taken up below, under Object.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule keeps a monk's comforts plain and his dwelling unlike a householder's, for the sake of his own contentment and of the people who visit the monastery and judge it by what they see there. The group of six had their beds and benches upholstered with cotton down, people walking about the dwellings said they were "just like householders who indulge in worldly pleasures", and the Buddha rebuked them with the words "This will affect people's confidence."9 Thanissaro sums up this rule and the Khandhaka rulings around it as keeping monks from furnishings that are "extravagant and ostentatious".10 Ajahn Brahm reads the object as the mahāsayana of the novice's precept, the bed too luxurious for a monk because of its covering, as Pc 87's is too luxurious because of its height.11

    The machinery is Pc 86's: making the article or having it made is wrong conduct for the effort, the full offence falls when the monk receives it, the four combinations of his own hands and another's all carry it, and making one for someone else, or using one another person made, is wrong conduct.12​ 13 Thanissaro's three factors, object, effort and intention, are the same as at the previous two rules.14 What differs is the object, and with it what is put right: the penalty is a tearing-off, so it is the upholstery that goes and not the furniture.15

    Object

    A bed or bench upholstered with cotton down. The Vibhaṅga defines cotton down in three words, down from trees, from creepers and from grass, and Thanissaro gives the Commentary as taking the triad to cover down from any plant, so that, he adds, kapok, flax, jute and cotton itself are all included.16​ 17 Bed and bench are defined by the same four construction types as at Pc 87.

    A boundary is drawn here that the previous rule does not need. Reasoning that cotton-down cushions are forbidden to a monk in every situation, Thanissaro takes bed and bench in this rule to reach all furniture, including the square stool and the tall chair that the Khandhakas exempt from Pc 87.18 It is his inference, and its premise sits awkwardly with the Khandhaka passage on what belongs to householders, in which monks who were afraid to sit on upholstered beds and benches set out in the dining halls were told that they might, a passage his own second volume renders the same way.19​ 20

    The word "luxurious" in Brahmāli's recitation raises the larger question. The rule as the Vibhaṅga states it is about a material, not a price: a plain bed stuffed with kapok breaks it, and an extravagant one stuffed with wool does not. Here Ajahn Brahm and Brahmāli agree on the spirit and differ on the offence. Ajahn Brahm takes this rule and Pc 87 together by their spirit, since the furniture they name has "little correspondence to furniture which is considered extravagant or luxurious now", and holds that a monk who makes what is luxurious in his own day, whatever its legs or stuffing, "deserves an offence of Pācittiya".21 Brahmāli finds Ajahn Brahm's reading of the spirit good sense, but keeps the offence where the text puts it: "strictly speaking, if it hasn't got cotton down upholstery, basically you can't fall into this offence", though luxurious furnishing still breaches the rule's spirit.22 Neither cites a passage making luxury as such an offence under this rule, and I take Brahmāli's to be the reading of the text and Ajahn Brahm's to be sound advice on how to live. The offence is the material; the luxury is the spirit.

    Effort and intention

    As at Pc 86.23

    The other side of the line is the part a monk actually needs: what he may stuff, and what he may sit on. The one cotton-down furnishing allowed him is a pillow, and only the size of his head; when the group of six used pillows half the size of the body, the same complaint was made of them and the pillow was cut back.24 For mattresses the Khandhakas allow five stuffings, wool, cloth, bark, grass and leaves, the things the monks found left on the ground after a hilltop fair.25 Thanissaro gives the Commentary as letting wool carry animal hair and feathers, which is how the down of a goose is allowable where the down of a tree is not.26 And a cotton-down quilt offered to the Saṅgha is not refused but opened: the down becomes pillows, and the other unallowable goods offered with it become floor covers.27

    In a layperson's house the general allowance given at Pc 87 holds: a monk may sit on his host's furnishings but not lie down on them. Three things are excepted even from sitting, the high couch, the luxurious couch and the cotton-down quilt, and the upholstered bed and bench of this rule are not among them.28 So the object a monk may not own is one he may sit on as a guest, and the one he may not sit on even as a guest is a quilt this rule never mentions.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • An offence of wrong conduct for the effort of making such a bed or bench or having one made, the full offence falling on receiving it, at which point the upholstery is to be torn off before the offence is confessed.29
    • An offence of wrong conduct where a monk makes one, or has one made, for the benefit of someone else.30
    • An offence of wrong conduct where a monk obtains one made by someone else and uses it without first stripping it.31
    • An offence of wrong conduct, in the Khandhakas, for using a pillow half the size of the body.32

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence in using cotton down for a back-and-knee strap, a belt, a shoulder strap, a bowl bag, or a water filter; nor in making a pillow; nor where a monk gets one made by another and strips it before using it; nor if he is insane or is the first offender.33

    Related Offences

    • Pc 87 — a bed or bench with legs over eight standard fingerbreadths; the same pair of objects limited by their height instead of their covering, and read with this rule as a pair.
    • Pc 86 — a needle case of bone, ivory or horn; the anchor for the making-and-putting-right machinery this rule shares entire.
    • Pc 14 — leaving a bed or bench out in the open; the same four construction types define bed and bench there.
    • Pc 89, Pc 90 — the next two rules in the chapter, which limit a requisite by its measure instead of its material.

    Related Suttas

    • DN 1 — the Buddha refrains from "high and wide beds" (uccāsayana-mahāsayanā), and Ajahn Brahm reads the mahāsayana, the wide bed, as this rule's object, too luxurious because of its covering as Pc 87's is because of its height.34​ 35

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; the step in which the monks report the matter to the Buddha is abbreviated in the source by its own …pe… and is given here in the standing summary form. Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual, the recitation, the class (session 032, 30 July 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1 and BMC2, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were all checked, and each Khandhaka passage I rely on is quoted from Brahmāli's translation. The Commentary appears only through Thanissaro's report of it. The wider reading under Object, that luxurious furnishing falls under the rule in spirit, is attributed to those who hold it and is not stated as the rule. DN 1 is quoted from Walshe's translation.
