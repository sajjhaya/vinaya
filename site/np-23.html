    NP 23

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time Venerable Pilindavaccha was clearing a slope near Rājagaha, intending to build a shelter. King Seniya Bimbisāra of Magadha went to him, bowed, sat down, and said, "Venerable, what are you having made?"

    "I'm clearing a slope, great king. I want to build a shelter."

    "Do you need a monastery worker?"

    "The Buddha hasn't allowed monastery workers."

    "Well then, sir, please ask the Buddha and tell me the outcome."

    Pilindavaccha sent a message to the Buddha, who addressed the monks: "Monks, I allow monastery workers." The king promised to provide a monastery worker, but after making the promise he forgot, and only remembered after a long time. He asked the official in charge of all practical affairs whether the monastery worker had been provided. He had not. "How long has it been since we made that promise?" The official counted the days and said, "It's been five hundred days." "Well then, provide him with five hundred monastery workers." The official did, and a separate village was established. They called it "The Monastery Workers' Village" and "Pilinda Village", and Pilindavaccha began associating with the families there.

    One morning, when they were holding a celebration in that village and the children were dressed up with ornaments and garlands, Pilindavaccha was walking on continuous almsround and came to the house of a certain monastery worker. The daughter of that house had seen the other children dressed up, and she cried, "Give me a garland! Give me ornaments!" Pilindavaccha asked her mother why the girl was crying, and the mother told him, adding, "Poor people like us can't afford garlands and ornaments." Pilindavaccha took a pad of grass and said, "Here, place this on the girl's head." She did, and it turned into a beautiful golden garland. Even the royal compound had nothing like it.

    People told King Bimbisāra, "In the house of such-and-such a monastery worker there's a beautiful golden garland. Even in your court, sir, there's nothing like it. So how did those poor people get it? They must have stolen it." King Bimbisāra had that family imprisoned.

    When Pilindavaccha next came to that house on almsround and learned from the neighbours what had happened, he went to King Bimbisāra's house, sat down on the prepared seat, and said, "Great king, why have you jailed the family of that monastery worker?" The king told him about the garland. Pilindavaccha then focused his mind on turning the king's stilt house into gold, and the whole house became gold. "Great king, how did you get so much gold?"

    "Understood, sir! It's your supernormal power." And he released that family.

    People said, "They say Venerable Pilindavaccha has performed a superhuman feat, a wonder of supernormal power, for the king and his court!" Being delighted and gaining confidence in him, they brought him the five tonics: ghee, butter, oil, honey, and syrup. Ordinarily, too, Pilindavaccha was getting the five tonics, and since he was getting so much, he gave it away to his followers, who ended up with an abundance of tonics. After filling up basins and waterpots and setting these aside, they filled their water filters and bags and hung these in the windows. But the tonics were dripping, and the dwellings became infested with rats. When people walking about the dwellings saw this, they complained and criticised them, "These Sakyan monastics are hoarding things indoors, just like King Seniya Bimbisāra of Magadha."

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised those monks, "How can these monks choose to live with such abundance?" They told the Buddha, who had the Saṅgha gathered, questioned the monks, and rebuked them: "How can those foolish men live like this? This will affect people's confidence …" And he laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — Pilindavaccha frees a family jailed over a golden garland by turning the king's house to gold; the people bring him so many tonics that his followers store them in pots and in bags hung dripping in the windows, the dwellings fill with rats, and people say the monks hoard like the king; the Buddha limits keeping the tonics to seven days.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "After being received, the tonics allowable for sick monks — that is, ghee, butter, oil, honey, and syrup — should be used from storage for at most seven days. If one uses them longer than that, one commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu keeps ghee, butter, cheese, oil, honey, or sugar for more than seven days, intending to eat it, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "There are these tonics to be taken by sick bhikkhus: ghee, fresh butter, oil, honey, sugar/molasses. Having been received, they are to be used from storage seven days at most. Beyond that, they are to be forfeited and confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Keeping any of the five 'tonics' beyond seven days is a nissaggiya pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "Now, [there are] those medicines which are permissable for sick bhikkhus, namely: ghee, butter, oil, [and] honey and molasses — having been accepted, they can be partaken of [while] being kept in store for seven days at the most. For one who lets it pass beyond [the seven days], [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture."6

    The renderings differ over two of the five words and over what the rule calls them. Navanīta is "butter" for Brahmāli and Ñāṇatusita and "fresh butter" for Thanissaro, and Brahmāli's recitation renders it twice, "butter, cheese"; whether cheese belongs there is argued under Object.7 Phāṇita is "syrup" for Brahmāli, for whom "molasses" "doesn't quite hit the mark" against Monier-Williams's "inspissated juice of the sugarcane", and "sugar/molasses" or "molasses" for the others.8 Bhesajja is literally "medicine", as Ñāṇatusita has it; "tonic" is Thanissaro's word and Ajahn Brahm's, his "because of the way these Five Tonics are used", which Brahmāli adopts, keeping "medicine" for the word's other uses.9​ 10 The recitation's "intending to eat it" builds in the reading of "keeps" in Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual, discussed under Effort.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    What was allowed for the sick must stay a remedy: the rule guards a monk's contentment by keeping his medicines from becoming a store. The tonics were allowed for a reason. The Khandhaka tells how monks with the autumn illness could not keep their meals down, and how the Buddha looked for something "generally regarded as tonics" that "would serve as nourishment, but aren't considered substantial food", and allowed the five, first before midday and then at any time.11 Pilindavaccha's followers turned that allowance into a hoard: basins and waterpots filled, bags hung dripping in the windows, rats in the dwellings, and people saying the monks were "hoarding things indoors, just like King Seniya Bimbisāra".12 The Buddha did not withdraw the tonics; he limited how long they may be kept.

    Thanissaro divides the offence into object and effort; the Manual divides the same ground into what is kept, and how and how long.13​ 14 I take Thanissaro's two, and add the two things a monk most needs alongside them: how a tonic may be used within the seven days, and how it may be mixed.

    Object

    The five are one of four classes of edibles, sorted by how long each may be kept once received: food, until midday; juice drinks, until the next dawn; the five tonics, seven days; and medicines, which Ajahn Brahm describes as "chemical, mineral or herbal preparations which are used by laypeople not as food but to cure sickness", for life.15 The other classes belong to the food rules, Pc 37 and Pc 38.

    The Vibhaṅga defines the five: ghee from cows, goats, buffaloes, or any animal whose meat is allowable; butter from the same animals; oil from sesame, mustard, the honey tree, castor, or fat; honey from bees; and syrup from sugarcane.16 The Khandhaka allows the fat of bears, fish, alligators, pigs and donkeys for oil, and since bear meat is not allowable, the list is read widely: Thanissaro reports the Sub-commentary taking in the fat of any animal whose flesh is allowable or carries only wrong conduct, human fat excluded.17 The Great Standards carry the list further. Thanissaro would bring every plant oil used as food under the rule, against the Commentary's mere wrong conduct for oils the Vibhaṅga does not name, and class aromatic oils from medicinal leaves and resins as medicines; and he follows the Vinayamukha in counting every sugar and molasses, maple syrup and beet sugar among them, where the Commentary excluded sugar from fruits classed as food.18​ 19 Fresh sugarcane juice, which the Commentary counted as syrup, is listed among the juice drinks in the Khandhaka, Thanissaro points out.20

    Disputed — what navanīta is, and whether cheese is a tonic.

    Navanīta is churned from curds, not from cream, as MN 126 describes and as it is still made in Indian villages; creamery butter is admitted under the Great Standards by everyone.21​ 22 Cheese was unknown in the Buddha's time. Thanissaro argues that it must come under one of the five products of the cow the Khandhaka allows, and that curds is the better fit, being closer in composition and more of a substantial food: on that reading cheese is food, finished at midday. Ñāṇatusita reaches the same exclusion from the language: "Cheese … is processed curd and can't be included in the term navanītaṃ."23​ 24 On the other side, Ajahn Brahm reports that Ajahn Mun took cheese as a tonic, and with him most of the Thai forest monks of his lineage, holding that it is more like navanīta than like anything the Buddha placed in another class. He sets the contrary view beside it, adds that butter is much closer to navanīta than cheese is, and does not decide.25 The Manual accepts Thanissaro's argument that navanīta is closer to butter than to cheese, yet judges that "both butter and cheese may be similar enough to navanītaṃ to be accepted as such."26 The argument from the language weighs heavily, but the Great Standards ask what a new thing is most like, not what it was called, and on that judgment the texts are silent. Communities differ, as Thanissaro says, and a monk follows his own.27

    Two methods in the Khandhaka decide what the Vibhaṅga does not name, and a monk will use them most. The first is the Great Standards: what the Buddha has neither allowed nor prohibited is allowable if it is like what is allowable and unlike what is not.28 Brahmāli applied it in class to a modern case, fish-oil capsules taken in the afternoon. Oils as such are tonics, but a product that comes in the size, shape and packaging society accepts as medicine, a jar labelled vitamins, he took to be a medicine.29 The second is the name: when Revata the Doubter saw sugar hardened with flour and ash, the Buddha ruled, "If they add flour or ash to sugar to harden it, it's still considered sugar."30 A trace of food that leaves a tonic its name leaves it a tonic. At the margin the test gives different answers. For Ajahn Brahm, boiled sweets are mostly sugar and go by the same name with or without their flour, and so remain a tonic, and dark chocolate stays a tonic while a little milk leaves it dark chocolate, becoming food only as milk chocolate; Thanissaro holds that a mixture "regarded as something else — candy, for instance — … counts as a food."31​ 32

    Effort

    The Manual reads "keeps" as "keeps ownership of for the purpose of eating", arguing from the rule's own sequence: the tonic is received, stored and eaten, and to eat what one does not own would be theft.33

    The count is NP 1's on a shorter span: "it becomes subject to relinquishment at dawn on the eighth day", the seventh dawn after receipt on the Commentary's count, so always a little less than seven full days.34​ 35 Ajahn Brahm's example: sugar received on the afternoon of 25 March is past its seventh dawn early on 1 April.36 The seven days belong to the tonic, not to its owner. If one monk gives it to another, the second has only what is left of the first monk's seven, counted, as Ajahn Brahm puts it, "from when the first monk received it."37​ 38 Perception does not mitigate: miscounting, or believing a tonic given away when it is not, leaves the full offence.39

    Use within the seven days. The tonics are allowed "for sick monks", and gilāna in the Vinaya is a wide word. Ajahn Brahm gathers the Vibhaṅga's own uses of it, a monk who cannot eat his fill at one sitting, or who has no comfort without a fire or a bath, to show that it reaches mere discomfort; Thanissaro reports the Sub-commentary's standard, that any reason, hunger or weakness, will do, "as long as one is not taking the tonic for nourishment as food."40​ 41 Taking a tonic as food is wrong conduct under the food rules.42 Sugar has its own allowance: the sick may take it as it is, and the healthy mixed in water.43

    Mixing. What a mixture is follows the Khandhaka's table. A tonic mixed with food is allowable only before midday on the day the tonic was received; mixed with a juice drink, only until the next dawn and only on its day of receipt; and a medicine mixed with a tonic is "allowable for seven days, but not beyond."44 So a tonic kept from yesterday, stirred into this morning's porridge, makes the porridge stored food, the offence of Pc 38, and honey received two days ago turns today's lemon juice into a drink that may not be taken.45 The one mixture that favours the monk is a medicine in a tonic: in Ajahn Brahm's example, sugar received four days ago, with hot water and tea leaves of three months, makes a tonic that may be taken at any hour and kept three more days.46 The penalty for a stored juice drink, and how food mixed before dawn is counted, belong to Pc 38.

    The class is fixed at receipt, and the door opens one way. A tonic received as a tonic may go into that morning's food and become food. A substance received "for the sake of food", a lump of brown sugar taken on almsround as part of the meal, can only ever be food, and no later change of mind makes it a tonic.47 A monk who makes a tonic himself is bound the same way. He may render fat into oil only if he received it and rendered it before midday, and in Ajahn Brahm's report the Samantapāsādikā will not let that oil be mixed with food; ghee he makes from butter received and churned in the morning may go with that morning's food or be kept seven days, and tonics he makes from milk, curds or cane juice received after midday may not be eaten at all.48​ 49

    Relinquishment. The procedure is NP 1's, in the tonic's own words: "Venerables, these tonics, which I have kept over seven days, are to be relinquished."50 The tonics are given back, but the Vibhaṅga then restricts them: the offender may neither eat them nor use them on his body, only burn them in lamps or use them as a black colouring; other monks may use them on the body but may not eat them.51 Brahmāli's practice in class was to give such a tonic up to a lay person outright, and if it was later freely offered again, that was the donor's business and not his.52 The Story's own model is Pilindavaccha, who gave away the tonics he could not use; the rule was laid down for those who stored them.53

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    "Determined", in the exemptions, means determined for use outside the body: ghee, oil or fat as oil for the head or as ointment, honey for wounds, syrup as a scent for a dwelling.54 A tonic so determined may be kept as long as one likes, but it may not afterwards be taken back as a tonic. A monk who sees the seventh dawn coming needs no assembly and no formula: Ajahn Brahm's monk living alone, who resolves on the sixth day to give his sugar away on almsround the next morning, no longer keeps it as a tonic, and commits no offence though the sugar outlasts the dawn.55​ 56 The clauses carry no assignment; in Thanissaro's report the Commentary lets a tonic held in shared ownership be kept past the seven days while the owners leave their shares undivided, though not afterwards used internally.57

    Disputed — what the clause on giving tonics up to an unordained person allows.

    In Thanissaro's report the Commentary records two elders. Mahā Sumana read the clause as the words run: a monk who within the seven days gives the tonic to an unordained person, giving up possession of it in his mind, may use it for another seven days if it comes back to him. Mahā Paduma answered that "abandons it" already covers that case, and that the clause speaks of a tonic kept past seven days, relinquished and returned, then given up to an unordained person and returned. The Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī agrees with the second.58 Thanissaro argues for the first. "If within seven days" governs every other clause and nothing marks this one off; every other clause describes a way of avoiding the offence, not of handling the forfeited article; and if "given away" let a monk give a tonic to anyone and take it back for seven more days, monks could trade hoards of tonics among themselves without end, the very hoard the Story forbids. On his reading, giving a tonic away to anyone ends a monk's responsibility for it, but a fresh seven days comes only from a gift to an unordained person within the first seven days, with possession genuinely given up.59 Ajahn Brahm states that rule without the Commentary's debate, and presses the condition: a monk who hands his sugar to a novice hoping he will be smart enough to offer it back "has not renounced the sugar."60 Thanissaro's reading keeps the rule's purpose in view, which is a strong argument for it, but one of the Commentary's elders and the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī read the same words otherwise, and the Vibhaṅga does not say which was right. On either reading the gift must be real; a gift made to be returned buys nothing.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — keeping a tonic less than seven days while perceiving it as more, or unsure of it; as at NP 1, apparently for using it.61​ 62
    • Dukkaṭa — taking a tonic as food.63
    • Dukkaṭa — eating after midday a tonic received for the sake of food; and eating one so received after keeping it overnight, the two together on a later afternoon.64
    • Dukkaṭa — consuming oil from fat received, rendered or filtered after midday, one for each step so done.65
    • Dukkaṭa — for the monk who receives the relinquished tonic: not giving it back.66

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if within seven days the tonics have been determined, given away, lost, destroyed, burned, stolen, or taken on trust; if, without any desire for them, he gives them up to a person who is not fully ordained, and he then obtains them again and then uses them; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.67

    Related Offences

    • NP 1 — the count to the dawn, and relinquishment and return.
    • NP 21 — the same keep-for-a-time design for bowls, with assignment among its exemptions.
    • Pc 37 — eating at the wrong time, whose Vibhaṅga gives the wrong conduct for taking a tonic as food.
    • Pc 38 — eating stored food, the offence a tonic mixed with later food becomes.
    • Pc 39 — asking for fine foods, ghee, butter, oil and honey among them, when not sick.
    • Pc 47 — the invitation to ask for medicines and other requisites, and its terms.

    Related Suttas

    • MN 2 — using "the medicinal requisites only for protection from arisen afflicting feelings and for the benefit of good health"; a tonic is taken for a sickness, and the seven days keep it that.68
    • AN 4.27 — "Putrid urine is a trifle among medicines, easily gained and blameless", one of the four trifles with which a bhikkhu is satisfied; a monk satisfied with the plainest medicine has no reason to store the richest.69

The factors, the oils, sugars and navanīta, the Sub-commentary's standard for use, the per-item count, shared ownership and the two elders with his argument on the final clause are Thanissaro's BMC1, and the commentaries are cited from his report of them, the external uses and the dawn from Brahmāli's endnotes, the self-made tonics from Ajahn Brahm's report; the reading of "keeps" and the seventh dawn are the Manual; the four classes, the cheese case, the name test's examples, the uses of gilāna, the mixtures, the one-way door, the tonics a monk makes himself, the determination examples and the condition of a real gift are Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes; Ñāṇatusita supplies the language of navanīta.
