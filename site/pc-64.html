    Pc 64

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time Venerable Upananda the Sakyan had committed an offence of intentional emission of semen. He told his brother's student about this, adding, "Don't tell anyone."1

    Soon afterwards another monk also committed an offence of intentional emission of semen. He asked the Saṅgha for probation, which he got. While he was on probation, he saw Upananda's brother's student and said to him, "I've committed an offence of intentional emission of semen. I asked the Saṅgha for probation, which I got. I'm now undergoing probation. Please remember me as such."2

    "Do others who have committed this offence need to do the same?"

    "Yes."

    "Venerable Upananda committed this offence and told me not to tell anyone."

    "So did you conceal it?"

    "Yes."3

    That monk then told other monks. The monks of few desires complained and criticised him: "How could a monk knowingly conceal a monk's grave offence?" They told the Buddha, who questioned him and rebuked him: "Foolish man, how could you do this? This will affect people's confidence." A monk who knowingly conceals a monk's grave offence commits an offence entailing confession.4

    Synopsis — Upananda asked the student of his brother to keep his saṅghādisesa quiet, and the request came to light because a second monk, doing the duties of probation, said out loud what a monk in that position is required to say. The rule that follows makes the silence itself the offence.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk knowingly conceals a monk's grave offense, he commits an offense entailing confession."5

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu conceals a bhikkhu's serious offence, he incurs a pācittiya."6

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu knowingly conceal (another) bhikkhu's serious offense, it is to be confessed."7

    Brahm

    "Concealing another monk's serious offence is an offence of Pācittiya."8

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should knowingly conceal a bhikkhu's depraved offence, [this is a case] involving expiation."9

    The renderings agree on what the rule forbids and part in two places. Brahmāli's recitation line and Ajahn Brahm leave out jānaṁ, "knowingly".10​ 11​ 12 The Vibhaṅga defines the word and builds its permutations on it, so that a monk who is unsure of the offence, or takes a grave one for a minor one, falls to a lesser penalty; that grading is taken up below, under Perception.13​ 14 The "another" that Thanissaro brackets and Ajahn Brahm prints is the word-analysis's own: "A monk's: another monk's."15

    The other difference is duṭṭhulla — "grave" in Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga, "serious" in three renderings, "depraved" in Ñāṇatusita's. He argues for his word at Sg 3, where the same adjective describes speech: it "cannot be simply translated as 'lewd'", because the grave offences of this rule "encompass all the Pār and Sd offences, and most of these have nothing to do with sexuality."16 Brahmāli's Glossary gives the word all three senses, "indecent; grave; coarse".17 Here it names a class of offence, and the Vibhaṅga's definition of the class is taken up under Object.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule protects the trust a community of monks lives by, together with the Saṅgha's health: a grave offence hidden by another monk's silence goes uncorrected, and the honour system that monastic life rests on begins to fail. Brahmāli puts it in a sentence, "Monasticism is an honor system that relies on trust", and draws the consequence that grave wrongdoing has to be rooted out quickly, before the trust fails.18 The origin story shows how such a silence comes to light. No one inquired into it. A monk on probation did what probation requires, telling a fellow monk of his offence, and the student Upananda had sworn to secrecy asked whether everyone who commits it must do the same.

    This rule is the other face of Pc 9. There a monk says too much, telling a monk's grave offence to someone not fully ordained; here he says nothing to those who ought to know. Thanissaro gives the offence four factors: object, perception, intention and effort.19 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual counts three, folding the knowing into the decision not to tell; neither count adds or removes a condition, and the difference is only whether a state of mind is listed beside the act or inside it.20 I follow Thanissaro's four, since the Vibhaṅga's own grading runs along the perception.

    Object

    The offence concealed must be another monk's, and it must be grave. The Vibhaṅga defines the word in one line: "the four offenses entailing expulsion and the thirteen entailing suspension."21 Thanissaro reports a narrower reading from the Commentary, as if the compilers meant the saṅghādisesas only, and answers that "as was also the case under Pc 9, this explanation clearly contradicts the Vibhaṅga, so it cannot stand", and the Vibhaṅga's definition is followed here.22 The fuller argument belongs to Pc 9, where the same restriction is made and disputed. Any lesser offence of a monk, and the misconduct of anyone not fully ordained, is an object for an offence of wrong conduct only.23 A monk's concealment of his own saṅghādisesa is a different matter, and it is paid for in probation (see Related Offences).

    Perception

    He must know of the offence, and the Vibhaṅga says how: "he knows by himself or others have told him or the offender has told him."24 It then grades by perception. A grave offence perceived as grave and concealed is the pācittiya. A grave offence he is unsure of, or takes for a minor one, is an offence of wrong conduct if he conceals it.25

    Intention

    The intention is written into the definition. A monk conceals when he resolves not to tell, thinking that if others find out they will accuse the offender, remind him, scold him, censure him or humiliate him.26 Thanissaro reads the five verbs as two fears. The first is that other monks will take the formal steps of an inquiry against the offender, and the second is that they will jeer at him. Either fear fulfils the factor.27 Any other reason for silence does not, and one of the non-offence clauses says so in as many words.28

    Friendship with the offender is the usual source of that motive, and fear of a monk one finds intimidating is the other. The third exemption marks where fear becomes a reason the rule accepts: it covers a monk who thinks the offender "cruel and harsh" and a threat to life or to the monastic life.29 Brahmāli drew the line there in class: intimidation alone is no reason to keep quiet, only a real danger, a threat of violence or the like.30 A monk who keeps quiet to shield the offender from correction falls under the rule; one who keeps quiet because the offender is violent is protecting himself.

    Effort

    The Vibhaṅga fixes the moment: the offence falls "by the mere fact of abandoning his duty".31 There is no further act and no result to wait for. The laying down of the duty is the offence, and Thanissaro gives the Commentary's consequence: telling afterwards does not undo it. He also adds a repair. The Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī, he notes, treats the effort as a mere decision, an act of mind, which goes against a principle the commentaries themselves draw from Pj 2, that the arising of a state of mind is never by itself enough for an offence. He therefore reads the factor through the non-offence clauses, which exempt a monk who sees no suitable monk to tell. The effort is fulfilled when, seeing a suitable monk, one decides not to tell him.32 Ajahn Brahm's conditions come to the same — knowledge, the intention to conceal, and the opportunity to inform — and he adds the everyday negative: a monk who says nothing because there is no one to tell, or because he forgets, has not concealed.33

    Thanissaro notes that none of the texts define a suitable monk; following the Commentary to Cv.III, he takes it to mean one of common affiliation and in good standing, and he adds the point that decides most real cases: the monk need not be on congenial terms with anyone. If one's only fear is that he will jeer at the offender or start a Community transaction against him, one is "duty bound to tell him."34 A monk may pass over the first suitable monk to tell a more fitting one — a senior member of the Community, a Vinaya expert, the offender's preceptor — since a silence not aimed at concealment is no offence; and once he has told one suitable monk, it seems, he need tell no one else. If the monk he has told turns out to want the matter buried, the texts, he notes, are silent. Unless one of the exempting dangers applies, a responsible monk, Thanissaro suggests, would find someone more responsible, "but this is a matter of one's conscience and not of the rules."35

    Concealment can also be shared. Thanissaro gives the Commentary's ruling that however many monks join in keeping the offence secret to prevent an inquiry into it, all of them are guilty of the offence.36

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The six clauses, set out under Exemptions, are generous for a rule of this seriousness. The fifth releases any silence not aimed at concealment, and it is what lets a monk wait for a better person to tell; the third reaches a real danger; and the sixth, which Ajahn Brahm reads as concealing "expecting the monk to confess by himself", lets the offender come forward first.37​ 38 Brahmāli allowed the offender that time so long as one's intention is pure, but not indefinitely: "after a few weeks you probably should do something." He was clear, too, about the order of approach: the monk himself first, so that he can put it right himself, then the abbot, and never the whole community, for that is how rumours start. "The point is not to offend the other person; it is to be nice to the other person."39 The rule makes silence an offence, and it does not thereby make gossip a duty. What it asks is that the right person hear, and that the offender be given the chance to speak first.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — concealing a grave offence one is unsure of, or perceives as minor.40
    • Dukkaṭa — concealing a minor offence, however it is perceived.41
    • Dukkaṭa — concealing the grave or minor misconduct of one not fully ordained. Thanissaro takes this to cover nuns, female trainees and novices of both sexes but not lay people, since monks have no duty to report lay misdeeds.42​ 43
    • Pācittiya (Commentary) — for each monk who joins in the concealment.44

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence "if he does not tell because he thinks there will be quarrels or disputes in the Sangha; if he does not tell because he thinks there will be a fracture or schism in the Sangha; if he does not tell because he thinks the person he is telling about is cruel and harsh and that he might become a threat to life or to the monastic life; if he does not tell because he does not see any suitable monks; if he does not tell, but not because he wants to conceal; if he does not tell because he thinks the other person will be known through his own actions"; nor if he is insane, or the first offender.45

    Related Offences

    • Pc 9 — telling someone not fully ordained of a monk's grave offence, without the Saṅgha's agreement: the converse rule, and the place where Buddhaghosa's narrower reading of "grave" is reported and set aside.46
    • The conclusion of the saṅghādisesa section — concealing one's own grave offence, which is paid for in probation for as many days as it was concealed, then six days of mānatta, then rehabilitation by a Saṅgha of at least twenty.47
    • Kd 12 — the duties of a monk on probation, among them the duty to inform that brought this rule's origin to light.48
    • Bi Pj 6 — for the nuns, concealing another nun's pārājika is itself a pārājika.49
    • Sg 8, 9 — groundlessly accusing a monk of a pārājika: the far side of the same duty, penalising speech about what one does not know where this rule penalises silence about what one does.50
    • Pc 12 — evasive speech, where a monk hides his own offence under questioning; the Concise BMC pairs it with this rule.51
    • Sg 12 — refusing correction altogether, the far end of the apparatus of mutual admonition to which this rule belongs; Ajahn Brahm carries this rule's exemptions across to it.52

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register, with the four exchanges given verbatim and the interrogation and rebuke in the standing summary form. Ajahn Brahm's retelling names the concealer only as "a fellow monk" and supplies procedural background the Vibhaṅga assumes; it is not merged into the Story. The Manual, the recitation, the class transcript (session 029, 19 June 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were all checked. The commentaries are known here only as Thanissaro reports them. Ajahn Brahm twice cites the Vinaya-mukha, which is not used in this book, and those citations are not followed. The class is cited for its practice counsel, how long to wait and whom to tell first; its other points are carried by the Vibhaṅga and Thanissaro.

    33. Ajahn Brahm, Vinaya Notes, vol. 2, Pc 64. Rule: "Concealing another monk's serious offence is an offence of Pācittiya." The conditions: "For one's inaction to become 'concealing' one must have knowledge (seen or heard) of another monk's offence, one intends to conceal and one has the opportunity to inform but one does not. For example, when one does not inform because there are no monks around to tell, or because one forgets, then it is not counted as concealing." And: "When one conceals expecting the monk to confess by himself then there is also no offence."↩︎
