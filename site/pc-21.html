    Pc 21

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time the senior monks who were instructing the nuns received robe-cloth, almsfood, dwellings, and medicinal supplies. When the monks from the group of six found out about this, they thought, "Well then, let's instruct the nuns." They went to the nuns and said, "Come to us, sisters, and we too will instruct you."

    Soon afterwards those nuns went to the monks from the group of six, bowed, and sat down. But after giving just a short teaching, those monks spent the day on worldly talk. They then dismissed the nuns, saying, "Go, sisters."

    The nuns went to the Buddha and bowed, and the Buddha said to them, "Nuns, I hope the instruction was effective?"

    "Sir, how could the instruction be effective? After giving just a short teaching, the monks from the group of six spent the day on worldly talk, and then dismissed us."

    The Buddha instructed, inspired, and gladdened those nuns with a teaching. The nuns bowed, circumambulated the Buddha with their right sides toward him, and left. Soon afterwards the Buddha had the Saṅgha gathered, questioned the monks from the group of six, confirmed that it was so, rebuked them, and told the monks to appoint an instructor of the nuns by a formal motion and three announcements. He then laid down the training rule.1

    After the rule had been laid down, the senior monks who instructed the nuns, now appointed to do so, still received robe-cloth, almsfood, dwellings, and medicinal supplies. When the monks from the group of six found out about this, they said, "Well then, let's go outside the monastery zone, appoint each other as instructors of the nuns, and then instruct them." After doing just that, they again went to the nuns and said, "Sisters, we too have been appointed. Come to us, and we will instruct you." Once again the nuns went to the monks from the group of six and everything unfolded as before. The Buddha had the Saṅgha gathered, questioned the monks from the group of six, confirmed that it was so, rebuked them, and laid down the eight qualities a monk must possess before the Saṅgha may appoint him.2

    Synopsis — The group of six, seeing that the monks who instruct the nuns are well supplied, appoint themselves to the task and then waste the day in worldly talk; the Buddha restricts the instruction to a monk the Saṅgha has formally appointed, and, when the group of six appoint one another outside the monastery zone, sets out the eight qualities such a monk must have.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk who has not been appointed instructs the nuns, he commits an offense entailing confession."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu gives the ovāda to the bhikkhunīs, except with the Saṅgha's agreement, he incurs a pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu, unauthorized, exhort the bhikkhunīs, it is to be confessed."5

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Exhorting the bhikkhunīs without the Sangha's permission is an offence of Pācittiya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu who has not been authorised should exhort the bhikkhunīs, [this is a case] involving expiation."7

    The rule has two words that matter, and the renderings part on one of them. Asammato, "not appointed", is kept as an adjective on the monk by Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation, Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita, and turned into an exception, "except with the Saṅgha's agreement", by the recitation and Ajahn Brahm; the sense is the same. Ovadeyya is "instructs" in Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga, "exhort" in Thanissaro, Ajahn Brahm and Ñāṇatusita, and in the recitation it is left as the name of an institution, "gives the ovāda".8 That spread is not merely stylistic. Whether the word means the fortnightly recitation of the eight important principles, or any teaching at all, is the rule's one real dispute, and it is taken up under Effort.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule guards a monk against taking up the teaching of the nuns for what it brings him, by leaving the choice of their teacher to his Saṅgha, for the restraint of monks like the group of six and the well-being of the Saṅgha. The Buddha's question to the nuns, "I hope the instruction was effective?", is the measure the Story applies, and the group of six failed it twice. They wanted the role because the senior monks who held it were well supplied, and gave a short teaching before spending the day on worldly talk; and when the Buddha required an appointment, they appointed one another outside the monastery zone. So the rule came to require both a formal act of the Saṅgha and a monk fit to be chosen by it.9

    This is the first rule of the chapter on the ovāda, the instruction a monk appointed by his Saṅgha gives the bhikkhunīs every half-month at their request, and it sets out the occasion's machinery; Pc 22 and Pc 23 guard the same occasion at its hour and its place. Thanissaro gives the offence two factors, object and effort, and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual gives four.10​ 11 I keep Thanissaro's two, since the Manual's teaching and its want of agreement both fall inside his effort, and the appointment, a whole formal procedure, sits there with them.

    Object

    "Nuns" means "they have been given the full ordination by both Sanghas."12 A bhikkhunī was ordained first in the Bhikkhunī Saṅgha and then in the Bhikkhu Saṅgha, and only one who has completed both is grounds for the pācittiya; one ordained on one side only is grounds for wrong conduct, and a trainee nun or a novice nun for nothing.13 Number is not a factor: the Manual gives "one or more bhikkhunīs".14

    Effort

    To instruct without being appointed, and "not appointed" means not appointed "through a legal procedure consisting of one motion and three announcements".15 The Vibhaṅga gives the procedure in full: the monk is first asked whether he is willing, then a competent monk puts the motion to the Saṅgha and the three announcements, and the Saṅgha's silence carries it.16 After the group of six appointed one another, the Buddha set out eight qualities a monk must have before the Saṅgha may appoint him. He is virtuous and restrained by the Monastic Code; he has learned much; he has properly learned both Monastic Codes in detail; he is well-spoken and has a good voice; he is liked by the nuns; he is capable of instructing them; he has never committed a heavy offence against a nun; and he has been fully ordained for at least twenty years.17 A monk undergoing penance or probation, or under the duties of a censure or other disciplinary act, should not accept the appointment, and if appointed should not instruct; the group of six, Thanissaro adds from the Commentary, never had these qualities even in their dreams.18 How long an appointment lasts is, in Brahmāli's view, for the Saṅgha to decide, since the texts give no instruction on it.19

    As in the pattern Thanissaro sets out at Pc 4, the monk's perception is not a mitigating factor, though the grid is larger here. It crosses the legitimacy of the appointing procedure with the completeness of the nuns' assembly, each with the monk's perception of it. Where the procedure was illegitimate, every case is a pācittiya, even where he believes it legitimate; where it was legitimate, every case is wrong conduct, except the one where the procedure is legitimate, the assembly complete, and he perceives both correctly.20​ 21 What carries the full offence is the defect in the Saṅgha's act, not the monk's belief about it.

    Disputed — whether "instructs" reaches only the eight important principles, or any teaching of the Dhamma.

    The Vibhaṅga's word-analysis draws the line at the principles, instructing in them being the full offence and in any other teaching wrong conduct, and Thanissaro builds this into his factor.22​ 23 The Manual reads the rule more widely, on two grounds: in the Nikāyas ovadati means teaching in general, and the Vibhaṅga's own procedure has the monk recite the principles only when the nuns are not keeping them, and otherwise give "the instruction", as though the two were different things. Brahmāli concludes that the word "most likely refers to any teaching of the Dhamma".24 Ajahn Brahm describes the procedure the same way, the monk free to talk "on any point of Dhamma or Discipline that he chose", though he draws no conclusion from it.25 The nuns' own Vibhaṅga defines "the instruction" as the eight principles at their Pc 58 and Pc 59.26 The stake is a grade of penalty: for an ordinary Dhamma talk by an unappointed monk, wrong conduct on the word-analysis, the full offence on Brahmāli's reading. Whether the procedure and the definition really conflict, and whether that would license setting the definition aside, no text settles; an unappointed monk who teaches the nuns only as the exemptions allow is safe on either reading.

    A monk may give a summary teaching, question the nuns, teach when they ask him to, and let nuns listen in while he teaches others.27​ 28 Brahmāli's example of the last is close to home: nuns who come to a monastery's regular evening talk are "just in a sense listening in" to a teaching given to others, which is not the ovāda at all.29

    For the monk who is appointed, the Vibhaṅga scripts the occasion. He sweeps the yard, sets out water, prepares a seat, gets a companion, and sits down. When the nuns have come and bowed, he asks, "Are you all here, sisters?" If they are, he asks, "Are you keeping the eight important principles?" If they are, he says, "This is the instruction"; if they are not, he recites the principles.30 If not everyone is there, he does not go on: instructing an incomplete assembly is wrong conduct, though Thanissaro cites the Sub-commentary for the point that nuns absent through illness do not make it incomplete.31​ 32

    The principles he recites, each "to be honored and respected all one's life, and … not to be breached", are these. A nun who has been fully ordained for a hundred years should bow down to a monk who was given the full ordination on that very day, and she should stand up for him, raise her joined palms to him, and do acts of respect toward him. A nun shouldn't spend the rainy-season residence in a monastery without monks. Every half-month a nun should seek two things from the Saṅgha of monks: asking it about the observance day and going to it for the instruction. A nun who has completed the rainy-season residence should invite correction from both Saṅghas in regard to three things: what has been seen, heard, or suspected. A nun who has committed a heavy offence must undertake a trial period for a half-month toward both Saṅghas. A trainee nun who has trained for two years in the six rules may seek for full ordination in both Saṅghas. A nun may not in any way abuse or revile a monk. From today onwards, nuns may not correct monks, but monks may correct nuns.33

    The rule began by keeping unqualified monks out of the instruction, and the Khandhaka ends by keeping qualified ones in it. When monks declined to give the half-monthly instruction, the Buddha made declining wrong conduct, except for a monk who is ignorant, sick, or about to depart; a monk living in the wilderness must agree and make an appointment to return.34 The instruction is a duty as well as a trust: a monk may not take it up until his Saṅgha gives it to him, and once it has, he may not lightly set it down.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • A dukkaṭa for an unappointed monk who instructs the nuns in a teaching other than the eight important principles, on the word-analysis; disputed above.35
    • A dukkaṭa for instructing a nun fully ordained on one side only.36
    • A dukkaṭa in every case where the appointing procedure was legitimate but the assembly was incomplete, or the monk misperceived either.37
    • Three dukkaṭas for an appointed monk who mishandles the occasion: giving another teaching after establishing that the nuns are all present, instead of asking about the principles; instructing in the principles when the nuns are not all present; and giving another teaching without first saying, "This is the instruction." Brahmāli's endnotes say it is "not entirely clear" how the first arises and call the reading of the third "a bit contrived", though perhaps right.38​ 39

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he recites; if he tests them; if he recites when asked to do so; if he asks a question; if he replies when asked a question; if he is speaking for the benefit of someone else and the nuns listen in; if it is to a trainee nun; if it is to a novice nun; if he is insane; if he is the first offender.40 "Tests them" is a teacher questioning a student to find out how much she knows, and "recites" means repeating a passage from memory. Thanissaro doubts the Commentary's claim that a monk so invited may speak on any topic, because osāreti is not the Vibhaṅga's verb for speaking on a topic.41​ 42

    Related Offences

    • Pc 22, 23 — instructing the nuns after sunset, and at their dwelling place; both borrow this rule's object and non-offence clauses, Pc 23 its graded definition of instructing too; at Pc 22 any teaching is the full offence.
    • Pc 24 — saying that monks instruct the nuns for the sake of gain; the mirror of this rule, protecting the appointed instructor from a false charge.
    • Pc 7 — teaching a woman more than five or six sentences of Dhamma without a knowledgeable man present; the informal limit, where this rule is the formal and authorised occasion, and the two share the exemption for answering a question.
    • Bhikkhunī Pc 58, 59 — the nuns' rules on going to the instruction and asking for it, which define it as the eight important principles.
    • Kd 20:9.4–9.5 — the delegation that asks for the instruction, and the dukkaṭa for a monk who declines to give it.

    Related Suttas

    • AN 8.52 — Ānanda asks what qualities a monk needs "to be agreed upon as an exhorter of bhikkhunīs", and the Buddha gives the Vibhaṅga's eight, ending with "seniority of twenty years or more".43
    • MN 146 — the instruction given as the rule intends: the elder monks "taking turns in advising the bhikkhunīs", Nandaka teaching by question and answer, and the Buddha's verdict that the nuns' "intention has been fulfilled".44

In the Nikāyas, the word ovadati and its cognate forms has no special relation to the eight garudhammas but rather refers to teaching in general… At this point nothing is said about the garudhammas, just that the ovāda is to be given… The obvious implication is that the monk should only recite the garudhammas if the bhikkhunīs are not keeping them… I conclude that ovadati in the present rule most likely refers to any teaching of the Dhamma."↩︎
