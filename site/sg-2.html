    Sg 2

    Story (Vatthu)

    At one time the Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove. Venerable Udāyī was staying in the wilderness, in a beautiful dwelling with a room in the middle and corridors on all sides. The bed and bench were nicely made up, the water for drinking and the water for washing were ready for use, and the yards were well swept. Many people came to see the dwelling, among them a brahmin and his wife, who asked to see it. "Well then, brahmin, please do."

    Udāyī took the key, lifted the latch, opened the door, and entered the dwelling. The brahmin entered after him, and then the brahmin lady. Opening some windows and closing others, Udāyī walked around the inner room and came up behind the brahmin lady, touching her all over. The brahmin thanked Udāyī and left, saying, "These Sakyan monastics who live in the wilderness are superb. Venerable Udāyī is superb!" But his wife said, "What's superb about him? He touched me all over just like you do."

    The brahmin then complained and criticized: "How could the ascetic Udāyī touch my wife all over? It's not possible to go to a monastery or a monk's dwelling with a wife from a respectable family, or with a daughter, a girl, a daughter-in-law, or a female slave from a respectable family. If you do, the Sakyan monastics might molest them." The monks heard the criticism, and the monks of few desires complained, "How could Venerable Udāyī make physical contact with a woman?" They told the Buddha, who had the Saṅgha gathered, questioned Udāyī, rebuked him, and laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — Venerable Udāyī, showing a brahmin and his wife round his dwelling, touches the wife all over; the brahmin complains that no family can safely bring its women to a monastery, and the Buddha lays down the rule against lustful physical contact with a woman.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk, overcome by lust and with a distorted mind, makes physical contact with a woman—holding her hand or hair, or touching any part of her body—he commits an offense entailing suspension."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu, motivated by lust, touches a woman, he incurs a saṅghādisesa."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu, overcome by lust, with altered mind, engage in bodily contact with a woman, or in holding her hand, holding a lock of her hair, or caressing any of her limbs, it entails initial and subsequent meetings of the Community."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "With lustful intentions, coming into physical contact with a woman is a saṅghādisesa."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu, under the influence of an altered mind, should engage in [intimate] physical contact together with a woman [such as]: the holding of a hand, or holding a braid [of hair], or caressing any limb: [this is a case] involving the community in the beginning and in the rest."6

    The five renderings agree on what the rule forbids. The recitation and Ajahn Brahm drop the rule's own list of the hand, the hair and any part of the body, and nothing is lost by it, since the word-analysis defines the hand as reaching "from the elbow to the tip of the nails" and any part of the body as "anything apart from the hand and the hair".7 Where the renderings part is the phrase otiṇṇo vipariṇatena cittena, which Thanissaro gives as two conditions, "overcome by lust, with altered mind", and the recitation and Ajahn Brahm as one, "motivated by lust". Ñāṇatusita holds that the phrase "expresses one idea", and the word-analysis agrees in effect: a mind distorted by anger or confusion is also distorted, "But in this case 'distorted' refers to the lustful mind." There is one requirement here, not two, and it is taken up under Intention.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule guards the monk against himself, and with him the women who come to a monastery. The second of the sexual saṅghādisesas moves from what a monk does to his own body to what he does to someone else's, and Udāyī, whose advice began Sg 1, is the offender again. The brahmin's complaint names the harm: if a monk could do this to a wife with her husband in the room, no family could safely bring its women to a monastery. Thanissaro opens on the same point, since the rule has sometimes been read as a sign of prejudice against women: "the Buddha formulated the rule not because women are bad, but because bhikkhus sometimes can be."8

    Thanissaro gives the offence four factors: object, a living woman; perception, that the monk perceives her as a woman; intention, that he is impelled by lust; and effort, that he comes into physical contact with her.9 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual divides the same ground into six, separating the lust from the aim at contact and adding that the monk must be aware the contact has happened.10 Thanissaro also analyses the contact a monk receives apart from the contact he makes, because the Vibhaṅga uses different terms for the two;11 but the Vibhaṅga's closing permutations reduce both to the same three questions — is he aiming at contact, does he make an effort with the body, does he experience contact — and the Manual's factors do the same.12 I keep Thanissaro's four, with object and perception together, and both kinds of contact fall under Effort, where the Manual's awareness of contact also belongs.

    Object and Perception

    A woman is "a human female, not a female spirit, not a female ghost, not a female animal; even a girl born on that very day, let alone an older one."13 Age makes no difference, and neither does whether she knows what is happening: the case studies give the full offence for contact with a sleeping woman.14 Ajahn Brahm draws the consequence plainly: molesting a very young girl, even a baby, is the full offence.15

    Every other being is a lesser category. The case studies give a serious offence for a female spirit, a paṇḍaka and a dead woman, and wrong conduct for a female animal and a wooden doll; the permutations add a man, at wrong conduct.16 Thanissaro reports the Commentary's refinements: the dead woman's serious offence holds only while the body is intact enough for intercourse under Pj 1, and is wrong conduct after; the doll extends to any female form and even to a picture of a woman; female devas count with female spirits, and female nāgas with female animals.17 Ajahn Brahm instead puts nāgas and ghosts with spirits, at the serious offence;18 the Vibhaṅga's own case names only the female spirit, and the difference is one grade of minor offence. One exception among animals stands out: a Khandhaka rule gives a serious offence for touching the genitals of cattle, where any other animal is wrong conduct, and the texts give no reason.19

    Paṇḍaka Brahmāli leaves untranslated. The Canon never defines it; from Pj 1's list of four kinds of human being — women, gynandromorphs, paṇḍakas and men — he concludes that the paṇḍaka was thought of as neither male nor female, a group so varied that no English umbrella term captures it.20 The Commentary lists five kinds: one who allays his desire by taking another man's penis into his mouth, a voyeur, a eunuch, one who is a paṇḍaka only in the waning fortnight, and one undeveloped from conception; the first two may go forth and the other three may not.21 Whether the first two count as paṇḍakas or as men under the Pātimokkha rules the Commentary does not say. Thanissaro argues that they count as men, since if validly ordained monks could have been paṇḍakas under these rules the texts would have had to say how to treat them, and they do not; he cautions, too, against reading the first kind as covering homosexual men in general.22 Ajahn Brahm thinks the term most likely named people raised to the role from childhood for the sake of a profession.23 Here the question decides between a serious offence and wrong conduct; at Pc 6, Pc 44, Pc 45 and Pc 67 it decides whether there is an offence at all, and it is left to them. Whether a paṇḍaka may be ordained belongs to the Khandhaka and is not taken up here.

    Perception changes the penalty, as Thanissaro reads the permutations, only for women and paṇḍakas. A woman whom the monk takes for a paṇḍaka, a man or an animal, or about whom he is unsure, is a serious offence; a paṇḍaka taken for anything else is wrong conduct; a man or an animal is wrong conduct whatever he takes it for.24 Thanissaro's example is a man dressed as a woman and taken for one, still wrong conduct; Ajahn Brahm's runs the other way, a girl molested in the belief that she is a boy, a serious offence.25

    Intention

    Overcome by lust is defined as "having lust, longing for, in love with", and the distorted mind, as above, is the lustful one.26 Ajahn Brahm observes that these definitions might suggest a strong and lasting state, and that the Samantapāsādikā requires less: a momentary impulse to touch an attractive woman's hand, acted on, is the full offence.27 Thanissaro reaches the same point from the Commentary's account of the altered mind, so that a passing attraction fulfils the factor.28 The Commentary also narrows the lust to the wish to enjoy the contact, under its scheme of eleven kinds of lust, so that a monk who touched a woman in lust for intercourse would have only a dukkaṭa. Thanissaro rejects the scheme here as at Pj 1, whose case studies give this rule's offence where an attempt at intercourse stops at the touch, and holds that any state of passion fulfils the factor;29 the argument belongs to Pj 1.

    The lust must also be one the monk can see in himself. Brahmāli warns in class against monks who confess this offence for touching a small girl just in case a single moment of lust, too quick to notice, had arisen: that, he says, "is not the point of this rule", whose lust is one "that you actually can experience", "a significant lust, and it's something that you certainly can see".30

    Without lust there is no saṅghādisesa, but not every other motive is free. A monk who touched his mother, his daughter or his sister out of affection was given wrong conduct; immediately after them, a monk who made physical contact with his ex-wife was given the full offence.31 She is family of a kind, and the case may be placed there to show that the allowance follows the kind of affection, not the degree of kinship; that reading is ours, and no source remarks on the case. Contact made in order to get away is no offence at all: the closing permutations give none to a monk "aiming to free himself", whatever effort he makes and whatever he feels, which covers the monk who pushes away a woman pressing herself on him.32

    For other motives, such as rescue, the Vibhaṅga says nothing, and the Commentary fills the gap with anāmāsa, a list of things a monk may not touch, women at its head, so that any intentional contact with a woman is wrong conduct; it then describes how a monk should behave when his mother is drowning, allowing him to hold out a rope or a board but not to take hold of her.33 Thanissaro finds no direct basis for it in the Canon and reports that many respected Vinaya teachers make an exception for touching a woman to save her from danger. Ajahn Brahm rejects the list outright, since it "has no support from the Vinayapiṭaka" and the escape permutation contradicts it, and follows the Vibhaṅga alone: wrong conduct for familial affection, and no offence for other motives that have nothing to do with lust.34 A monk may pull a girl from the path of a car or catch an old woman as she falls. Ajahn Brahm adds the warning that goes with the permission: such contact should be allowed only when it is truly necessary, because once contact with a woman is felt, lust is easily aroused, and a second contact may be the saṅghādisesa.35

    One modern case is the handshake. Brahmāli holds in class that a monk who shakes a woman's hand because she has held it out has no offence, since he is not acting from lust; but the mind can change very quickly, and any further movement made once lust has arisen is a new effort.36

    Effort

    Makes physical contact is defined simply as "misconduct", and the effort is any of a long list: touching, stroking downwards or upwards, pulling down, lifting up, pulling, pushing, squeezing, pressing, taking hold of, contacting.37 The case of a lustful monk who struck a woman with his shoulder as she passed, given the full offence, shows that the list covers any similar action,38 and the case of a lustful monk who raised his foot while a woman was paying respect to him, also the full offence, shows how little is enough.39 Ajahn Brahm gives a modern instance: a monk who places himself by the exit of a crowded bus, hoping a woman will brush past him, has the full offence if she does.40

    The Vibhaṅga grades contact by how directly it is made. The monk's body to her body is the full offence. His body to something connected to hers, or something connected to his body to hers, is a serious offence, and the case studies give it for pulling a rope or a staff a woman was holding and for pushing her with an almsbowl. Something connected to his body against something connected to hers, or something thrown, is wrong conduct, and so is shaking the bridge, the tree or the boat she is on.41

    Disputed — whether contact through the clothes a woman is wearing is direct or indirect.

    The Commentary treats it as indirect: unless the cloth is thin enough for the contact to be "skin-to-skin, skin-to-hair, or hair-to-hair", a monk who fondles a clothed woman has only a serious offence.42 Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes follow it, praising the point about thin cloth and giving the serious offence to a monk who pats a woman's coat where it covers her buttocks, though an editor's note records that "many other bhikkhus doubt this 'rubber glove theory'".43 Thanissaro argues that the Vibhaṅga follows ordinary usage, in which one who pushes a clothed woman is "pushing both the cloth and her": in none of the case studies does the Buddha ask whether the contact was with a clothed part of the body, not even in the shoulder case, where either of them might have been covered or bare, and the indirect contacts the case studies name are with things a woman is holding.44 The Manual agrees that the commentarial idea "is not directly traceable to the Vibhaṅga" and gives a working test: if she is clothed, the full offence arises only if the monk feels her body under the clothes, so that the question of body hairs falls away.45 The case studies favour Thanissaro, and the Manual's test applies his reading where it is least clear, so I would teach the Manual's test; but the Commentary's reading is the inherited one, and the case studies settle the matter only by their silence, and the lean that rests on it is ours.

    A monk who aims at contact and exerts himself but touches nothing has wrong conduct, as in the case of one who thought, "I'll take hold of a woman", and did not make contact.46

    We now come to the contact a monk receives. The same three questions decide it. If he aims to free himself there is no offence, whatever he does and whatever he feels: the case studies give none to a monk whom a number of women led about arm in arm, because he did not consent, and the Commentary adds a monk who keeps perfectly still under a lustful woman's advances, thinking that she will lose interest and go.47 If he wants the contact, he has the full offence if he makes an effort with the body and experiences contact, wrong conduct if he makes an effort but experiences none, and no offence if he makes no effort.48 The effort can be very small. The Commentary counts a wink, a raised eyebrow, rolled eyes or a wagging finger;49 Ajahn Brahm counts a word of instruction as well, though the Vibhaṅga's own phrase is an effort "with the body".50 Where the monk's aim changes in the course of the contact, from wanting it to wanting to escape, the Sub-commentary holds that the second aim is what counts.51

    So the monk who stands perfectly still while an infatuated woman embraces him, and enjoys it, has no offence. Ajahn Brahm states the case without softening it: even if he "enjoyed every minute of the contact", so long as he made no effort to encourage, assist or prolong it, he has no offence.52 The rule stops there, at what the Saṅgha can judge, and the Buddha's teaching does not. Asked by the brahmin Jānussoṇi what breaks the celibate life, he began with an ascetic who "does not actually engage in intercourse with women. But he consents to being rubbed, massaged, bathed, and kneaded by them. He relishes this, desires it, and finds satisfaction in it", and called it "a breach, flaw, blemish, and blotch of the celibate life."53 What the rule leaves without a penalty, the path still counts as a fault.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Thullaccaya — lustful contact with a woman he takes for a paṇḍaka, a man or an animal, or about whom he is unsure; with a paṇḍaka, a female spirit or a dead woman; with the genitals of cattle; and contact through something connected to the body, whether he makes it or, wanting it, makes an effort while she does.54
    • Dukkaṭa — lustful contact with a paṇḍaka he takes for something else, a man, an animal or a doll; contact between two things connected to the body, or by something thrown; shaking what she stands, sits or has climbed on; an effort that makes no contact; and touching a mother, daughter or sister out of affection.55
    • Saṅghādisesa — one for each person: a monk who lustfully rubs against two women in a bus incurs two saṅghādisesas, and the Commentary adds one for each separate act. Thanissaro notes that the difference shows only in the formal proclamations that go with the penalty.56

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if it is unintentional; if he is not mindful; if he does not know; if he does not consent; if he is insane; if he is deranged; if he is overwhelmed by pain; or if he is the first offender.57 Thanissaro illustrates the first four: touching a woman by accident as she puts food in his bowl; pushing her away, startled, when she runs into him; not knowing that he has run into a woman in a crowd, or being touched while he is asleep; and the monk led about arm in arm, who did not consent.58 The list leaves out the non-offences of the closing permutations, which stand all the same: a monk who aims to free himself, and one who wants the contact but makes no effort.59

    Related Offences

    • Sg 1 — emission: a monk who lets a woman bring him to emission has that rule's offence even if he lies still, the exception Thanissaro notes to this rule's allowance for contact received without effort.60
    • Pj 1 — an attempt at intercourse that stops at the touch, which the case studies there give this rule's offence; the serious offence for mouth-to-mouth penetration, to which Thanissaro adds this rule's penalty where there is lust; and the Commentary's eleven kinds of lust, rejected there.
    • Sg 3 — lewd speech, which also grades remarks about things connected with the body, and from which Thanissaro suggests the Commentary may have drawn its reading of clothing.61
    • Pc 52 — tickling, an offence even without lust.
    • Pc 74 — striking in anger, the counterpart of contact in lust, with the same allowance for striking out in order to escape.62
    • Pc 6, 44, 45, 67 — rules on which it turns whether the first two kinds of paṇḍaka count as men.

    Related Suttas

    • SN 35.127 — Piṇḍola Bhāradvāja's advice to King Udena, to regard women as mothers, sisters and daughters, and the king's answer that lust sometimes arises even towards these; no woman is outside the rule because of who she is.63
    • SN 47.6 — the quail seized by the hawk for straying out of its own resort into the domain of others, which for a monk is "the five cords of sensual pleasure", tactile objects among them.64
    • AN 7.50 — the seven bonds of sexuality short of intercourse, the first of them consenting to be rubbed and massaged by women, the edge the rule draws at the contact a monk receives.65
