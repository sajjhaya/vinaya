    Sg 6

    Story (Vatthu)

    At one time the Buddha was staying at Rājagaha in the Bamboo Grove. At that time the monks of Āḷavī were building huts by means of begging. The huts were intended for themselves, had no sponsoring owner, and were inappropriately large, and since they were never finished, the monks kept on begging and asking, "Please give a man, a servant, an ox, a cart, a machete, a hatchet, an ax, a spade, a chisel; give creepers, bamboo, reed, grass, clay." People felt so oppressed by the begging and asking that when they saw a monk they became alarmed and fearful. They turned away, took a different path, ran off, and closed their doors. They even ran away when they saw cows, thinking they were monks.

    Venerable Mahākassapa, after completing the rainy-season residence at Rājagaha, came to Āḷavī and stayed at the Aggāḷava Shrine. When he entered Āḷavī for alms, people did the same to him. After his meal he said to the monks, "There used to be plenty of almsfood in Āḷavī, and it was easy to get by on alms. But now there's a shortage, and it's hard to get by. Why is that?" The monks told him. When the Buddha came to Āḷavī soon afterwards, Mahākassapa told him what had happened, and the Buddha had the Saṅgha gathered, questioned the monks of Āḷavī, rebuked them, and gave a teaching.

    "Once upon a time, monks, two sages who were brothers lived near the river Ganges." The dragon king Maṇikaṇṭha emerged from the river, encircled the younger sage with seven coils, and spread his large hood over his head, and from fear of the dragon the younger sage became thin, haggard, and pale. His elder brother asked whether he had seen anything belonging to the dragon, and he said he had seen a gem on its neck. "In that case, ask the dragon for that gem." Three times the dragon came, and each time the sage said, "Sir, give me the gem. I want the gem." The dragon king spoke these verses:

    My food and drink are abundant and sublime, And they appear because of this gem. I won't give it to you—you ask too much— Nor will I return to your hermitage. Like a youth holding a sword polished on a rock, You frighten me, asking for this gem. I won't give it to you—you ask too much— Nor will I return to your hermitage.

    He left and never returned, and the younger sage, no longer seeing that beautiful dragon, became even thinner and paler. The elder sage spoke to him in verse:

    One shouldn't beg from those one wishes to be dear to; One is detested for asking for too much. When the brahmin asked the dragon for his gem, It left and was never to be seen again.

    "One will be disliked even by animals, monks, for begging and asking, let alone by human beings." The Buddha then told of a monk living in a forest grove on the slopes of the Himalayas, disturbed by a great flock of birds that roosted there at night, whom he sent back to call out three times in each part of the night, "Listen to me, good birds. I want a feather from anyone roosting in this forest grove. Each one of you must give me a feather." The birds thought, "The monk is asking for a feather; he wants a feather," and they left the grove and never returned. And he recalled the verse Raṭṭhapāla spoke when his father asked why, of all the people who came begging, his own son did not beg from him:

    The beggar is disliked, And so is one who doesn't give when asked. That's why I do not beg from you; Please don't hate me for this.

    "If the gentleman Raṭṭhapāla could say this to his own father, how much more can one person to another. It's hard, monks, for householders to acquire and protect their possessions. And still, foolish men, you kept on begging and asking for all sorts of things." Then he laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — The monks of Āḷavī beg so persistently for materials for their unfinished huts that the people flee at the sight of a monk; the Buddha tells of a dragon asked for its gem and birds asked for their feathers, and lays down a rule limiting the size of a hut a monk builds by begging for himself and requiring the Saṅgha's approval of its site.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "When a monk, by means of begging, builds a hut without a sponsoring owner and intended for himself, it is to be no more than twelve standard handspans long and seven wide inside. He must have monks approve a site where no harm will be done and which has space on all sides. If a monk, by means of begging, builds a hut on a site where harm will be done and which lacks space on all sides, or he does not have monks approve the site, or he exceeds the right size, he commits an offense entailing suspension."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "It may be that a bhikkhu, by means of begging, is building a kuṭi for himself without a sponsoring owner. If he does not get the Saṅgha to approve the site or if the kuṭi exceeds twelve by seven standard hand-spans, he incurs a saṅghādisesa."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "When a bhikkhu is having a hut built from (gains acquired by) his own begging—having no sponsor and destined for himself—he is to have it built to the standard measurement. Here the standard is this: twelve spans, using the sugata span, in length (measuring outside); seven in width, (measuring) inside. Bhikkhus are to be assembled to designate the site. The site the bhikkhus designate should be without disturbances and with adequate space. If the bhikkhu should have a hut built from his own begging on a site with disturbances and without adequate space, or if he should not assemble the bhikkhus to designate the site, or if he should have the standard exceeded, it entails initial and subsequent meetings of the Community."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Building a kuṭi, without a sponsor, destined for oneself, without having obtained the Saṅgha's approval is a saṅghadisesa. Building a kuṭi, without a sponsor, destined for oneself, exceeding the maximum allowed size is also a saṅghadisesa."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "By a bhikkhu who is having a hut, which is without an owner, [and] is designated for himself, built through means begged by himself, [that hut] is to be built according to the [proper] measure. This is the measure here: twelve spans of the sugata-span in length, [and] inside seven [spans] across. Bhikkhus are to be brought to [it] for appointing the site. By those bhikkhus a site is to be appointed which is not entailing harm [to creatures and which is] having a surrounding space. If a bhikkhu, through means begged by himself, should have a hut built on a site entailing harm [to creatures], [and] not having a surrounding space, or if he should not bring bhikkhus to [it] for appointing the site, or if he should let [it] exceed the measure: [this is a case] involving the community in the beginning and in the rest."6

    The five renderings divide in two places. The recitation and Ajahn Brahm drop the conditions of harm and space from the rule; the Vibhaṅga gives them only wrong conduct, a reading discussed below under Object. And the word for the hut's ownership is rendered "no sponsor" by Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm, "without a sponsoring owner" by Brahmāli and "without an owner" by Ñāṇatusita.7 Which reading is right decides whether a hut on monastery land falls under this rule or the next, and the argument belongs to Sg 7, where the word with an owner is the rule's own subject.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule limits what a monk may build for himself by begging, so that his dwelling does not become the laity's burden. The monks of Āḷavī built huts for themselves, too large and never finished, and the begging that went into them emptied the town's almsbowls and sent people running from cows. The rule answers with a size and a procedure. A hut a monk begs for himself must be small, and its site must be inspected and approved by the Saṅgha before he builds. Ajahn Brahm gives the reason for the size plainly: it prevents overburdening the lay people who supply the materials.8

    Thanissaro gives the offence three factors: effort, completing a hut through begging for its materials; object, a hut that exceeds the standard or whose site the Saṅgha has not approved; and intention, that the hut is for one's own use.9 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual follows the rule's phrases instead.10 Thanissaro's three give the order here.

    Intention

    "Intended for himself" means for his own use.11 Thanissaro gives the Commentary's reading: this means claiming ownership, regarding the hut as "mine", and so a hut built for someone else, a preceptor, a teacher or the Community, is outside the rule; but a monk building a meeting hall who means to claim it and live in it as well is inside it.12 Ajahn Brahm counts an abbot's hut as built for another, since it passes to the next abbot if he leaves.13 In practice, Brahmāli says in class, this rule applies when a monk is living by himself, in the forest or even in a village, and building for himself; a hut inside a monastery will normally be owned by the monastery or its organisation, and then the rule would not apply.14

    Object

    The hut is defined as "plastered inside or plastered outside or plastered both inside and outside".15

    Disputed — whether only a hut plastered in the Commentary's way counts as a hut under the rule.

    What counts as plaster is narrower in the Commentary, which Thanissaro reports as requiring it to be clay or white lime, to be on the roof, and to meet the plaster of the walls, so that a building with a gap left between them is not a hut; Thanissaro grants that these look like loopholes, but finds nothing in the Vibhaṅga to prove them wrong, and suggests that only a fully plastered building was then thought permanent.16 The Manual reads the word as a standard instead, a kuṭi "equivalent to, or better than, one made of wattle and daub".17 Ajahn Brahm reaches the same place from the exempt structures, which are simple and inexpensive: other simple dwellings are exempt with them, and wooden chalets and brick huts are included.18 I lean to Brahmāli and Ajahn Brahm, since the burden the rule regulates is the same whether or not the walls are plastered; but the Commentary's reading is the older one, and nothing in the Vibhaṅga rules it out.

    The size is at most twelve standard spans long, measured outside, and seven wide, measured inside, and neither may be exceeded "even by the width of a hair".19 On Thanissaro's estimate of the span as 25 cm, which the Manual adopts, this is about 3 by 1.75 metres; but the measure is contested. The Commentary's span comes, on Thanissaro's reckoning, to about 75 cm, and Brahmāli's own appendix estimates, very approximately, 60 cm, though he would rather decide a case by the Vinaya's broader criteria, such as not being indulgent, than by exact numbers.20​ 21​ 22 Since each limit stands alone, a hut ten spans by eight breaks the rule on its width, though its floor is smaller than twelve by seven.23

    The site must be one where no harm will be done and which has space on all sides. Harm means the abode of creatures from ants and termites to snakes, elephants and tigers, or the border of a field, a place of execution, a charnel ground, a bar, a street, a public hall and the like;24 the Commentary reads the small creatures as there for their own sake, so as not to destroy their nests.25 Space means room to go around the hut with a yoked cart or with a ladder,26 which Thanissaro does not take to require cutting down the trees.27

    The procedure is the Vibhaṅga's. The monk clears the site, and Thanissaro reads this as clearing the undergrowth so the site can be judged, leaving any trees until it is approved.28 He asks the Saṅgha three times to inspect it; the whole Saṅgha goes, or it appoints inspectors who know what harm and space mean; if the site fails, they tell him not to build there, and if it passes, he asks three times for its approval, and only then builds.29 The transaction for both steps is a motion and one announcement, the form the Vibhaṅga prints and its Pāli names (ñattidutiya), as Thanissaro says.30 The word-analysis in Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga says "one motion and three announcements", and Ajahn Brahm gives three as well; the text they render does not.31

    Disputed — whether a correct-sized hut on an approved site that in fact has harm or lacks space is a saṅghādisesa.

    The rule's wording suggests it, and the Sub-commentary calls that reading a misinterpretation without saying why.32 Ajahn Brahm argues that the approval of such a site is invalid, so that "either way there must be a saṅghādisesa".33 Thanissaro grants that an improperly performed transaction is usually invalid, but holds that the Vibhaṅga, which gives such a site wrong conduct, seems to make a special exemption for a monk who followed the procedure as best he and his fellow monks knew how;34 and the Manual calls the saṅghādisesa "too strict a judgement" for the same reason.35 I follow Thanissaro and the Manual, since the penalty the Vibhaṅga states outright is better evidence than an inference about the validity of the approval; but why the Vibhaṅga is lenient here it does not say, and the reason offered, tentatively, is Thanissaro's; the Manual rests on the Vibhaṅga's penalty alone, and I follow both.

    Effort

    "By means of begging" is the Story's own list: a man, a servant, an ox, a cart, tools, creepers, bamboo, reed, grass and clay, begged by the monk himself.36 A hut a donor sponsors is not begged for; whether it then falls under Sg 7 depends on a question disputed there. The Vibhaṅga names no exemption for relatives, as NP 6 does for robe-cloth;37 Thanissaro reports that the Commentary lets a monk ask relatives or those who have invited him for tools, but only as a loan.38 The offence comes when the hut is finished. Each act of building toward a hut that will break the rule is wrong conduct, the last but one a serious offence, and the last the saṅghādisesa; and it is the same whether he finishes what he began or what others began, himself or through others.39 The penalties add up: an oversized hut on an unapproved site is two saṅghādisesas.40

    A monk who has others build for him must tell them the requirements. If he leaves without telling them, he has every offence their hut incurs; if he told them and then hears that they are going wrong, he must go himself or send word, or it is wrong conduct; and if the hut is unfinished when he returns, he must give it to someone else or have it demolished and rebuilt, or face the full offence. If he told them and, once reminded, they still ignore him, he has no offence, and the builders, if they are monks, have wrong conduct for each requirement they ignore. The Vibhaṅga's grid counts the size among these; Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm leave it out, since the builders are building for another.41​ 42

    The Vibhaṅga does not say what a monk may beg for in building at all; the Commentary does, and Thanissaro notes that not all Communities hold it binding: labour may be asked for, tools only borrowed, and materials only hinted at, beyond things of no value such as reeds, grass and clay.43 His conclusion reaches past this rule: even where a building is not covered by it, a monk engaged in construction should not be burdensome to the laity. The rule limits one kind of hut; the Story's lesson, a town where people ran from cows, is for every monk who builds.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Saṅghādisesa — for each defect of the finished hut: an oversized hut; a hut on an unapproved site; two for both.44
    • Thullaccaya — the last act but one in building such a hut.45
    • Dukkaṭa — each earlier act of building such a hut; a finished hut on a site where harm will be done, and another on a site that lacks space; failing to go or send word on hearing that the builders are ignoring the requirements; for monk-builders who ignore them, one for each.46

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if it is a shelter, a cave, or a grass hut; if it is built for someone else; if it is anything other than a dwelling; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.47 Brahmāli renders the first two "shelter" and "cave"; Thanissaro reports the Commentary as taking the first to be a cave and leaving the second undefined except that it may be built of wood, stone or earth, and Ajahn Brahm says no one is quite sure which of the two is the cave. A grass hut is any building with a grass roof.48

    Related Offences

    • Sg 7 — a large dwelling with a sponsoring owner, where the size limit falls away and the Saṅgha's approval of the site remains; the meaning of "owner" is argued there.
    • Pc 10, 11 — digging the ground and damaging plants, which clearing a site must not breach.49
    • Pc 19 — the limits on building a large dwelling for which the materials are given.

    Related Suttas

    • SN 16.1 — Mahākassapa, content "with any kind of lodging", who does not "engage in a wrong search, in what is improper" for the sake of a requisite; the Āḷavī monks' begging was that search.50
    • AN 4.27 — "The foot of a tree is a trifle among lodgings, easily gained and blameless"; the contentment that makes a small hut enough.51
