    Pc 58

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. A number of monks and wanderers were travelling the road from Sāketa to Sāvatthī when thieves came out and robbed them. The king's men set out from Sāvatthī, caught the thieves with the stolen goods, and sent a message to the monks: "Venerables, please come and pick out your own robes." But the monks did not recognise them.

    People complained and criticised them: "How can they not recognise their own robes?" The monks heard the complaints and told the Buddha. He had the Saṅgha gathered, gave a teaching on what is right and proper, and then addressed the monks: "Well then, monks, I will lay down a training rule for the following ten reasons: for the well-being of the Sangha, for the comfort of the Sangha, for the restraint of bad people, for the ease of good monks, for the restraint of the corruptions relating to the present life, for the restraint of the corruptions relating to future lives, to give rise to confidence in those without it, to increase the confidence of those who have it, for the longevity of the true Teaching, and for supporting the training." A monk who uses a new robe without first applying one of three kinds of stain commits an offence entailing confession.1

    Synopsis — Monks who had been robbed on the road could not tell their own robes from anyone else's when the king's men recovered them, and the rule that followed requires every robe to be marked before it is worn. It is one of six rules the Vibhaṅga introduces with the formula of ten reasons.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "When a monk gets a new robe, he should apply one of three kinds of stains: blue-green, mud-color, or dark brown. If a monk uses a new robe without applying any of the three kinds of stains, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu receives a new robe and then wears it without marking it with green, blue, brown, grey, or black, he incurs a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "When a bhikkhu receives a new robe, any one of three means of discoloring it is to be applied: green, brown, or black. If a bhikkhu should make use of a new robe without applying any of the three means of discoloring it, it is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Taking possession of, and wearing a new robe which has not been marked is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "By a monk with the gain of a new robe a certain stain [from] amongst the three stains is to be applied: dark-blue or muddy [-grey] or dark-brown. If a bhikkhu, not having applied a certain stain [from] amongst the three stains, should use a new robe, [this is a case] involving expiation."6

    The renderings agree on the rule and differ only in how many colours they name. The Pāli has three, and Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation, Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita keep three.7 Brahmāli's recitation line gives five, and the expansion serves the reader. The Vibhaṅga glosses the first colour as two and the third as "whatever is dark brownish", and Thanissaro draws the same consequence, that a mark in blue ink would do.8​ 9 The range is wider than three words suggest, and the colour matters less than that a mark is made.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A robe that bears its owner's mark is one he can know as his own and one made a little less fine, and the rule keeps both for the monk who wears it, together with the regard of lay people. When monks robbed on the road could not pick out their recovered robes, people asked how they could fail to recognise their own.10 The story is about recognition. The rule's own word for the mark, dubbaṇṇakaraṇa, names it a thing that makes the cloth unattractive, and Ajahn Brahm argues from the exemptions that this is its main purpose: a robe made partly from marked cloth, or patched, needs no new mark, as a new rug at NP 15 must have old cloth worked into it, and recognition follows from the mark.11​ 12 Either way the mark turns a length of cloth into a monk's requisite, his own and plain.

    Thanissaro gives the offence two factors: object, a new robe; and effort, using it without first marking it.13

    Object

    "New" does not mean new. The Vibhaṅga defines a new robe as one on which "a mark has not been made", so that an unmarked robe kept for years is still new, and Ajahn Brahm draws the consequence that wearing an old robe never marked is the full offence.14​ 15​ 16 The converse holds too: a robe another monk has marked and then given away need not be marked again.17 What brings the duty is acquiring a robe as a monk. Brahmāli thought it probable that a man who ordains in robes he already owned need not mark them, since he acquires nothing, while a robe from a novice, a layperson, the stores or a shop has most likely never been marked and must be.18

    The robe here is narrower than robe-cloth. At Pc 59 and Pc 60 the Vibhaṅga defines a robe as one of the six kinds of cloth "but not smaller than what can be assigned to another"; here the definition stops at the six kinds.19​ 20 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual takes the robe as at Pc 26, "any wearable robe", a definition he notes omits the assignable minimum.21 Thanissaro reports the Commentary reading the omission as deliberate: the rule means completed robes worn over the shoulders or around the waist, the three robes, the rains-bathing cloth and the skin-eruption cloth, and not sitting cloths, handkerchiefs or shoulder bags, none of which is grounds for any offence. Ajahn Brahm adds socks, a hat and a bowl bag. A shoulder cloth, being worn on the body, would seem to fall within it, as Thanissaro suggests, "as would any other item a bhikkhu might wear around his body".22​ 23

    Effort

    The Vibhaṅga says only that he "uses" the robe; in Thanissaro's account the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī makes it wearing the robe over the shoulders or around the waist.24​ 25 Since the mark is made after dyeing, Thanissaro notes, trying on a robe that is still being sewn and has not yet been dyed does not count. Brahmāli reached the same answer for a robe tried on in a monastery's storeroom by a monk choosing one: until it is his, there is nothing yet to mark.26​ 27

    The mark itself can be very small. The Vibhaṅga allows "even … what fits on the tip of a blade of grass", in any of the colours.28 The Commentary Thanissaro cites is more exacting: a round dot, no smaller than a bedbug's back and no larger than the iris of a peacock's eye, in one to four corners, and no line or angular mark. Thanissaro sets the restrictions aside as having "no basis in the Canon or the Great Standards", and the Concise BMC takes the size with the shape.29​ 30 The Thai custom of three small dots in one corner, made with a formula, is neither required nor against the texts, and Ajahn Brahm, who gives the same size as a requirement, thought one dot enough and the words unnecessary.31​ 32

    Perception

    Where the robe is unmarked, the pācittiya falls under all three perceptions; where it is marked but the monk thinks it is not, or is unsure, it is an offence of wrong conduct. Thanissaro refers the pattern to Pc 4.33​ 34

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    Six of the seven clauses concern a robe marked before. Either the mark has faded or the marked part has worn through, or unmarked cloth has been added to a marked robe, by sewing, patching or adding a border.35 Thanissaro reports an emergency from the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī that the Vibhaṅga does not name: a monk whose robes have been snatched away or destroyed may wear unmarked cloth, on the allowance for makeshift robes under NP 6.36

    A mark is there to be recognised, and a mark every monk makes in the same corner, in the same three dots, can no longer do that. Brahmāli's counsel was to mark a robe "in your personal way … so you can recognise it".37 It is a small act, made once, and it answers the question the people asked when the monks could not tell their robes apart.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — wearing a robe that has in fact been marked while perceiving that it has not, or being unsure.38

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he applies the stain and then uses the robe; if the mark has disappeared; if the area where the mark was applied is worn; if what had been marked is sewn together with what has not been marked; if it is a patch; if it is a lengthwise border; if it is a crosswise border; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.39

    Related Offences

    • Pc 59, 60 — using a robe one has assigned to another, and hiding a monk's bowl or robe: the rules that close the chapter with this one, and whose definition of a robe carries the size limit this one leaves out.
    • NP 15 — a new rug must have old cloth worked into it: Ajahn Brahm reads the two rules as one principle, that a new cloth requisite is made less attractive before use.40
    • NP 6 — asking for robe-cloth from an unrelated householder: the source of the allowance for a monk whose robes have been destroyed.41

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; the single substory is given nearly whole, and the ten reasons are given in full, as Brahmāli's English gives them. The Manual, the recitation, Brahmāli's endnotes, the class transcript (session 029, 19 June 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1 and BMC2, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were checked. Ajahn Brahm twice cites the Vinaya-mukha, which this book does not use; his positions are carried as his own. The class is cited for two views no written source gives, on when a robe is acquired and on a personal mark; its point on the purpose is carried by Ajahn Brahm. The Commentary is known here only through Thanissaro.
