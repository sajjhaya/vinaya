    Pc 73

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time the monks from the group of six were misbehaving. They said to each other, "Let's make the other monks think that we committed these offenses because we didn't know the rules." Then, during the recitation of the Monastic Code, they said, "Just now did we find out that this rule too is included in the Monastic Code and comes up for recitation every half-month."1

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised them: "How could the monks from the group of six say this during the recitation of the Monastic Code?" They told the Buddha, who questioned them and rebuked them: "Foolish men, how could you say this during the recitation of the Monastic Code? This will affect people's confidence."2

    Synopsis — The group of six, having misbehaved, agreed on a line to use during the fortnightly recitation: that they were only now hearing the rule. The line is true every time it is said, which is what makes it useful and what makes it a deception; the Buddha gave the Saṅgha a formal act to expose it, and made the persistence after that act the offence.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "During the half-monthly recitation of the Monastic Code, a monk might say, 'Just now did I find out that this rule too has come down in the Monastic Code, is included in the Monastic Code, and comes up for recitation every half-month.' If other monks know that that monk has previously sat through at least two or three recitations of the Monastic Code, then that monk is not let off because of ignorance, and he is to be dealt with according to the rule. Further, he should be charged with deception: 'It's a loss for you that you don't pay proper attention during the recitation of the Monastic Code.' And for the act of deception, he commits an offense entailing confession."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu deceptively claims he did not know a pātimokkha rule, but other bhikkhus know he has previously heard the pātimokkha at least twice, they should officially charge him with deception. If he still continues his deception, he incurs a pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu, when the Pāṭimokkha is being recited every half-month, say, 'Just now have I learned that this case, too, is handed down in the Pāṭimokkha, is included in the Pāṭimokkha, and comes up for recitation every half-month'; and if the bhikkhus should know of that bhikkhu, 'This bhikkhu has already sat through two or three recitations of the Pāṭimokkha, if not more,' the bhikkhu is not exempted for being ignorant. Whatever the offense he has committed, he is to be dealt with in accordance with the rule; and in addition, his deceit is to be exposed: 'It is no gain for you, friend, it is ill-done, that when the Pāṭimokkha is being recited, you do not pay attention, properly taking it to heart.' As for the deception (§), it is to be confessed."5

    Brahm

    "Continuing to pretend ignorance as an excuse for one's offences, having been formally censured by mohāropanakamma, is an offence of Pācittiya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu when the Disciplinary Code is being recited half-monthly should say so, 'Only now I know! This too, indeed, is a case which has been handed down in the Sutta, which has been included in the Sutta, which comes up for recitation half-monthly' [and] if other bhikkhus should know [about] that bhikkhu [thus], 'This bhikkhu has sat [in] two or three times previously when the Disciplinary Code was being recited. What to say about more [times than that]!' [then] there is no release for that bhikkhu through not-knowing, and whatever the offence is that he has committed there, that he is to be made to do according to the case and moreover his deluding is to be exposed, 'Because of that, friend, [there are] losses for you, because of that [it] has been ill-gained by you, that you, when the Disciplinary Code is being recited, not focussing carefully, [do not] pay attention.' Because of that deluding, this [is a case] involving expiation."7

    The renderings agree on the rule's two stages and part over two things, one of which changes what the rule requires. The first is a number. The Pāli says the offender has sat through the recitation "two or three times", and Brahmāli renders it two ways: "at least two or three recitations" in the full translation and "at least twice" in the recitation.8​ 9 How many hearings a monk may plead ignorance through is a real difference, and it is settled under Object. The second is moha, the rule's word for what the offender does: "deception" in Brahmāli and Thanissaro, "deluding" in Ñāṇatusita, and "confusion" or "negligence" in older translations. The Vibhaṅga decides it, because its word-analysis calls the offender mohetukāma, "wanting to deceive", and a confused monk does not want anything; the point is taken up under Intention.10

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    Hearing the Pātimokkha recited every fortnight was how a monk of the Buddha's day came to know the rules he had undertaken, and this rule keeps him from turning that hearing into a hiding place. The group of six had misbehaved, and planned in so many words to have the others think they had offended in ignorance; the line they chose was true, because they had indeed just heard the rule recited.11 What the rule guards is a monk's straightness with his fellow monks about his own conduct, and the recitation itself, which loses its purpose once a monk can sit through it and claim to have heard nothing. The Buddha's rebuke adds the people's confidence: a Saṅgha whose members plead ignorance of their own rules is not one the laity can trust to keep them.

    The rule has two stages, and the full offence is at the second. The sentence said with intent to deceive is an offence of wrong conduct. The other monks, knowing he has heard the recitation before, do not let him off: he is dealt with for whatever he actually did, and the Saṅgha charges him with deception by a formal act. If he keeps up the deception after that, it is a pācittiya.12 Pc 12, on answering evasively when formally questioned, is built the same way, and both rules are about a monk who will not answer straight. Thanissaro gives three factors: object, intention and effort.13

    Object

    A rule in the Pātimokkha, which the monk has heard recited in full before. How many times? The Vibhaṅga says two or three, and its non-offence clause exempts a monk who has heard it in full fewer than two or three times.14 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual, Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm all read that as three, the Manual "so as to give the offender the maximum benefit of the doubt"; the recitation's "at least twice" stands against them.15​ 16​ 17 I follow the three, on the plain ground that the phrase is a range and the rule is penal, so a Saṅgha should have to satisfy itself of the larger number. Thanissaro puts the practical weight of the number well: three recitations cover a month to a month and a half, "not too short a time for a new bhikkhu to read and remember the rules in translation."18 He grants that the rule was framed when Pāli was the monks' own language, so that hearing the recitation was how a monk learned the rules, and that neither the Vibhaṅga nor the commentaries make an exception for anyone else. For a monk today who hears the recitation in a language he does not understand, the count runs against him all the same; he is expected to have read the rules, and only the absence of any wish to deceive (below) clears him.

    Intention

    The monk must want to deceive, and the Vibhaṅga makes sure of it by calling him mohetukāma where the rule has only "that monk".19 The addition does all the work, because what he says is true. He has just heard the rule — again. Thanissaro states the mechanism exactly: he has indeed just heard it, "even if for the umpteenth time", and hopes the others will infer that he heard it for the first time.20 An out-and-out lie would come under Pc 1.21 The Canon itself puts the same sentence where no penalty follows it. In the Khandhaka's collection of cases, two monks who have committed a saṅghādisesa say the very same words during the recitation, and no penalty attaches to the saying of it; the only penalty the passage adds is for the one who then conceals his offence.22 The passage does not raise their intention, and the inference is mine: the words are the same in both places, and what makes one of them an offence here is the wish to be believed ignorant.

    Effort

    The first rung is the sentence itself, said with that wish: an offence of wrong conduct. The second waits on the Saṅgha. A competent monk puts a motion naming the monk and his inattention at the recitation, followed by a single announcement, and the Saṅgha's silence carries it.23​ 24 Ajahn Brahm gives the act its name, mohāropanakamma.25 If the monk deceives again after that, the pācittiya falls. The Manual draws the line at the act itself: the pācittiya falls unless he gives up the deception before the act is completed.26 His opinion of the act does not help him. Where the act was legitimate, the pācittiya falls whether he thinks it legitimate, is unsure, or thinks it improperly done; where the act was in fact illegitimate, he has an offence of wrong conduct in all three cases.27​ 28 So a monk who keeps up the deception because he believes the Saṅgha got the quorum or the wording wrong is not saved by being right, and is not excused by being wrong.

    The Vibhaṅga does not say what happens to a monk who persists even then. Brahmāli gives a possible continuation, depending on the circumstances, as his own inference: a monk who keeps up the deception is breaking a rule and will not make amends, and is "potentially subject" to suspension from the Saṅgha.29

    Disputed — whether the rule is confined to the formal half-monthly recitation.

    Thanissaro says it is not: the non-offence clauses make no exception for the same ploy used at other times, and the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī, in his report of it, leaves the recitation out of the offence's factors, so the rule "covers the use of a half-truth to feign ignorance of the rules at any time."30 The Manual says the clause "does not seem to affect the incurring of an offence".31 Ñāṇatusita argues the other way from the text. At Pc 72 the Vibhaṅga glosses "when it is being recited" as reciting, having it recited, or studying; here it glosses the same phrase with a single word, reciting, and this rule adds "half-monthly", which Pc 72 does not; so this rule, he concludes, applies only to the official half-monthly recitation.32 I take the wider reading, because a monk who works the same half-truth in private is doing the same thing for the same reason, and the Saṅgha's act that completes the offence can be brought wherever the deception is found. But the two word-analyses do differ, as Ñāṇatusita shows; the likeliest account is that the narrower gloss records the occasion — a rule whose machinery is a formal act of the assembled Saṅgha — rather than a condition of the offence. That account is mine, offered to fill a gap the texts leave, and a monk who keeps clear of the half-truth everywhere is safe on either reading.

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The third non-offence clause, that he does not want to deceive, is the rule. Ajahn Brahm names the modern case it covers — a monk who has heard the recitation three or more times and attended to it, but does not understand Pāli — and adds the half that is not an exemption: whatever he now realises were offences must still be confessed, since "ignorance does not absolve one from the offence."33 The Manual calls this "an important point of vinaya": once a man is a bhikkhu, "one falls into an offence immediately upon breaking a rule, whether one knows about that rule or not."34 The rule's own words are that he "is to be dealt with according to the rule", and Ñāṇatusita, from how the idiom is used in the Khandhakas, takes them exactly: dealt with under the rule he actually broke.35 The pretence gains him nothing.

    What the Saṅgha says to the monk, as it charges him, is worth keeping in mind at every recitation: "It's a loss for you that you don't pay proper attention during the recitation of the Monastic Code."36 The words are addressed to the deceiver, but the loss they name is open to any monk who sits through the recitation without listening.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • An offence of wrong conduct for saying the sentence with intent to deceive, before any formal act has been carried out.37
    • An offence of wrong conduct for deceiving after an act that was illegitimate, however he perceives it.38
    • The original offence remains, to be dealt with under its own rule.39
    • Suspension, potentially, for a monk who persists after the act — not stated in the Vibhaṅga, and given by Brahmāli as an inference that depends on the circumstances.40

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he has not heard it in full; if he has heard it fewer than two or three times in full; if he does not want to deceive; if he is insane; if he is the first offender.41

    Related Offences

    • Pc 12 — answering evasively when formally questioned. The same two stages; both rules make the full offence wait on a formal act.
    • Pc 1 — deliberate lying. The rule an out-and-out lie would fall under.42
    • Pc 72 — disparaging the training rules. The rule before, sharing the recitation clause, whose wider gloss is the evidence in the Disputed passage above.
    • Pc 71 — refusing to train when corrected. The chapter's first rule, and with this one a pair of literally true sentences used to put off an obligation.
    • Kd 13 — the same sentence in the mouths of two monks, with no penalty for saying it.43

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; it is very short, and the group of six's plan is quoted in their own words because the rule's intention factor is that sentence. The Manual, the recitation, the class (session 030, 16 July 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were all checked; Ñāṇatusita is used for the translation-level questions of moha, the recitation clause and yathādhammo, where his argument is grammatical. The Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī's factors are given as Thanissaro reports them. Ajahn Brahm cites the Vinayamukha for the genuine-ignorance exemption; the exemption is the Vibhaṅga's own third clause and does not rest on it. The class transcript marks no speakers; the one passage kept is Brahmāli's reading of the Vibhaṅga aloud and his inference from it.

    23. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 73, final ruling and word-analysis. "Might say: after misbehaving, he thinks, 'Let them think that I committed these offenses because I didn't know the rules.' If, during the recitation of the Monastic Code, he then says, 'Just now did I find out that this rule too has come down in the Monastic Code …' then he commits an offense of wrong conduct." The transaction: "A competent and capable monk should inform the Sangha: 'Please, venerables, I ask the Sangha to listen. Monk so-and-so did not pay proper attention during the recitation of the Monastic Code. If the Sangha is ready, it should charge monk so-and-so with deception. This is the motion …'" The two rungs: "If he deceives, but he has not been charged with deception, he commits an offense of wrong conduct. If he deceives, and he has been charged with deception, he commits an offense entailing confession."↩︎

    24. Brahmāli, Pātimokkha Manual, Pc 73. Factors: "1) Having breached a rule, he desires to deceive others about it (IV 145,4-7 + 29)"; "2) They should charge him through a saṅghakamma of one motion and one announcement (IV 145,9-19)"; "3) Unless he gives up his deception before the end of the saṅghakamma (IV 145,20-21)."
