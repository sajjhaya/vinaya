    NP 21

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time the monks from the group of six were storing up many almsbowls. When people walking about the dwellings saw this, they complained and criticised them, "How can the Sakyan monastics store up many bowls? Are they starting up as bowl merchants or setting up a bowl shop?"

    The monks heard the complaints of those people, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised those monks, "How can the monks from the group of six keep extra bowls?" After rebuking those monks in many ways, they told the Buddha. Soon afterwards he had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned the monks: "Is it true, monks, that you do this?" "It's true, sir." The Buddha rebuked them: "Foolish men, how can you do this? This will affect people's confidence …" And he laid down a rule that a monk who keeps an extra almsbowl commits an offence entailing relinquishment and confession.

    Soon afterwards Venerable Ānanda was offered an extra bowl. He wanted to give it to Venerable Sāriputta, who was staying at Sāketa. Knowing that the Buddha had laid down a rule against keeping an extra bowl, Ānanda thought, "What should I do now?" He told the Buddha, who said, "How long is it, Ānanda, before Sāriputta returns?"

    "Nine or ten days, venerable sir."

    Soon afterwards the Buddha gave a teaching and addressed the monks: "Monks, you should keep an extra almsbowl for ten days at the most." And he laid down the final rule.1

    Synopsis — the group of six store up many almsbowls and people ask whether they are setting up a bowl shop; the Buddha forbids keeping an extra bowl; Ānanda is then offered a bowl he wishes to hold for Sāriputta, who will return in nine or ten days, and the Buddha allows an extra bowl to be kept for ten days at the most.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "A monk should keep an extra almsbowl for ten days at the most. If he keeps it longer than that, he commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu owns a bowl for more than ten days that is neither determined nor stored through vikappana, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "An extra alms bowl may be kept ten days at most. Beyond that, it is to be forfeited and confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Keeping an extra alms bowl beyond ten days is a nissaggiya pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "An extra bowl can be kept for ten days at the most. For one who lets it pass beyond [the ten days], [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture."6

    The renderings agree. The Pāli is NP 1's with the object changed from robe-cloth to bowl, and Ñāṇatusita glosses every word of it by pointing back there.7 The recitation, as at NP 1, builds the Vibhaṅga's definition of "extra" into the rule, and it renders the keeping as owning, which follows Brahmāli's own gloss of the word at NP 1 in his Pātimokkha Manual, "keeps ownership of", so that a bowl borrowed is not his to keep.8​ 9​ 10 Nothing else in the rule depends on the wording.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule protects a monk's contentment with one bowl, and with it the regard of the people who see how monks live. A row of bowls looked to them like stock: "Are they starting up as bowl merchants or setting up a bowl shop?"; and the Buddha's rebuke names what was at stake, "This will affect people's confidence."11 The Khandhaka records the same complaint when the group of six used bowls of gold and silver, "They're just like householders who indulge in worldly pleasures!"12 The second story shows what the allowance is for. It follows directly on Ānanda's answer, "Nine or ten days", so the case that produced the ten days is a gift held for a friend, not a spare kept against need.13

    The rule is NP 1 with a bowl in place of cloth: the same meaning of "extra", the same count to the dawn, the same relinquishment and return. Thanissaro gives the offence two factors, object and effort, and the Manual defines the extra bowl and refers the keeping and the count to NP 1.14​ 15

    Object

    A bowl is extra when it is neither determined for use nor assigned to another.16 A monk may have only one bowl determined at a time, and the Manual's definition follows from that: an extra bowl is "any allowable alms bowl apart from his one determined bowl."17​ 18 Two consequences are easy to miss. A monk's only bowl, if he has never determined it, is itself an extra bowl, and its ten days are running. And determination is not ownership: a monk who rescinds the determination of his bowl still owns it, and it is extra again from that moment.

    Only a bowl fit to be determined comes under the rule. The Vibhaṅga itself says what a bowl is: "there are two kinds of bowls: the iron bowl and the ceramic bowl", in three sizes, large, medium and small, measured by the cooked rice, fresh food and curry each holds, and "Anything larger than this is not a bowl, nor anything smaller."19 Thanissaro gathers the Commentary's requirements under five heads: proper material, proper size, fully paid for, properly fired, and not damaged beyond repair.20

    Material. The Khandhaka forbids the wooden bowl, then bowls made with gold, silver, gems, beryl, crystal, bronze, glass, tin, lead or copper, and allows "two kinds of almsbowls: iron bowls and ceramic bowls"; it forbids a skull as a bowl as well.21 Applying the Great Standards, the Council of Elders in Thailand has ruled stainless steel allowable, because it is steel, and aluminium not, because it shares the dangers of tin.22

    Size. The ancient measures cannot now be fixed with any precision. The Vinayamukha's experiment, reported by Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm, made the small bowl a little larger than a human skull, and Ajahn Brahm draws out the sense of it: full, the small bowl holds a monk's one meal for the day, which is presumably why nothing smaller would serve.23​ 24 A bowl above the large size or below the small is not a bowl for this rule, and every size between falls under it.25 Brahmāli's counsel in class was practical: the limits are too ill-defined to draw a modern line, and a monk need only avoid the outrageous.26

    Fully paid for, and fired. A bowl not yet fully paid for is not yet the monk's to determine, and does not come under the rule until it is; a bowl the maker gives as a gift counts as paid. Thanissaro reports that the Commentary also requires a ceramic bowl to be fired twice, to harden it, and an iron bowl five times, against rust. Stainless steel, which does not rust, needs no firing; it is commonly darkened only so that it will not stand out.27

    Not damaged beyond repair. A hole a millet grain can pass through ends the determination of an iron bowl but not its being a bowl: the monk plugs it and determines it again, and its ten days run from the mend.28​ 29 When damage past mending makes a bowl no longer a bowl, and so entitles a monk to ask for another, belongs to NP 22.

    What a thing is, for this rule, can depend on the use it is kept for. Brahmāli applied this in class to a modern case, a discarded bowl a monk took to make something else out of. Since it was never going to be used as a bowl, he said, it was no longer one, and the rule did not reach it; but the other use has to be clear.30

    A bowl is determined as cloth is, bodily, by touching it while resolving on it, or verbally, customarily with imaṃ pattaṃ adhiṭṭhāmi, "I determine this bowl", and it may be determined even when it is not at hand.31 The determination lapses for the causes given at NP 1, the bowl's own being the millet-grain hole; an assignment survives the hole and lapses for the rest.32 A second bowl a monk does not want to give away is therefore assigned, exactly as spare cloth is; how an assignment is made and undone, and the offence of using the bowl before it is undone, belong to Pc 59.33

    Effort

    The count is NP 1's: the bowl "becomes subject to relinquishment at dawn on the eleventh day", which the Commentary, in Brahmāli's note, makes the tenth dawn after the bowl is received, so that the allowance always falls a little short of ten full days.34 The count starts when the bowl becomes an extra bowl fit to be determined. Ajahn Brahm gives the cases: an iron bowl received unfired, from its fifth firing; a holed bowl, from the mend that makes it usable; a bowl sent as a gift by post, from its arrival.35

    Disputed — when the count begins for a bowl that is ready but not yet in the monk's hands.

    Responsibility can begin before the bowl is in hand, on the Commentary's reading in Thanissaro's report: where the maker promised to send word when it was done, the ten days run from the messenger's word; only where he promised to send the bowl itself does the count wait until it is brought.36 Thanissaro sets against this the Khandhaka's principle for cloth, that the time does not begin until the robe reaches one's hand, and writes, "It would seem that the same principle should apply here."37 Ajahn Brahm's posted bowl does not decide it, since a bowl sent is the Commentary's own exception.38 Which reading is right the texts do not settle, and Thanissaro's rests on an analogy from cloth. A monk can act safely on either, for a bowl may be determined in words without being in the same room as it.39

    Perception does not mitigate. A monk who miscounts the days, or believes the bowl determined, assigned, given away or broken when it is not, commits the full offence.40

    The relinquishment is NP 1's, in the bowl's own words, "Venerables, this almsbowl, which I have kept over ten days, is to be relinquished. I relinquish it to the Sangha", and the bowl is given back.41 When the group of six kept a relinquished bowl, the Buddha ruled that it must be returned, and returned, it is an extra bowl again, with ten fresh days.42​ 43 The ten days, then, are time enough to pass a bowl on, as Ānanda meant to, or to assign it; they are not time to keep a second bowl at hand.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — using a bowl that is to be relinquished before relinquishing it; NP 21 is one of the rules where the Vibhaṅga spells this out (see NP 1).44​ 45
    • Dukkaṭa — keeping a bowl less than ten days while perceiving it as more, or unsure of it; as at NP 1, apparently for using it under that perception.46​ 47
    • Dukkaṭa — for the monk who receives a relinquished bowl and does not give it back.48

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if, within ten days, the bowl is determined, assigned to another, given away, lost, destroyed, broken, stolen, or taken on trust; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.49 "Destroyed" and "broken" mean damaged beyond repair.50 Taking on trust is treated at NP 1.

    Related Offences

    • NP 1 — the anchor of this rule's machinery: "extra", determination and assignment, the count to the dawn, relinquishment and return.
    • NP 22 — when a damaged bowl stops being a bowl, and when a monk may ask for a new one.
    • NP 23 — the same design on a seven-day count, for tonics.
    • Pc 59 — making and undoing an assignment, and using an assigned bowl before it is undone; Ajahn Brahm leaves the full methods to that rule.

    Related Suttas

    • AN 8.30 — "This Dhamma is for one with few desires, not for one with strong desires", a bhikkhu being "content with any kind of robes, almsfood, lodgings, and medicines and provisions for the sick" (see NP 1); one bowl is enough for one who is content, and the ten days the rule allows grew from a gift held for a friend, not from a wish for a spare.51
    • SN 16.1 — Mahākassapa, whom the Buddha praises as "content with any kind of almsfood"; contentment with whatever comes to one bowl leaves no use for a second.52

    A note on sourcing

    The Story, definitions, permutations, relinquishment, non-offence clauses and the sequel on returning the bowl are from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation, and the materials and the skull from his Khandhaka. The factors, the five requirements, the Council of Elders' rulings, the Vinayamukha's experiment, the millet-grain hole and the countdown argument are Thanissaro's BMC1, and the Commentary is cited from his report of it, the tenth dawn from Brahmāli's endnote; the definition of an extra bowl is the Manual; determination, assignment, the lapse of each and the starting of the count are Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes, with his report of the Vinayamukha. The class on this rule (session 016, 20 March 2012) is cited for the size counsel and the repurposed bowl. The Concise BMC restates BMC1, stating Thanissaro's countdown view without his hedge, and is not cited; Ñāṇatusita is used for his rendering. Ajahn Brahm's Story adds a habit of gifts between Ānanda and Sāriputta that the Vibhaṅga does not carry, and it is not used. AN 8.30 and SN 16.1 are quoted from Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation.
