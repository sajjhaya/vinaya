    Sg 3

    Story (Vatthu)

    At one time the Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove. Venerable Udāyī was staying in a beautiful dwelling in the wilderness, and on one occasion a number of women came to the monastery to see the dwellings. They approached Udāyī and said, "Venerable, we would like to see your dwelling."

    After showing them his dwelling, he praised and disparaged their private parts; he requested and implored, asked and enquired, described, instructed, and abused their private parts. The shameless and indecent women flirted with Udāyī; they called out to him, laughed with him, and teased him. But after leaving, those of them who had a sense of shame complained to the monks, "Venerables, this isn't suitable or appropriate. We wouldn't want to hear such speech from our own husbands, let alone from Venerable Udāyī."

    The monks of few desires complained and criticized him, "How could Venerable Udāyī speak indecently to women?" They rebuked him and told the Buddha, who had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned Udāyī. "It's true, sir." The Buddha rebuked him and laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — Venerable Udāyī, showing a group of women his dwelling, speaks to them about their private parts; the shameless flirt with him, those with a sense of shame complain, and the Buddha lays down the rule against lustful indecent speech to a woman.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk, overcome by lust and with a distorted mind, speaks indecent words to a woman, as a young man might to a young woman and referring to sexual intercourse, he commits an offense entailing suspension."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu, motivated by lust, speaks sexually explicit words to a woman, he incurs a saṅghādisesa."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu, overcome by lust, with altered mind, address lewd words to a woman in the manner of young men to a young woman alluding to sexual intercourse, it entails initial and subsequent meetings of the Community."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Flirting with a woman, mentioning her or his genitals, anus or sexual intercourse, is a saṅghādisesa."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu, under the influence of an altered mind, should speak suggestively with depraved words to a woman, like a young man to a young woman, [with words] concerned with sexual intercourse: [this is a case] involving the community in the beginning and in the rest."6

    The lust is rendered as at Sg 2, and the five renderings part on two words. Duṭṭhulla is "indecent", "lewd" or "sexually explicit" in most of them, but Ñāṇatusita holds that it means "depraved", since the rule itself speaks of depraved words "concerned with sexual intercourse", implying that some depraved words are not; the sexual sense comes from that last phrase. Obhāseyya is "speaks" or "address" in most, but in Ñāṇatusita "speak suggestively", to hint, which he finds fits the Vibhaṅga's examples, "in which indirect, but easily understandable, suggestions are given."7 The recitation's "sexually explicit" leans the other way, and drops "as a young man might to a young woman". Whether words that only hint can bring the full offence is the rule's live question, and it is taken up under Effort.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule guards a monk's speech when lust moves it toward a woman. The third of the sexual saṅghādisesas moves from touch to speech, and Udāyī is again the offender. The Story records two responses to what he said: the shameless women laughed and teased him back, and the women with a sense of shame left and complained that they would not want to hear such speech even from their own husbands. Both responses mattered, for whether the woman understands decides the offence.

    The Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī, as Thanissaro gives it, lists five factors: object, a woman experienced enough to know what is lewd and what is not; perception, that he perceives her as a woman; intention, that he is impelled by lust; effort, that he refers to her genitals, her anus or her having intercourse; and result, that she immediately understands.8 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual and Ajahn Brahm give the same five.9

    Object and Perception

    The woman is one who "understands and is capable of discerning good and bad speech, what is decent and what is indecent."10 The Commentary notes that a woman who cannot may be too young to know or, as an adult, too innocent or unable to know, and Thanissaro adds a woman who does not understand the language spoken.11 A paṇḍaka is a serious offence and a man or an animal wrong conduct, as at Sg 2, though the Vibhaṅga gives these permutations only as "to be expanded" from the previous rule, with appropriate substitutions; Brahmāli notes that neither the Pāli nor the commentaries say how, and expands them by Sg 2. The editions differ over the ellipses, and Thanissaro follows the Thai, which includes the man and the animal and so closes what would otherwise be a gap.12 A woman whom the monk takes for a paṇḍaka, a man or an animal is a serious offence if he refers to her genitals, anus or intercourse; a paṇḍaka, man or animal whom he takes for something else is wrong conduct.13

    Intention

    The lust is defined as at Sg 2, and the least of it is enough. Thanissaro takes the rule to cover a monk who simply enjoys such talk in a woman's presence, with no wish to have sex with her; Ajahn Brahm, following the Samantapāsādikā, counts even a momentary impulse that prompts one lewd word.14 What the rule does not cover is anger: insults about a woman's body spoken out of anger fall under Pc 2.15

    Where there is no lust, the words alone are not the offence. Ajahn Brahm gives two cases. A monk explaining Pj 1 names kinds of intercourse and the parts of the body plainly, with a mind free of lust, and has no offence however a woman in the audience reacts. A monk giving a prepared talk on the loathsomeness of the body, which names the genitals, notices an attractive woman and feels lust arise, but keeps to his script, and has no offence, because it was not lust that put the words in his mouth; if, because of the lust, he adds further mention of the genitals, he has the full offence.16 The test is whether lust is shaping what is said; its mere presence in the room does not meet it.

    Effort

    Indecent words are speech connected with the anus, the vagina or sexual intercourse, and the Vibhaṅga lists nine ways of using them: praising, disparaging, requesting, imploring, asking, enquiring, describing, instructing and abusing. Its examples include "When will you please your mother?" and "How do you give to your husband?", and the abuse is illustrated by insults about a woman's body, from lacking genitals or not menstruating to being manlike.17 Thanissaro notes that the full offence needs words about her genitals, anus or intercourse; none of the texts mention a monk who speaks to one person about another's, and he concludes that it is apparently not an offence.18 Brahmāli guards the conclusion in class: it does not mean that such talk is acceptable, only that the saṅghādisesa is limited to the listener's own body, and the Dhamma and the Vinaya are to be told apart here.19 A gesture referring to these things serves as well as words, by the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī, and Ajahn Brahm treats a lewd letter like speech.20

    Disputed — whether words that do not name the genitals, anus or intercourse can bring the full offence.

    The Commentary holds that they cannot: without a direct mention there is no saṅghādisesa.21 Ajahn Brahm's grading is close to it. "Will you sleep with me?" uses a well-known expression for intercourse and is the full offence, but a roundabout question such as "Will you stay the night?", understood at once, is a serious offence, and one not understood is wrong conduct.22 Thanissaro answers that the Vibhaṅga's own examples defeat the Commentary, since the questions about the husband and the mother refer to intercourse without naming it and are given as ways of committing the full offence. A euphemism or slang expression therefore fulfils the factor, and it need not be well known: the monk's intending it fulfils the effort, and her understanding it the result.23 The Manual agrees, listing the words as fulfilling the factor "whether the words are direct or indirect"; in class Brahmāli adds that the indirect words are euphemisms "generally understood to mean these kind of things", which puts him between Ajahn Brahm and Thanissaro on whether a euphemism must be well known.24 The Vibhaṅga's examples are hard to set aside, and Ajahn Brahm's own roundabout question, "How do you do 'it' with your husband?", is close to the Vibhaṅga's full-offence "How do you give to your husband?". I would teach Thanissaro's reading against the Commentary's demand for a direct mention; on whether the euphemism must be well known I do not choose between his reading and Brahmāli's lecture. The examples settle the matter only as illustrations of the summary, and this resolution is my own reading, not a ruling of the texts.

    Result

    The Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī requires that the woman understand immediately, and the case studies show what follows when she does not.25 A lustful monk asked a woman in a newly dyed woollen cloak, "Sister, is that red thing yours?", and she told him about her cloak; the same pun on coarse, thick, rough and long hair, a question to a woman back from her field whether she had sown, and an offer to a woman at work to lie down while he worked all went past their listeners, and each was wrong conduct. A monk who said to a female wanderer, "Sister, I hope the path has an end?", was told to keep going, and was given a serious offence. And a monk who told a woman that she gave her husband what she did not give the monks, and, asked what that was, named it, had the full offence, as did a monk who said the same of "the highest gift".26 Thanissaro finds the pattern that the Sub-commentary missed: the path case is the only one that uses a word for the genitals, magga, which also means "road", the meaning the woman understood. A direct reference that she does not understand is therefore a serious offence, and a euphemism or an implied reference that she does not understand is wrong conduct.27 Understanding that comes only afterwards does not raise the lesser offence to the full one, and Ajahn Brahm adds that words she does not hear at all are wrong conduct.28

    How is a monk to know whether she understood? Thanissaro answers from the case studies: by her immediate response.29 The origin story shows both kinds. The women who laughed and teased had understood, and so had the women who left and complained.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Thullaccaya — lustful reference to any part of a woman's body below the collar bone and above the knees, apart from the anus and the vagina; reference to her genitals, anus or intercourse when he takes her for something else; the same to a paṇḍaka; and a direct reference she does not understand.30
    • Dukkaṭa — lustful reference to any part of her body above the collar bone or below the knees, or to anything connected to her body, such as her clothes or jewellery; a paṇḍaka's other parts; any reference to a man or an animal; a paṇḍaka, man or animal taken for something else; and a euphemism she does not understand.31
    • Saṅghādisesa — one for each woman who understands: Thanissaro's example is a monk who teases twenty old ladies that their time for sex is past, and incurs twenty.32

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he is aiming at something beneficial; if he is aiming at giving a teaching; if he is aiming at giving an instruction; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.33 The first three are one principle: without lustful intent a monk may recite and explain this rule in front of women, or teach the loathsomeness of the body, and the Commentary adds a monk who tells a woman born with deformed genitals to be heedful in her practice so as not to be born that way again.34 The exemption is for a purpose, not a topic. The Commentary, followed by the New Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī Sub-commentary, gives a monk teaching the Vibhaṅga of this rule to a bhikkhunī who departs from his normal tone of voice and keeps sniggering as he recites the examples of lewd speech, and finds him guilty of an offence, the full one on Thanissaro's account.35 A monk who makes an innocent remark that his listener takes as lewd has no offence.36

    Related Offences

    • Sg 2 — lustful physical contact, defined by the same lust and graded over the same beings.
    • Sg 4 — the narrower case of recommending intercourse with oneself as a service.
    • Pc 2 — insulting speech, the home of insults about a woman's body spoken in anger.37

    Related Suttas

    • SN 35.127 — Piṇḍola Bhāradvāja's advice to King Udena, that young monks keep the celibate life by regarding women as mothers, sisters and daughters, the regard in which no lewd word to a woman is spoken.38
    • AN 5.198 — the five factors of well-spoken speech: "spoken at the proper time; what is said is true; it is spoken gently; what is said is beneficial; it is spoken with a mind of loving-kindness"; the speech the women with a sense of shame had a right to hear from a monk, and did not.39
