    Pc 57

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Rājagaha in the Bamboo Grove. The monks were bathing in the hot springs. Just then King Seniya Bimbisāra of Magadha went to the hot springs, intending to wash his hair. He waited respectfully for the monks to finish, but they kept on bathing until dark. Only then was King Bimbisāra able to wash his hair. And because the town gates had been shut, he had to spend the night outside the city.

    In the morning, with his make-up still on, he went to the Buddha, bowed, and sat down. The Buddha said to him, "Great king, why have you come so early in the morning, with your make-up still on?" The king told him what had happened. The Buddha then instructed, inspired, and gladdened him with a teaching, after which the king got up from his seat, bowed down, circumambulated the Buddha with his right side toward him, and left.

    Soon afterwards the Buddha had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned the monks: "Is it true, monks, that monks bathed without moderation, even after seeing the king?" It was true. He rebuked them and laid down the first ruling: a monk who bathes at intervals of less than a half-month commits an offence entailing confession.

    The rule was then amended five times. Monks who did not bathe in the heat or with a fever went to sleep covered in sweat and their robes and beds got dirty; the Buddha allowed bathing at intervals of less than a half-month in the heat and in the fever season. Sick monks told those looking after them that they had been comfortable before and were not comfortable now; he allowed it when sick. Monks doing building work went to sleep covered in sweat; he allowed it when working. Monks who had travelled went to sleep covered in sweat; he allowed it when travelling. And a number of monks making robes out in the open, hit by dusty winds and fine rain, went to sleep still wet; he allowed it when there is wind and rain.1

    Synopsis — The longest chain of amendments in the chapter, and the last five all answer the same fear: the monks were afraid of wrongdoing, so they did not bathe, and in four of them their robes and beds got dirty. The rule was laid down because monks had bathed without moderation in front of a king, and it was rebuilt five times because they then would not bathe at all.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk bathes at intervals of less than a half-month, except on an appropriate occasion, he commits an offense entailing confession. These are the appropriate occasions: it is the two-and-a-half-month period of summer and the fever season, comprising the last one-and-a-half months of summer and the first month of the rainy season; he is sick; he is working; he is traveling; there is wind and rain."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu in the central Ganges plain bathes more than once a fortnight, except on given occasions, he incurs a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu bathe at intervals of less than half a month, except at the proper occasions, it is to be confessed. Here the proper occasions are these: the last month and a half of the hot season, the first month of the rains, these two and a half months being a time of heat, a time of fever; (also) a time of illness; a time of work; a time of going on a journey; a time of wind or rain. These are the proper occasions here."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Bathing more frequently than once a fortnight in the 'middle country' except on certain occasions, is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should bathe within less than half a month, except at the [right] occasion, [this is a case] involving expiation. Here the occasion is this [thinking]: 'one and a half month is what remains of the hot season,' [and 'this is] the first month of the rainy season'—these two and a half months [are] the occasion of dry heat, [and] the occasion of humid heat—[also:] the occasion of being sick; the occasion of work; the occasion of going on a journey; the occasion of [dusty] wind and rain; this is the occasion here."6

    The renderings agree on the rule and differ in two places. The first is territory. The rule names none: "outside the central Ganges plain" stands among the non-offence clauses.7​ 8 Brahmāli's recitation line and Ajahn Brahm put the region into the rule itself.9​ 10 Nothing changes in what the rule requires, since an exemption covering the whole world outside one region is in effect a condition on the rule, and Circumstance gives the region. The second is the first occasion, which Brahmāli renders "summer and the fever season", Thanissaro "a time of heat, a time of fever", and Ñāṇatusita "dry heat" and "humid heat". The word-analysis defines both as parts of the calendar, and the two words share one origin story while sickness has its own, which is Ñāṇatusita's ground for reading the second term as weather rather than fever.11​ 12 Whatever the English, the occasion is a season of the year and not a state of the body.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    Knowing the measure of the body's comforts is part of a monk's restraint, and this rule holds him to the moderation the Buddha found lacking in the monks at the hot springs. King Bimbisāra waited while the monks bathed until dark, so that he was shut out of his own city for the night, and the question put to the monks was whether they had bathed "without moderation, even after seeing the king".13​ 14 The same king used the word when the group of six stripped his mango park to the last unripe fruit and he answered without complaint, "Still, the Buddha has praised moderation."15 Thanissaro reads the first ruling, made before any occasion was allowed, as a temporary measure for the monks who had inconvenienced the king, and the mango rule as its like.16 What has outlasted both rulings is the moderation itself, and the confidence of the people who watched how monks took their ease.

    The prohibition was loosened five times, as monks who would not bathe went to sleep in their sweat, and later lifted altogether outside the central Ganges plain. Thanissaro gives what remains a single factor, effort: living in the middle Ganges Valley, one bathes at intervals of less than half a month except at the proper occasions.17 The place and the occasions are set out under Circumstance, and the bath itself under Effort.

    Circumstance

    The rule was lifted for the world outside the central Ganges plain at the request of Venerable Mahākaccāna, who sent his pupil Soṇa Kuṭikaṇṇa to the Buddha from Avantī with five requests, one of them for unrestricted bathing in Avantī, where people valued bathing and cleanliness. The Buddha granted more than was asked, "outside the central Ganges plain" where Soṇa had asked for Avantī, and the plain had already been bounded, in the ruling on the first request, by landmarks in five directions: two towns in the east, a river in the south-east, a town in the south, a brahmin village in the west and a mountain in the north.18 And so, as Thanissaro puts it, there is nowhere in the world outside the middle Ganges Valley where this rule applies; Ajahn Brahm draws the same consequence, that it is "only of practical importance when residing in India".19​ 20

    Within the plain the occasions are wide, and once they were added, in Thanissaro's words, "they relaxed it considerably".21 The hot season and the season of humid heat together make two and a half months of the year. A monk is sick when he is "not comfortable without bathing", the same formula as Pc 56's fire. He is working "even if he just sweeps the yard of a building", and the yard, pariveṇa, is the ground outside a dwelling. The least work that counts is sweeping it; Ajahn Brahm and the Concise BMC give the minimum as cleaning one's dwelling.22​ 23​ 24​ 25 He is travelling if he means to go, is going, or has gone half a yojana, which Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga gives as six kilometres, on the scale of his Appendix I, and his Pātimokkha Manual, Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm as about eight.26​ 27​ 28​ 29 And there is wind and rain when a dusty wind blows and two or three drops fall on the body.30 A monk on pilgrimage in the plain who has walked has, more often than not, his occasion already.

    Effort

    The Vibhaṅga defines bathing by what it is done with, bath powder or soap: every effort is an offence of wrong conduct, and the pācittiya falls when the bath is finished.31 Brahmāli renders cuṇṇa and mattika, powder and clay, by their function, as the two principal substances for cleaning the body.32 A rinse with water alone, then, seems not to be the bathing the rule counts, and the Manual's first point is a bath that includes a cleaning agent.33

    Perception

    What one believes about the interval does not soften the offence: where less than a half-month has passed, the pācittiya falls under all three perceptions, the pattern Thanissaro refers to Pc 4. Where more than a half-month has passed but the monk thinks it has not, or is unsure, it is an offence of wrong conduct.34​ 35

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    Beside the occasions and the territory, a monk may bathe while crossing a body of water, and in an emergency, for which Thanissaro gives the Commentary's example, a monk chased by bees who jumps into the water.36​ 37

    The letter of the rule now reaches few monks, and Brahmāli, writing of the Khandhaka's rules on bathing and grooming, says why that is no loss: "Such universal principles are sometimes better guides to proper conduct than specific rules, which often lose their relevance in the course of time."38 A monk outside the central Ganges plain who lingers in a hot shower while others wait for it has not broken this rule. He has kept the letter, and missed the measure the Buddha asked of the monks at the hot springs.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — every effort toward a bath within the half-month, outside an occasion; the pācittiya falls when the bath is finished.39
    • Dukkaṭa — bathing when more than a half-month has passed, but perceiving it as less, or being unsure.40

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if it is an appropriate occasion; if he bathes at intervals of a half-month; if he bathes at intervals of more than a half-month; if he bathes while crossing a body of water; if he is outside the central Ganges plain; if there is an emergency; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.41

    Related Offences

    • Pc 56 — lighting a fire when not ill: the neighbouring rule, with the same definition of illness as not being comfortable without, and the same pattern of amendment for monks afraid of wrongdoing.
    • Pc 53 — playing in water: bathing is not playing, and playing while bathing is that rule's offence, not this one's.
    • Kd 15:1 — the Khandhaka's rules on how one bathes, where this rule regulates how often: rubbing the body against trees, posts or walls, and the ornamented scrubbers, are each an offence of wrong conduct, with an allowance for a monk weak from old age.42

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; the first substory is given nearly whole and the five amendments are summarised. The Manual, the recitation, Brahmāli's Appendix I, Glossary and introductions, the class transcript (session 029, 19 June 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1 and BMC2, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were checked, with the Khandhaka passages on Soṇa's requests, the mangoes and bathing. Ajahn Brahm's telling of the origin story differs from Brahmāli's in its details, and the Story keeps Brahmāli's. The class is not cited: its points on the rule's narrow reach and its spirit are carried by Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm. The Commentary is known here only through Thanissaro.
