    Pc 32

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Rājagaha in the Bamboo Grove, the squirrel sanctuary. At that time, because of his loss of material support and honour, Devadatta and his followers had to ask families repeatedly to get invited to meals. People complained and criticised him: "How can the Sakyan monastics repeatedly ask families to get invited to meals? Who doesn't like nice food? Who doesn't prefer tasty food?" The monks heard the complaints, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised those monks. They told the Buddha, who questioned Devadatta, established that it was true, and rebuked him — "Foolish man, how can you do this? This will affect people's confidence" — and laid down a first preliminary ruling: "If a monk eats in a group, he commits an offense entailing confession."1​ 2

    That ruling was then amended seven times, and every amendment came about the same way. People invited monks; the monks, "knowing that the Buddha had prohibited eating in a group and being afraid of wrongdoing," did not accept; the matter was reported to the Buddha; and he allowed the case.3 Sick monks were invited, and he allowed a sick monk to eat in a group. During the robe-giving season people prepared a meal together with robe-cloth and said, "We wish to offer a meal and then give robe-cloth"; the monks refused, and as a result the people "only got a small amount of robe-cloth," and he allowed eating in a group during the robe-giving season. Monks who were making robes were invited, and he allowed it at a time of making robes. Monks travelling with a group of people said, "Please wait a moment while we walk for alms," and were told, "Venerables, please eat right here"; they refused, and he allowed it when travelling. Monks travelling by boat said, "Please go to the shore for a moment while we walk for alms," and were told the same; they refused, and he allowed it on board a boat. Monks who had completed the rainy-season residence in various regions came to Rājagaha to visit the Buddha and were invited by people who saw them; they refused, and he allowed it on big occasions.4

    The eighth case was different, because the person refused did not simply report it. A relative of King Seniya Bimbisāra of Magadha who had gone forth with the Ājīvaka ascetics went to the king and said, "Great king, I wish to make a meal for the monastics of all religions." "That's fine, sir, if you first feed the Sangha of monks headed by the Buddha." "I'll do that." He sent a message to the monks — "Please accept a meal from me tomorrow" — and they did not accept. The Ājīvaka ascetic then went to the Buddha, exchanged pleasantries with him, and said, "Good Gotama has gone forth and so have I. One who has gone forth should receive alms from another who has gone forth. Good Gotama, please accept a meal from me tomorrow together with the Sangha of monks." The Buddha consented by remaining silent. The Ājīvaka understood that the Buddha had consented, and he left. Soon afterwards the Buddha gave a teaching and addressed the monks: "Monks, I allow you to eat in a group when the meal is given by a monastic." He then amended the training rule for the last time.5

    Synopsis — Devadatta, his support failing, keeps his faction fed by soliciting invitations from household after household, and the Buddha forbids eating in a group; seven times over, scrupulous monks then refuse invitations they should have accepted, and each refusal produces an exemption, the last of them prompted by an Ājīvaka ascetic who took his rebuffed invitation to the Buddha in person.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk eats in a group, except on an appropriate occasion, he commits an offense entailing confession. These are the appropriate occasions: he is sick; it is the robe-giving season; it is a time of making robes; he is traveling; he is on a boat; it is a big occasion; it is a meal given by a monastic."6

    Condensed recitation translation — "Eating as part of a specifically invited group of four or more bhikkhus, except on given occasions, is a pācittiya."7

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "A group meal, except at the proper occasions, is to be confessed. Here the proper occasions are these: a time of illness, a time of giving cloth, a time of making robes, a time of going on a journey, a time of embarking on a boat, a great occasion, a time when the meal is supplied by monks. These are the proper occasions here."8

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Eating food obtained in a group of four or more (Ganabhojane), except on certain occasions is an offence of Pācittiya."9

    Ñāṇatusita

    "In eating [a meal] in a group, except at the [right] occasion, [there is a case] involving expiation. Here the occasion is this: the occasion of illness; the occasion of a giving of robe [-cloths]; the occasion of a robe-making; the occasion of going on a [long] journey; the occasion of voyaging on a boat; the occasion of a great [gathering]; the occasion of a meal [made] by an ascetic; this is the occasion here."10

    The rule's own word is gaṇabhojana, a group meal, and nothing in the word says what makes a meal a group meal. That is the whole of this rule's difficulty, and two of the renderings answer it inside the rule. Thanissaro's "a group meal", Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga and Ñāṇatusita leave it open, as the Pāli does. Brahmāli's recitation has "a specifically invited group of four or more bhikkhus", and Ajahn Brahm's "a group of four or more" with no invitation at all: the two rival readings, each written into a rule statement.11​ 12 I state the rule from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga, and the readings are set out below under Object. The occasions are rendered alike in substance, and where a word is unclear the Vibhaṅga's own definitions settle it.13

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    Four monks fed together at a donor's naming are already the size of a Saṅgha, and this rule keeps the invited meal from becoming the ground a faction grows on, for the unity of the Saṅgha and out of compassion for the families who feed it. Devadatta, his support failing, kept his followers fed by asking household after household for invitations, and the people's complaint was the plain one, "Who doesn't like nice food?"14 The purposes the Buddha gave for the rule, as the Cullavagga records them, are the restraint of evil-minded monks, the comfort of well-behaved ones, that those with evil desires "will not split the Community by (forming) a faction", and compassion for families.15

    The ruling was then amended seven times. Ajahn Brahm reads the number as evidence that the rule in its first form was hard to keep, and the origin story bears him out: each amendment follows monks who, for fear of wrongdoing, refused a meal they might have taken.16​ 17 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual gives eleven factors, seven of them the occasions; the rule itself comes down to the meal, the eating and the exemptions.18

    Object

    The Vibhaṅga defines a group meal as one where four monks, having been invited, eat any of the five cooked foods.19 It does not matter who first thought of the meal: Thanissaro reports the Parivāra as extending the rule to a meal the donor offers on his own initiative as well as to one a monk asks for.20 What the invitation must be is less settled.

    Disputed — what makes an invitation to four or more monks a group meal: the naming of food, the naming of the monks, or nothing beyond their number.

    Thanissaro reports the Commentary as requiring that the invitation mention a meal or food, so that an invitation to eat "in the morning" escapes the rule; Ajahn Brahm reports this as the usual practice in Thailand.21​ 22 Against it, Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm both argue that the Vibhaṅga's formula for an invitation recurs at Pc 33 and Pc 46, where a food-naming condition would make no sense, and that in the amendment stories monks refuse invitations that name no food.23​ 24 Thanissaro holds that the question is whether the invitation names the individual monks, so that a Community meal, and a meal whose monks the Saṅgha's meal designator chooses, fall outside the rule; the Manual follows him by name.25​ 26 Ajahn Brahm holds that "the words of the donor's invitation are irrelevant": any cooked food given to four or more monks is a group meal, though he grants that his reading "is impractical".27 Two of the modern authorities hold the second reading, and it is the one a monk will most often meet; but the Vibhaṅga does not choose among the three, and I do not; only the Vibhaṅga's occasions and non-offences below keep a monk safe on every one.

    On that second reading, the Khandhaka's arrangements do the work. Where a whole Community could no longer be fed, the Cullavagga allowed donors other kinds of meal: meals for so many monks chosen by the Saṅgha's meal designator, the bhattuddesaka; meals for monks invited by name; meals for which lots are drawn; and meals at regular intervals.28 Since an official of the Saṅgha then decides who goes, no clique can form; the rule bites where the donor names four or more monks, or "Ven. X and four of his friends", whether to the monks or to the designator.29​ 30 The arrangement is not antique. Brahmāli's own monastery appointed its abbot designator by formal act of the Saṅgha, so that, as he put it, "you cannot fall into offences such as this if it goes through the proper channel".31

    Effort

    The offence falls in the eating: wrong conduct for accepting the food intending to eat it, and a pācittiya for every mouthful.32 Whether the monks then eat together makes no difference; four who receive the meal and eat it apart still incur the full penalty.33 Perception does not mitigate. A monk who does not see a group meal as one still incurs the pācittiya, and one who takes an ordinary meal for a group meal, or is unsure, incurs wrong conduct.34

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The seven occasions are wider than they sound, and the Vibhaṅga defines each.35 Sickness includes "even if he has cracked feet". The robe-giving season is the last month of the rains, or five months for a monk who has taken part in the robe-making ceremony, whose machinery is at NP 1. Making robes covers any work on a robe, and Brahmāli's endnote reports the Mahāpaccarī's "Even one who just threads the needle."36 Travelling covers before, during and after a journey of half a yojana, which Thanissaro puts at eight kilometres and Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga, on a shorter yojana, at six; a boat journey of any length counts.37​ 38 A big occasion is one where two or three monks can get by on almsround but four cannot. A meal given by a monastic is one made by any kind of wanderer; Thanissaro reports the Commentary as including one's own co-religionists, which, he adds, lets a monk feed a group of friends from his own resources.39 Beyond the seven, there is no offence where only two or three eat together, where they eat together after walking for alms, at a regular meal, a lottery meal, a half-monthly meal, or on the observance day and the day after.40

    So the rule is not easy to fall into, and what it guards is the one thing three monks cannot do and four can. Thanissaro gives the reason for the number: "the smallest faction that can create a split in the Community is four bhikkhus", and Ajahn Brahm agrees.41​ 42 The rule was laid down against Devadatta, whose faction was being fed by the households it courted, and the number in it is the number at which a faction becomes a schism.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • An offence of wrong conduct for receiving the food with the intention of eating it, outside the appropriate occasions.43
    • A pācittiya for every mouthful swallowed.44
    • An offence of wrong conduct where it is not a group meal but the monk perceives it as one or is unsure.45

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if it is an appropriate occasion; if two or three eat together; if they eat together after walking for alms; if it is a regular meal invitation; if it is a meal for which lots are drawn; if it is a half-monthly meal; if it is on the observance day; if it is on the day after the observance day; if it is anything apart from the five cooked foods; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.46 On Thanissaro's reading, a Community meal and a designated meal are outside the rule as well.47

    Related Offences

    • Pc 33 — the next rule, sharing this rule's formula for an invitation.
    • Pc 46 — the third rule with the same formula, and with this one among the allowances relaxed in the robe season.
    • NP 1 — where the robe season and the robe-making ceremony are set out.
    • Sg 10, Sg 11 — the schism rules, and the reason four is this rule's threshold.
    • Pc 4 — the perception pattern, which runs here in the severe direction.

    Related Suttas

    • SN 16.11 — Ānanda, asked by Kassapa why this rule was laid down, gives its reasons, among them that "those of evil wishes, by forming a faction, not create a schism in the Saṅgha", and "sympathy towards families": the faction and the families of Devadatta's story.48
