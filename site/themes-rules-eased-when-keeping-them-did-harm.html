      10. Rules Eased When Keeping Them Did Harm

    Rules Eased When Keeping Them Did Harm

    The rule against eating at a public guesthouse day after day was laid down against the group of six, who were served there respectfully and stopped going back to the monastery, while people complained, "We don't prepare the almsfood just for them; we prepare it for everyone." Soon afterwards Venerable Sāriputta, travelling to Sāvatthī, ate at such a guesthouse and fell so ill that he could not leave. The next day the people there said to him, "Please eat, venerable." He knew what the Buddha had prohibited, "and because he was afraid of wrongdoing, he did not accept. As a consequence, he missed his meal." When the Buddha heard of it, he said, "Monks, I allow a sick monk to stay on at a public guesthouse and eat alms there."1 That is Pc 31 in its final form, which reaches only "a monk who is not sick".

    The same turn comes in rule after rule, and Brahmāli's phrase for it is the same each time: the monks were "afraid of wrongdoing". After Pc 40 forbade eating what had not been given, the monks would not use water or tooth cleaners, and the Buddha allowed them "to use water and tooth cleaners after taking them yourselves". After Pc 56 forbade lighting a fire to warm oneself, sick monks who had been comfortable by a fire stopped lighting one, and then the monks stopped lighting lamps and saunas too, and the Buddha allowed the sick monk his fire and any monk a fire "if there's a suitable reason".2 After Pc 39 forbade asking for fine foods, sick monks who had been comfortable on them stopped asking, and the Buddha allowed "a sick monk to eat fine foods that he has requested for himself".3 Pc 57 was laid down after monks bathed until dark while King Bimbisāra waited, and the monks then would not bathe in the heat or with a fever, or when sick, or after building work or travel, or after robe-making out in the wind and rain, and they went to sleep covered in sweat or still wet. The Buddha allowed each of these in turn, five amendments in all.4

    At Pc 33 the harm reached the giving itself. During the robe-giving season people invited the monks, saying, "We wish to offer a meal and give robe-cloth", and the monks, already invited elsewhere, would not accept, so that little robe-cloth came in. The Buddha allowed it in the robe season and in the time of making robes. Then, with the rule already in its final form, Ānanda went with the Buddha to a family, was offered cooked food and would not accept it because he was expecting another meal. The Buddha said to him, "Accept it, Ānanda", and showed him how: "assign that meal to someone else and then receive this."5

    In none of these stories is the scruple rebuked. The monks who would not bathe, eat or warm themselves were keeping the rule as they understood it, and the Buddha answered them each time with an allowance. Nor did any of them ease the rule for himself. Sāriputta went hungry, the sick monks stayed cold, and the case went to the Buddha, who decided it. The fear of wrongdoing carried the hard case to the one who could judge it, and it seems fair to say that this is how the rules learned.

    The allowances reached the harm and stopped there. The final wording keeps the first fault where it was. The group of six who settled in at the guesthouse would still commit the offence, because none of them was sick. A monk who lights a fire to warm himself for the pleasure of it still commits the offence, and so does one who asks for ghee and honey when he is well. What Pc 56 still forbids is a fire lit for warmth by a monk who does not need it.

    One at a time, these amendments read as exceptions; taken as a whole, they show a rule's final form as a record of the cases it was tried against. The words "who is not sick" at Pc 31, Pc 39 and Pc 56 stand for Sāriputta at the guesthouse and the sick monks by their unlit fires. A monk who keeps a rule with all its exceptions keeps the Buddha's judgment on each case that made it, and a monk who is afraid of wrongdoing will find many of his own hard cases there, already decided.

    Rules — Pc 31, Pc 33, Pc 39, Pc 40, Pc 56, Pc 57

    1. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 31, origin story: "We don't prepare the almsfood just for them; we prepare it for everyone"; "Please eat, venerable"; "and because he was afraid of wrongdoing, he did not accept. As a consequence, he missed his meal"; "Monks, I allow a sick monk to stay on at a public guesthouse and eat alms there"; final ruling: "If a monk who is not sick eats more than one almsmeal at a public guesthouse, he commits an offense entailing confession."↩︎

    2. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 40, origin story: "Soon afterwards the monks did not use water or tooth cleaners because they were afraid of wrongdoing"; "Monks, I allow you to use water and tooth cleaners after taking them yourselves"; Pc 56, origin story: "But now that the Buddha has prohibited this, we don't warm ourselves because we're afraid of wrongdoing. Because of that we're not comfortable"; "Soon afterwards the monks did not light lamps, small fires, or saunas because they were afraid of wrongdoing"; "Monks, I allow you to light a fire, or to have one lit, if there's a suitable reason."↩︎

    3. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 39, origin story: "Monks, I allow a sick monk to eat fine foods that he has requested for himself."↩︎

    4. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 57, origin story: "Soon afterwards, because they were afraid of wrongdoing, the monks did not bathe when it was hot or when they had a fever, and they went to sleep covered in sweat"; "Monks, I allow a sick monk to bathe at intervals of less than a half-month."↩︎

    5. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 33, origin story: "We wish to offer a meal and give robe-cloth"; "But knowing that the Buddha had prohibited eating a meal before another and being afraid of wrongdoing, they did not accept. As a result, they only got a small amount of robe-cloth" (the Pāli is impersonal, cīvaraṁ parittaṁ uppajjati, "little robe-cloth arose"); "Being afraid of wrongdoing, Ānanda did not accept it"; "Accept it, Ānanda"; "Well then, Ānanda, assign that meal to someone else and then receive this."↩︎
