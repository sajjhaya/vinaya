    Pc 35

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. A certain brahmin had invited the monks for a meal. When the monks had finished and refused an invitation to eat more, they went to their respective families, where some ate and some took away almsfood.1

    Soon afterwards that brahmin said to his neighbours, "The monks have been satisfied by me. Come, and I'll satisfy you, too." — "How could you satisfy us? Those monks who were invited by you came to our houses. Some of them ate there and some took away almsfood." That brahmin complained and criticised those monks, "How could the venerables eat in our house and afterwards eat elsewhere? Am I not able to give them as much as they need?"2

    The monks heard the complaints of that brahmin, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised those monks, "How could those monks finish their meal and refuse an invitation to eat more, and then eat elsewhere?" The Buddha questioned the monks, established that it was true, rebuked them, and laid down a preliminary ruling: "If a monk has finished his meal and refused an invitation to eat more, and then eats fresh or cooked food, he commits an offense entailing confession."3

    Soon afterwards the monks were bringing back fine almsfood for the sick monks. But because the sick monks were unable to eat as much as they had intended, the monks threw the leftovers away. When the Buddha heard the loud sound of crows cawing, he asked Venerable Ānanda, "Ānanda, why is there this loud sound of crows cawing?" Ānanda told him what had happened, and the Buddha said, "But Ānanda, don't the monks eat the leftovers from those who are sick?" — "No, sir." Soon afterwards the Buddha gave a teaching and addressed the monks: "Monks, I allow you to eat the leftovers both from those who are sick and from those who are not sick. And, monks, this is how you make food left over: 'I've had enough.'" He then stated the rule in its final form.4

    Synopsis — A brahmin who has fed the monks to their own declared satisfaction learns from his neighbours that they went on to eat elsewhere, and his complaint — that he was thought incapable of feeding them — produces a rule against eating again once one has refused; and when the rule's first effect is that food carried back for the sick is thrown to the crows, the Buddha adds the allowance that gives this rule its whole practical apparatus, the making of food left over by four words spoken by a monk who cannot eat it himself.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk has finished his meal and refused an invitation to eat more, and then eats fresh or cooked food that is not left over, he commits an offense entailing confession."5

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu who has eaten and refused an offer of more food eats food that is not leftover, he incurs a pācittiya."6

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu, having eaten and turned down an offer (of further food), chew or consume staple or non-staple food that is not leftover, it is to be confessed."7

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Having finished one's meal, that day receiving more food and eating it, except when it is left over from another monk's meal, is an offence of Pācittiya."8

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu who has eaten [a meal], who has been invited [to take more and refused], should chew uncooked food or eat cooked food which is not left over, [this is a case] involving expiation."9

    The renderings agree on what the rule forbids and differ in two places, neither of which changes it. The first is the pair of food-words, "fresh" and "cooked" in Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga against Thanissaro's "non-staple" and "staple"; the choice between them is worked through at Pc 29, though it is this rule's word-analysis that defines both categories.10 The second is pavārita, where the renderings run from "refused an invitation to eat more" to "refused an offer of more food" to Ñāṇatusita's "invited [to take more and refused]". They mean the same thing: Brahmāli explains that the word can mean both "invited" and "satisfied", and that here the monk "has expressed his satisfaction after being invited to take more".11​ 12 His endnote also corrects the Pāli's punctuation of the definition, so that the refusal has five aspects, as the Parivāra counts them, and not four.13 Ajahn Brahm's "having finished one's meal" is his own term for the whole phrase, complete on his six factors once a monk has eaten even a grain of rice and refused more offered by a layperson within reach, so it puts into the line the very question discussed below under Effort.14

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A monk's "enough" at a donor's table is a word given, and this rule holds him to it, so that a household's hospitality is never quietly set aside for another's, for the confidence of the households that feed him. The brahmin of the origin story had fed the monks to their own declared satisfaction and boasted of it to his neighbours, only to learn that they had gone on to eat in other houses: "Am I not able to give them as much as they need?"15 Thanissaro explains the custom behind the complaint: a donor kept offering until the food ran out or the monks refused, so the refusal was his measure of success, and "where there is pride there is bound to be wounded pride". He adds a second purpose that runs the other way: since it is the refusal that binds, a monk whose donor stopped offering before he refused may eat elsewhere later that morning, and the rule protects monks "from being forced to go hungry by stingy or impoverished donors".16

    Almost all of the rule is definition. It is a single clause, and what a monk needs to know is packed into two lists in the Vibhaṅga: the five aspects of having refused, and the steps by which food is made left over. Thanissaro gives two factors, object and effort, and they are taken here in that order, with the procedure for leftover food left to Non-offences.17

    Object

    The object is fresh or cooked food that is not left over.18 The tonics are outside it, since the definition of fresh food excludes them, but only when taken as tonics. A monk in this rule's position who receives them for the purpose of food commits an offence of wrong conduct, and another for every mouthful.19 Taken for a reason, they are no offence, and Thanissaro gives the Commentary's definition of the reason: thirst for juice drinks, and for the tonics and medicines the illness they treat, which, as Thanissaro notes from NP 23, includes hunger and fatigue.20 The question is the description under which a thing is eaten, whatever the thing.

    Effort

    The effort is eating such food after having eaten and refused, and both conditions are defined.

    Having refused. The Vibhaṅga's five aspects are the part of this rule to know by heart: "there is eating; there is cooked food; they stand within arm's reach; there is an offering; there is a refusal."21 Each must be present. Thanissaro reports the Commentary filling them out: a monk who has already finished when the food comes is not eating, so refusing it does not bind him; food that is not one of the five cooked foods, such as fruit or cheese, does not count, and nor does cooked food he may not eat, whether because of how it was obtained (Sg 13) or what it is.22 The offerer must be within arm's reach, the measure Pc 40 defines, and the refusal may be by word or by gesture.23 Nothing in the texts, Thanissaro observes, says the offerer must be a lay person, so food handed round by monks and novices would apparently count as well.24 Ajahn Brahm disagrees for monks, though not for novices: his factor is "A layperson brings more bhojana", food "distributed by monks" leaves a factor missing, and a snack "handed out by a layperson or novice" binds.25

    Having eaten. The Vibhaṅga defines it as having eaten any of the five cooked foods, "even what fits on the tip of a blade of grass", which could mean having taken a first mouthful or having finished a meal.26

    Disputed — whether a monk who has refused while his bowl still holds food may go on eating it.

    In Thanissaro's report, the Commentary takes having eaten as the first mouthful, concludes that the word adds nothing, and lets the monk continue only so long as he does not move from where he sits.27 Ajahn Brahm reads the texts as stricter still: the rule bites at the moment of refusal, so a monk who refuses a latecomer after his first mouthful may not take a second, though his own view is that he should be allowed to finish, which he admits has "little support in the texts".28 Thanissaro takes it as having finished the meal, arguing from the Canon's own use of bhuttāvin, which in the three other places he cites means one who has finished; on that reading a monk driven from his seat by spilt soup or by ants may still finish what his donor is begging him to eat. Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual takes the same side.29​ 30 I follow Thanissaro, whose argument from the Canon's usage I find the strongest of the three; but the Commentary's reading is the older, and the rule's own words do not decide between them.

    On the reading followed here, the operative question is when the meal is finished. The Commentary's answer, which Thanissaro reports, is either when there is no food left in one's bowl, hand or mouth, or when one has decided one has had enough of that meal.31 Until then he may turn food down and accept it as he likes, refusing vegetables, say, and accepting rice.32 Brahmāli applied the same test to a case at a meal offering, where a birthday cake came out with the tea after the monks had said they had had enough. The rule did not apply, in his view, since they had declined what was being served without deciding that the meal was over, and the donors were the same people, so that refusing the cake, not accepting it, would have disappointed them.33 Receiving food with the intention of eating it after that point is an offence of wrong conduct, and every mouthful a pācittiya.34 Perception does not mitigate: whether he perceives the food as not left over, is unsure, or thinks it left over, the full offence stands, on the pattern of Pc 4.35

    Brahmāli found one real difficulty in the rule, and named it himself. Pc 33 allows a monk to eat several invited meals in the order the invitations came, yet at each of them he will at some point say he has had enough. His reading is that this rule bears on donors who do not know: where they are aware that he has another meal to go to, they cannot be disappointed, as with the breakfast offered before lunch to a monk travelling in Singapore or Malaysia. He calls it "a little bit basically my interpretation", with no evidence beyond the conflict with Pc 33.36 On Thanissaro's account of the refusal the answer is narrower: a monk who is not offered more cooked food, or does not refuse it, has not refused, and a series of meals is caught only where he refuses at one of them.37

    The Mahāvagga once relaxed the rule in famine, and the relaxation was later withdrawn; Thanissaro concludes that the famine allowances "were part of the Buddha's repertoire but not of the Community's after his parinibbāna".38

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    What makes this rule livable is the second sub-story. The monks who carried fine food back for the sick had themselves refused, so when the sick could not finish it nobody present could eat it, and it went to the crows.39 The Buddha's allowance has two limbs. A sick monk's leftovers are left over as they stand, and Ajahn Brahm adds that a sick monk unable to eat enough at one sitting may eat from his own leftovers as often as he likes.40​ 41 The other limb is a short formal act, which Thanissaro sets out in seven factors, with X the monk holding the food and Y the monk who makes it left over:

    1. the food is allowable;
    2. it has been formally received by any bhikkhu except Y;
    3. X lifts it up in Y's presence;
    4. Y is within arm's reach of X;
    5. Y has finished his meal;
    6. Y has not yet got up from the seat where he finished and refused; and
    7. Y says, "All that is enough" — Alam'etaṁ sabbaṁ.42

    The monk who makes the food allowable is precisely the monk who cannot eat it; any other monk may. Thanissaro gives the two cases a monk will meet: X has refused but cannot finish his bowl, and having Y make it left over lets him take it back and finish it later; or friends of the donor arrive late with food after everyone but X has refused, and once it is made left over, all but Y may eat.43 The Commentary also allows a monk who has finished to accept more food, have another finished monk make it left over, and eat it, which Ajahn Brahm reports with the comment that "it is against the spirit of this rule".44 The procedure is little used, but it is the Buddha's answer to food carried back for the sick and thrown to the crows, and a monk who does not know it cannot use it.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — receiving fresh or cooked food that is not left over with the intention of eating it.45
    • Dukkaṭa — receiving post-midday, seven-day or lifetime tonics for the purpose of food, and a further dukkaṭa for every mouthful.46
    • Dukkaṭa — where the food is left over but he does not perceive it as such, or is unsure. Where it is not left over, all three perceptions carry the full pācittiya.47

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he eats it after having it made left over; if he receives it with the intention of having it made left over and then eating it; if he is taking food for the benefit of someone else; if he eats the leftovers from a sick person; if, when there is a reason, he uses post-midday tonics, seven-day tonics, or lifetime tonics; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.48

    The second covers a monk who accepts food he may not yet eat in order to have it made left over, so the procedure may be planned for and not only fallen back on. The fifth exempts the tonics' proper use, not the tonics as such.49

    Related Offences

    • Pc 36 — the next rule: tricking a monk who has refused into eating, in order to accuse him. It borrows this rule's definitions.
    • Pc 33 — eating before an invitational meal; the two are read as a pair, and Brahmāli names the friction between them.
    • Pc 29 — where the naming of the two food-categories is worked through.
    • Pc 40 — where arm's reach and formal receiving are defined; both are factors here.
    • Pc 4 — the rule whose perception pattern this one follows.
    • NP 23 — the definition of illness that governs when a tonic may be used for its purpose.
    • Sg 13 — one of the ways cooked food can be unallowable, so that refusing it does not bind.
    • Bhikkhunī Pc 44 — where this rule and Pc 33 are a single rule.

    Related Suttas

    • MN 3 — the Buddha's parable of almsfood left over after he "had eaten, refused more food" (bhuttāvī … pavārito, the rule's own two conditions), offered to two hungry monks who have done neither, and theirs to eat if they wish: the rule binds only the monk who has refused, and the one who declines it anyway is the more commended.50
