    How an Accusation Is Heard

    The Vibhaṅga of Pj 2 ends on two cases set side by side. Of the friends Paṇḍaka and Kapila, one was crossing a river when a lump of fat a pig butcher had let slip stuck to his foot. He grabbed it, meaning to give it back, and the owners told him, "You're not a monastic anymore!" A cowherd woman who had seen him crossing invited him to intercourse, and thinking he was no longer a monastic, he accepted. There was no pārājika for the fat, and there was one for the intercourse.1 In the next case a student of Venerable Daḷhika, plagued by lust, stole a turban from a shopkeeper and told his teacher that he was no longer a monk and would disrobe. Daḷhika had the turban brought and valued. It was worth less than five māsaka, and the student was still a monk.2 Brahmāli's comment is that the teacher "is not content with just accepting his student's word", and that it is the duty of a teacher, "and presumably any fellow monastic, to go to some length to help a co-monastic get out of trouble, if at all possible."3 One monk believed a charge he had not examined, and acted on it. The other accused himself, and his charge was examined.

    The same care is shown towards an accused monk. Asked before the Saṅgha whether he remembered doing what the nun Mettiyā said, Dabba the Mallian appealed to what the Buddha knew of him, and the Buddha would not accept the appeal but made him say whether he had done it.4 His story belongs to Characters of the Vinaya. What belongs to this theme is that the Buddha heard the charge against an arahant and made the arahant answer it, and that afterwards, Thanissaro notes, he put the Saṅgha in charge of judging such accusations and gave it a definite pattern, so that its judgments would be "as fair and accurate as possible."5

    The accuser is held to account too. A groundless charge that a monk has committed a pārājika is a saṅghādisesa under Sg 8, and a charge built on a pretext, a true sight with false names attached, is the same offence under Sg 9: Mettiya and Bhūmajaka, coming down from the Vulture Peak, had seen two goats mating and claimed, "now we've seen Dabba copulating with the nun Mettiyā."6 A groundless charge of a saṅghādisesa is a pācittiya under Pc 76.7 "Whether anyone actually believes the charge is not a factor here," Thanissaro writes at Sg 8, so the offence falls on the accuser even when the Saṅgha sees through him.8

    The witness has a voice of her own. Visākhā found Udāyī sitting with a young woman on a concealed seat and told him, "You may not be aiming at that act, but it's hard to convince people with little confidence."9 Aniyata 1 and Aniyata 2 let a trustworthy laywoman's charge open the case, and Thanissaro finds it "remarkable", given the position of women in Indian society at the time, that the Buddha was at one point willing to give her word more weight than the accused monk's.10

    Yet in the Vibhaṅga every case is decided by what the monk admits. If he says he was sitting, he is dealt with for the sitting; if he says he was standing, "he is not to be dealt with."11 Thanissaro explains the change, with a hedge: apparently the two rules were laid down early, and when the general guidelines for accusations came later, some of the group of six "abused the system to impose penalties on innocent bhikkhus they didn't like", so the Buddha added a number of checks "to prevent the system from working against the innocent", one of which gave precedence to dealing with a monk by what he admits.12 Whether a Saṅgha may ever act on overwhelming evidence against a monk who admits nothing is a question that belongs to Aniyata 1, and it is not reopened here.

    No single rule shows the whole of this. The self-accused monk, the accused, the accuser and the witness each come from a different rule and a different class, and in every case the procedure leans the same way, towards the monk who may be innocent. Thanissaro says as much of the Saṅgha's guidelines for accusations: "the main thrust of these guidelines is to prevent an innocent bhikkhu from being unfairly penalized", and as for a guilty monk who escapes, "the laws of kamma guarantee that in the long run he is not getting away with anything at all."13 A monk who hears a charge, against a brother or against himself, has Daḷhika to follow. He sent for the turban and had it valued.

    2. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pj 2, case studies, the last: "Sir, I'm not a monastic anymore. I'll disrobe"; "Venerable Daḷhika had the turban brought and valued. It was worth less than five māsaka coins. Saying, 'There's no offense entailing expulsion,' he gave a teaching."↩︎

    3. Brahmāli, Introduction to the Monks' Analysis, part I, on Pj 2: "The teacher, however, is not content with just accepting his student's word. He asks the student to bring the turban, and then has it valued … This goes to show that it is the duty of a teacher, and presumably any fellow monastic, to go to some length to help a co-monastic get out of trouble, if at all possible."↩︎

    4. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Sg 8, origin story: "Sir, you know what I'm like"; "Dabba, the Dabbas don't give such evasive answers. If it was done by you, say so; if it wasn't, then say that."↩︎

    5. Thanissaro, BMC1, Sg 8: "at some point after this rule was formulated, the Buddha put the Saṅgha in charge of judging accusations of this sort and gave them a definite pattern to follow to ensure that their judgments would be as fair and accurate as possible."↩︎

    6. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Sg 9, origin story: "Previously we spoke of what we had heard, but now we've seen Dabba copulating with the nun Mettiyā."↩︎

    7. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 76, final ruling: "If a monk groundlessly charges a monk with an offense entailing suspension, he commits an offense entailing confession."↩︎

    8. Thanissaro, BMC1, Sg 8: "Whether anyone actually believes the charge is not a factor here."↩︎

    9. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Ay 1, origin story: "Venerable, it's not appropriate for you to sit down in private alone with a woman on a concealed seat suitable for the deed. You may not be aiming at that act, but it's hard to convince people with little confidence."↩︎

    10. Thanissaro, BMC1, Ay 1: "the Buddha at one point was willing to let the bhikkhus give more weight to the word of a female lay follower than to that of the accused bhikkhu. This in itself, considering the general position of women in Indian society at the time, is remarkable."↩︎

    11. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Ay 1, definitions: "'It's true that I was seated, but I didn't have sexual intercourse,' then he is to be dealt with for the sitting"; "'I wasn't seated, but standing,' then he is not to be dealt with."↩︎

    12. Thanissaro, BMC1, Ay 1: "Apparently what happened was that this rule and the following one were formulated early on"; "some group-of-six bhikkhus abused the system to impose penalties on innocent bhikkhus they didn't like (Mv.IX.3.1), so the Buddha formulated a number of checks to prevent the system from working against the innocent"; "One of those checks, in effect, was to give precedence to As 4".↩︎

    13. Thanissaro, BMC1, Ay 1: "Obviously, the main thrust of these guidelines is to prevent an innocent bhikkhu from being unfairly penalized. As for the opposite case—a guilty bhikkhu getting away with no penalty—we should remember that the laws of kamma guarantee that in the long run he is not getting away with anything at all."↩︎
