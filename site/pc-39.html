    Pc 39

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. The monks from the group of six were eating fine foods that they had requested for themselves. People complained and criticised them, "How can the Sakyan monastics eat fine foods that they have requested for themselves? Who doesn't like nice food? Who doesn't prefer tasty food?"1

    The monks heard the complaints. The monks of few desires complained and criticised those monks, and the Buddha questioned them, established that it was true, rebuked them, and stated the rule. It listed the foods it covered — ghee, butter, oil, honey, syrup, fish, meat, milk and curd — and made it an offence to request any of them for oneself and eat it.2​ 3

    Some time later a number of monks were sick. The monks looking after them asked, "I hope you're bearing up? I hope you're getting better?"4

    "Previously we ate fine foods that we had requested ourselves, and then we were comfortable. But now that the Buddha has prohibited this, we don't request because we're afraid of wrongdoing. And because of that we're not comfortable."5

    They told the Buddha. Soon afterwards he gave a teaching and addressed the monks: "Monks, I allow a sick monk to eat fine foods that he has requested for himself." He then restated the rule with the exemption in it.6​ 7

    Synopsis — The group of six ask laypeople for the best foods and eat them, and the laity's complaint is a shrug rather than an accusation: of course good food is nicer. The rule that follows names nine foods and forbids requesting them for oneself; when sick monks then stop asking and stop recovering, the Buddha amends it, and the two words "who is not sick" carry the whole of what the rule now means in practice.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk who is not sick asks for any of these kinds of fine foods for himself—that is, ghee, butter, oil, honey, syrup, fish, meat, milk, and curd—and then eats it, he commits an offense entailing confession."8

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a healthy bhikkhu asks for ghee, butter, cheese, oil, honey, sugar, fish, meat, milk, or curd, and then eats it, he incurs a pācittiya."9

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "There are these finer staple foods: ghee, fresh butter, oil, honey, sugar/molasses, fish, meat, milk, and curds. Should any bhikkhu who is not ill, having requested finer staple foods such as these for his own sake, then consume them, it is to be confessed."10

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Eating 'special food' having begged for it, except when ill, is an offence of Pācittiya."11

    Ñāṇatusita

    "Those foods which are superior, namely: ghee, butter, oil, honey and molasses, fish, meat, milk, curd; if any bhikkhu, who is not ill, having requested such superior foods for his own benefit, should eat [them], [this is a case] involving expiation."12

    The renderings agree on what the rule forbids and differ over names. The category is "fine foods" in Brahmāli, "finer staple foods" in Thanissaro, "'special food'" in Ajahn Brahm and "superior foods" in Ñāṇatusita; whether the second half of paṇītabhojana is a "staple" is the question Pc 29 works through, and I say "fine foods" and leave Thanissaro his own term.13​ 14​ 15 The list itself comes apart in Brahmāli's works. His recitation gives ten nouns for nine words, rendering navanīta as both "butter" and "cheese".16 And phāṇita is "syrup" in his Vibhaṅga translation and his Appendix I, each arguing for the word against "molasses", but "sugar" in his recitation.17​ 18 I say "syrup". None of these differences changes what is covered. The rule's verb, viññāpetvā, which Ñāṇatusita says has "no exact equivalent" in English, does matter, and Effort takes it up.19

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    What a monk asks for shows what he wants, and this rule keeps the finest foods from becoming the object of his asking, so that need and not appetite decides what he requests, for his own restraint and for the regard of the laity. The group of six asked laypeople for the best foods and ate them, and the laity did not so much accuse them as shrug: "Who doesn't like nice food? Who doesn't prefer tasty food?" The rule then forbade the asking outright, until sick monks, afraid of wrongdoing, stopped asking for the foods that had made them comfortable, and were no longer comfortable.20

    Thanissaro gives three factors, object, effort and result, and almost all the practical weight falls on the exemption, agilāno, "who is not sick", which follows them under Non-offences.21 His order is kept here.

    Object

    The object is the nine fine foods, and the Vibhaṅga defines each: ghee, butter, milk and curd from cows, goats, buffaloes or any animal whose meat is allowable; oil from sesame, mustard, the honey-tree, castor or fat; honey from bees; syrup from sugarcane; fish, "what lives in water"; and the meat of animals whose meat is allowable.22 The first five are the five tonics, whose home is NP 23. "Whose meat is allowable" points to the Mahāvagga, which forbids the flesh of human beings, elephants, horses, dogs, snakes, lions, tigers, leopards, bears and hyenas, human flesh as a serious offence and the rest as wrong conduct.23​ 24 The same chapter adds the rule a monk meets far more often: meat is allowable only if it is pure in three respects, that he has not seen, heard or suspected that the animal was killed for him.25

    Disputed — whether a monk who asks for one of the nine on its own, such as plain milk, has broken this rule or a lesser one.

    Thanissaro reads the Vibhaṅga's "such kinds of fine foods" as covering each of the nine "either on their own or mixed with other foods", so that plain milk and milk on cereal are alike: "The rule means what it says."26 He reports the Commentary taking the fine foods to be the nine substances mixed with grain, so that milk on its own is not one, and asking for it falls under Sk 37, on asking for curry and rice, as wrong conduct; he thinks the older commentators balked at calling some of the nine bhojana, and answers that the Vibhaṅga defines its terms rule by rule.27​ 28 The Vibhaṅga's definitions and its offence ladder are stated of the substances without qualification, and I think Thanissaro has the better of it. But the Commentary's is the older reading, and a monk in a Community that follows it is not without ground. Either way, asking for these things for oneself when well is an offence.

    The category is built for this rule and does not travel. The Concise BMC notes that outside it milk and curds count as ordinary non-staple food, so the definition is not to be carried into Pc 35.29 Within it, the nine count mixed as well as pure, as Thanissaro reads the Vibhaṅga; Brahmāli gave chocolate, for its sugar, as the instance, unless the amount is negligible.30​ 31

    Effort

    The effort is asking, and the Vibhaṅga punishes it in steps: wrong conduct for each request, wrong conduct again for receiving the food intending to eat it.32 What makes a request is not defined. Ajahn Brahm places it in the relationship: asking laypeople who are neither relatives nor people who have given an invitation.33 Where the texts are silent, Thanissaro reasons to two edges. The food apparently must come because of the request, from the one asked or from someone who heard; food from someone who knew nothing of it apparently does not count. And, as a suggestion borrowed from the Commentary's treatment of a revoked banishment at Sg 13, if those who heard keep offering it afterwards, a monk should tell them he may not accept it, and may then accept if they say, unprompted, that they offer it of their own wish.34 Food already meant for the Saṅgha is not being asked for at all: the Concise BMC puts it generally, that asking a layperson to formally offer what has already been provided for the Saṅgha is not a request.35 Where what arrives is not what was asked for, Thanissaro reports the Commentary counting it no offence to accept and eat it, though the asking still carries its wrong conduct.36

    Result

    The pācittiya is for every mouthful.37 The Parivāra keeps the limit of that as a riddle, asking how many offences entailing confession one might commit at a single moment, with the answer nine; Brahmāli's endnote gives the Commentary's explanation, a monk who has asked for all nine fine foods and pressed them "into a single mouthful".38

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    "Who is not sick" is defined broadly: one "who is comfortable without fine foods".39 The threshold is discomfort, not diagnosis, and Brahmāli called it "defined incredibly broadly": any good reason the food is needed will do, and the one thing that will not is craving.40 Thanissaro reaches a compatible line from the code itself. Mere hunger is answered by Sk 37, which lets a hungry monk ask for rice and bean curry, so illness here, he thinks, is probably fatigue, weakness or malnutrition that comes from lacking these particular foods.41 Perception does not soften the rule. A monk who is not sick commits the full offence whether he thinks himself sick or not; one who is sick but does not perceive it, or is unsure, commits wrong conduct.42

    The exemption was not in the rule at first: it came with the sick monks of the second sub-story, and the Buddha's answer to them was to let them ask.43 And so what the rule withholds from a monk is the asking that comes from craving, not the care that comes from need.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — for each request, and again on receiving the food intending to eat it.44
    • Dukkaṭa — where he is sick but does not perceive himself so, or is unsure. Where he is not sick, all three perceptions carry the full pācittiya.45
    • Dukkaṭa, under Sk 37 — asking for ordinary food, rice or bean curry, for oneself when not sick.46

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he is sick; if he asked for it when he was sick, but eats it when he is no longer sick; if he eats the leftovers from one who is sick; if it is from relatives; if it is from those who have given an invitation; if it is for the benefit of someone else; if it is by means of his own property; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.47

    Asking for another covers the case a monastery meets most: a monk who is well may ask on behalf of a sick monk, and Ajahn Brahm gives the bedridden monk as the instance.48 The invitation is the subject of Pc 47. Beyond the list, the Mahāvagga lets a monk setting out on a wilderness road with little food look for provisions, among them oil and ghee; Thanissaro gives the Commentary's order: he waits first for offerings, then asks relatives or those who have invited him, or tries the almsround, and asks others only after that, and never for more than the journey needs.49​ 50

    Related Offences

    • NP 23 — the five tonics, the first five of the nine; their home.
    • Sk 37 — the same clause with rice and bean curry as its object; the lesser offence where this rule does not reach, and the reason mere hunger is not this rule's illness.
    • Pc 29 — where the naming of the food categories is worked through.
    • Pc 47 — the invitation, pavāraṇā, to ask for tonics and medicines.
    • Pc 35 — where the staple foods are defined, and which this rule's category is not to be read into.
    • Pc 4 — the rule whose perception pattern this one follows.

    Related Suttas

    • AN 8.30 — to a contented monk at ease in the jhānas his scraps of almsfood seem a fine dish, and his medicine of fermented cow's urine "ghee, butter, oil, honey, and molasses", five of the rule's nine fine foods: contentment makes the asking needless.51
    • MN 91 — the Buddha "takes his food experiencing the taste, though not experiencing greed for the taste", the line between need and appetite that the rule draws.52
