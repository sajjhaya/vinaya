    Pc 31

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time a certain association was preparing an almsmeal at a public guesthouse not far from Sāvatthī.1

    Then, after robing up in the morning, the monks from the group of six took their bowls and robes and entered Sāvatthī for alms. Not getting anything, they went to that public guesthouse. Because it was a long time since they had been there, people served them respectfully. A second and a third day those monks did the same thing. Then they thought, "What's the point of returning to the monastery? Tomorrow we'll just have to come back here." So they stayed on and on right there, eating alms at the guesthouse, while the monastics of other religions left.2

    People complained and criticised them: "How can the Sakyan monastics stay on and on, eating alms at the guesthouse? We don't prepare the almsfood just for them; we prepare it for everyone." The monks heard the complaints, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised those monks. They told the Buddha, who questioned them, established that it was true, and rebuked them — "Foolish men, how could you do this? This will affect people's confidence" — and laid down a preliminary ruling.3​ 4

    Soon afterwards Venerable Sāriputta was travelling through the Kosalan country on his way to Sāvatthī when he came to a public guesthouse. Because it was a long time since he had been there, people served him respectfully. After he had eaten, Sāriputta became severely ill, and he was unable to leave that guesthouse. On the second day, too, those people said to him, "Please eat, venerable." But since he knew that the Buddha had prohibited eating alms at a public guesthouse after staying on and on, and because he was afraid of wrongdoing, he did not accept. As a consequence, he missed his meal.5

    When he arrived at Sāvatthī, he told the monks what had happened, and they in turn told the Buddha. Soon afterwards the Buddha gave a teaching and addressed the monks: "Monks, I allow a sick monk to stay on at a public guesthouse and eat alms there." He then amended the training rule.6

    Synopsis — the group of six settle in at a charitable guesthouse outside Sāvatthī and eat there day after day until the public complains that the food was not laid on for monks alone; the Buddha forbids more than one meal there, and when Sāriputta falls too ill to leave and goes hungry rather than break the new rule, he exempts the monk who cannot leave.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk who is not sick eats more than one almsmeal at a public guesthouse, he commits an offense entailing confession."7

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu who is able to get food elsewhere eats more than one meal consecutively at a public alms centre, he incurs a pācittiya."8

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "A bhikkhu who is not ill may eat one meal at a public alms center. Should he eat more than that, it is to be confessed."9

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Eating food obtained at a hospice having eaten food from there the day before, unless one is too sick to travel, is an offence of Pācittiya."10

    Ñāṇatusita

    "By a bhikkhu who is not ill one alms-meal in a resthouse can be eaten; if he should eat more than that, [this is a case] involving expiation."11

    The renderings agree that a monk who is not sick may eat one meal at such a place and no more. The five renderings name the place four ways, guesthouse, alms centre, hospice, resthouse; none translates āvasatha, which can mean any dwelling, and all paraphrase what the Vibhaṅga describes, a place where food is laid on for the general public.12 The recitation is where they part, and it compresses twice. It has "a bhikkhu who is able to get food elsewhere" for the monk who is not sick, where the Vibhaṅga's own gloss is that "he is able to leave that public guesthouse"; and it has "more than one meal consecutively", where the rule has no such word.13​ 14 The second compression takes a side in the one live question at this rule, discussed below under Effort; I state the rule from the full translation. The object, "cooked food", is the Vibhaṅga's term, and its rendering is discussed at Pc 29.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    Food laid out for everyone is no monk's to settle on, and this rule keeps a monk to the almsround he went forth to live by, for his own contentment and for the confidence of the people who give. The group of six found the guesthouse easier than walking for alms, "What's the point of returning to the monastery?", and stayed on while the wanderers of other religions moved through; the people named the injury in a sentence, "We don't prepare the almsfood just for them; we prepare it for everyone", and the Buddha's rebuke was that it "will affect people's confidence".15

    The chapter on food opens here and runs to Pc 40. Its subject is less food itself than the ways a monk could take more of it, or take it more easily, than the almsround allows. Thanissaro's Addendum gives this rule two factors, object and effort; Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual gives eight, which resolve into what the meal must be, what the monk must do, and the one circumstance that excuses him; the meal comes first.16​ 17

    Object

    The Vibhaṅga defines the almsmeal in one sentence, and each clause of it works.18 The food must be one of the five cooked foods, defined at Pc 35; it must be prepared for everyone and not for anyone in particular; there must be as much as one wants; and the place can be anywhere at all. Each condition is also a non-offence: there is no offence if the food is not one of the five, if it is prepared specifically for the monk, or if there is not as much as he needs.19 Thanissaro gives the reason for the last, and it concerns the donors: where the centre fixes each person's portion, owners who were unhappy to feed a monk could give him little or nothing, so the pressure the rule relieves is not there.20 He also draws a consequence from the first: since anything but the five is no offence, a monk who ate none of them at his first meal could take them at a second.21 What such a place is now he answers with the soup kitchen and the homeless shelter, which would fit "if run in this way".22

    Effort

    A monk who is not sick may eat there once. Receiving food beyond that with the intention of eating it is wrong conduct, and every mouthful swallowed is a pācittiya.23 There is no offence if he eats after being invited by the owners, which removes the whole ground of the people's complaint, nor if he eats "while coming or going".24

    Disputed — whether a monk who has eaten at such a place, and left, may come back and eat there the next day.

    The origin story is about monks who stayed, and Thanissaro reads the rule that way: what it forbids is eating there two days running "without leaving in the interim", and "coming or going" means simply leaving and coming back.25 Thanissaro gives the Commentary's stricter reading: it forbids two days running whether or not the monk stays, takes "coming or going" to mean a journey, and even then allows two days running only where floods or robbers prevent him travelling on; Ajahn Brahm's rule agrees with it, allowing a second meal "as long as it is not on consecutive days".26​ 27 Brahmāli reads the Commentary on a journey as allowing a return after a day's travel, and in the Manual leaves the question open: the meaning of "coming and going" is "not clear", and the two commentaries read it in opposite ways.28​ 29 The sources cannot be brought to one answer. I would keep to the stricter reading, as the safer practice; and the origin story recommends, by example, not making a habit of the place at all.

    Circumstance

    The excuse is sickness, and the Vibhaṅga defines it narrowly: a sick monk is one unable to leave the guesthouse.30 It means the incapacity Sāriputta had, which is narrower than ill health in general, though the Manual allows that weakness counts as well, since gilāna "is elsewhere defined very broadly".31 Perception does not mitigate on the severe side: a monk who is not sick commits the full offence whether he thinks himself sick or not. A monk who is sick but thinks he is not, or is unsure, commits wrong conduct.32

    The two sub-stories mark the rule's two edges. The group of six stayed on because it was easier than walking back for alms. Sāriputta, too ill to leave, would not break the new rule: he refused the second day's meal and went hungry.33 And so the rule is aimed at the first, and the Buddha's amendment shows it was never meant to bind the second.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • An offence of wrong conduct for receiving food beyond the first almsmeal with the intention of eating it.34
    • A pācittiya for every mouthful swallowed.35
    • An offence of wrong conduct where the monk is sick but perceives himself as not sick, or is unsure.36

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he is sick; if he is not sick and he eats once; if he eats while coming or going; if he eats after being invited by the owners; if the food is prepared specifically for him; if there is not as much as he needs; if it is anything apart from the five cooked foods; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.37 Thanissaro also reports the Commentary's view that eating at a centre run by one family one day and at another's the next is no offence.38

    Related Offences

    • Pc 32, Pc 33 — the next two rules of the chapter, both about meals taken by invitation; this rule concerns a meal taken by neither invitation nor almsround.
    • Pc 35 — where the five cooked foods that are this rule's object are defined.
    • Pc 29 — the preceding food rule, where the rendering of "cooked food" is discussed, and where perception mitigates because the rule says "knowing"; here it does not.
    • Pc 4 — the perception pattern, which runs here in the severe direction.

    Related Suttas

    • AN 4.28 — contentment "with any kind of almsfood", the virtue that keeps a monk on his almsround, which the group of six left for a table laid out for everyone.39

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, both sub-stories, keeping the group of six's own reasoning and the people's complaint in their words. Ajahn Brahm's retelling has the managers of the hospice complain and the group of six find almsround "uncertain and too much bother", neither of which the Vibhaṅga says, and is not carried. Brahmāli's recitation, the Manual and the class transcript (session 025, 24 May 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1, Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were checked; the class is not cited, since the written sources carry each point it makes. The Commentary is known here only through Thanissaro, Brahmāli's endnote and the Manual. AN 4.28 is quoted from Bodhi's translation.

On scope: "The Commentary, though, maintains that it forbids eating in the center two days running, without making any mention of whether the bhikkhu stays on at the center or not. On "coming or going", that "in the context of the origin story" it "seems to mean that one may take a second meal if one simply leaves the center and then comes back", while the Commentary reads "coming or going on a journey" and allows two days running only "unless there are dangers, such as floods or robbers".
