    Pc 70

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time the novice monastic Kaṇṭaka had the following bad and erroneous view: "As I understand the Teaching of the Buddha, the things he calls obstacles are unable to obstruct one who indulges in them."1​ 2

    A number of monks heard that the novice monastic Kaṇṭaka had that view. They went to see him and asked, "Is it true, Kaṇṭaka, that you have such a view?"

    "Yes, indeed. As I understand the Buddha's Teaching, the things he calls obstacles are unable to obstruct one who indulges in them."

    "No, Kaṇṭaka, don't misrepresent the Buddha, for it's not good to misrepresent him. The Buddha would never say such a thing. The Buddha has given many discourses about the obstacles being obstructive and how they obstruct one who indulges in them. The Buddha has said that the enjoyment provided by worldly pleasures is small, whereas the suffering and trouble with them are huge, and so their drawbacks are greater."3

    But even though the monks corrected Kaṇṭaka like this, he stubbornly held on to that bad and erroneous view, and he continued to insist on it. Since those monks were unable to make him give up that view, they went to the Buddha and told him what had happened.4

    Soon afterwards the Buddha had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned Kaṇṭaka: "Is it true, Kaṇṭaka, that you have such a view?"

    "Yes indeed, venerable sir."

    "Foolish man, who do you think I have taught like this? Haven't I given many discourses about the obstacles being obstructive and how they obstruct one who indulges in them? I have said that the enjoyment provided by worldly pleasures is small, whereas the suffering and trouble with them are huge, and so their drawbacks are greater. I have said that worldly pleasures are similar to a skeleton … similar to a snake's head; the suffering and trouble with them are huge, and so their drawbacks are greater. And yet, foolish man, by misunderstanding you have misrepresented me, hurt yourself, and made much demerit. This will be for your long-lasting harm and suffering. This will affect people's confidence, and cause some to lose it."5

    Having rebuked him, the Buddha gave a teaching and addressed the monks: "Well then, monks, the Sangha should expel the novice monastic Kaṇṭaka. And this is how he should be expelled: 'From today, Kaṇṭaka, you may not refer to the Buddha as your teacher. And, unlike other novices, you can no longer share a sleeping place with the monks for two or three nights. Go! Away with you!'"6

    The Saṅgha then expelled Kaṇṭaka. Soon afterwards the monks from the group of six befriended Kaṇṭaka, and they were attended on by him, lived with him, and shared a sleeping place with him. Yet they knew that he had been expelled.7

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised those monks: "How can the monks from the group of six befriend Kaṇṭaka, and be attended on by him, live with him, and share a sleeping place with him, even though they know that he has been expelled?" They told the Buddha, who questioned them and rebuked them: "Foolish men, how can you do this? This will affect people's confidence."8

    Synopsis — A novice took up Ariṭṭha's view, would not be talked out of it, and was expelled with a formula that stripped him of the Buddha as his teacher and of his right to sleep near the monks; the group of six then took him up as if nothing had happened. The rule that follows is the last of the chapter, and it is the previous rule applied to someone who was never in communion to begin with.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "Also if a novice monastic says, 'As I understand the Buddha's Teaching, the things he calls obstacles are unable to obstruct one who indulges in them,' then the monks should correct him like this: 'No, don't misrepresent the Buddha, for it's not good to misrepresent the Buddha. The Buddha would never say such a thing. In many discourses the Buddha has declared the obstacles to be obstructive and how they obstruct one who indulges in them.' If that novice monastic continues as before, he should be told: 'From today on you may not refer to the Buddha as your teacher. And, unlike other novices, you can no longer share a sleeping place with the monks for two or three nights. Go! Away with you!' If a monk befriends that novice monastic, or he is attended on by him, lives with him, or shares a sleeping place with him, even though he knows that he has been expelled in this way, he commits an offense entailing confession."9

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu supports, is attended on by, shares a meal with, or shares a sleeping place with a novice he knows is currently expelled for holding such a view, he incurs a pācittiya."10

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "And if a novice should say the following: 'As I understand the Dhamma taught by the Blessed One, those acts the Blessed One says are obstructive, when engaged in are not genuine obstructions,' the bhikkhus are to admonish him thus … And should that novice, thus admonished by the bhikkhus, persist as before, the bhikkhus are to admonish him thus: 'From this day forth, friend novice, you are not to claim the Blessed One as your teacher, nor are you even to have the opportunity the other novices get — that of sharing dwellings two or three nights with the bhikkhus. Away with you! Get lost!' Should any bhikkhu knowingly befriend, receive services from, commune with, or lie down in the same dwelling with a novice thus expelled, it is to be confessed."11

    Brahm

    "Eating with, sleeping with, taking care of or being attended on by a novice, knowing that he has been expelled, is an offence of Pācittiya."12

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu knowingly should treat kindly such an expelled novice, or should make [him] attend [to himself], or should eat together with [him], or should use a sleeping place together with [him], [this is a case] involving expiation."13

    The rule's four verbs are Pc 69's three with one dropped and two added. Sambhuñjeyya and saha vā seyyaṁ kappeyya carry over, and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual resolves both by pointing there; saṁvaseyya is gone, since a novice takes part in no formal act; and in its place stand upalāpeyya and upaṭṭhāpeyya.14​ 15 The first is what a mentor does for a pupil, the second what a pupil does for a mentor. Upalāpeti is rendered "befriends" in Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga, "supports" in his recitation, "befriend" in Thanissaro, "treat kindly" in Ñāṇatusita, and the Vibhaṅga defines it by four dealings a mentor has with a pupil.16​ 17 The word is not about warmth but about patronage, and "supports" is the closer English. The definitions are taken up below, under Effort.

    The other word that matters is nāsita, "expelled". Brahmāli renders nāseti with the same English he uses for pārājika, since the two are closely related in the Vinaya.18 Here, though, the expulsion is not the loss of status a pārājika brings, and Object takes up which kind it is.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule protects the Saṅgha's youngest members from a view that misrepresents the Buddha, and keeps the one sanction the Vinaya gives against a novice who holds it from being emptied by the monks around him. The origin story is in two parts. The novice Kaṇṭaka took up Ariṭṭha's view, would not be corrected, and was expelled with a formula: from today he may not claim the Buddha as his teacher, nor share a sleeping place with the monks for two or three nights as other novices may — "Go! Away with you!" Then the group of six befriended him, were attended on by him, and lived and slept with him, knowing he had been expelled.19 Their kindness undid the sentence, and with it the reason for him to let the view go.

    This rule is a lean mirror on two sides at once: its object comes from Pc 68, whose view it quotes, and its effort from Pc 69, whose verbs it repeats, and the Manual makes both referrals.20 What follows is this rule's own. Thanissaro gives three factors: object, an expelled novice who has not given up the view; perception, that one knows it; and effort, one of the four acts.21

    Object

    Thanissaro sorts the word first, from the Commentary. There are three kinds of expulsion: from affiliation, which is the suspension of Pc 69 and applies only to the ordained; from one's status; and as a punishment, the formula spoken to Kaṇṭaka, which is the kind meant here. The novice so expelled loses his teacher and his right to share dwellings with the monks, "although he retains his status as a novice."22

    This expulsion is not the one the Khandhaka's ten grounds bring. Those ten — killing, stealing, unchastity, lying, drinking, disparaging the Three Jewels, holding wrong views, raping a nun — bring expulsion from his status; Thanissaro keeps the two apart, and Pc 70, he says, "covers the second form" — expulsion as punishment.23​ 24

    Disputed — who expels the novice: the Saṅgha, his mentor, or at least two monks.

    The Vibhaṅga prints the formula and nothing about a procedure, though its word-analysis puts the correction, and then the formula, in the mouths of "other monks who see it or hear about it".25 Its origin story names the Saṅgha: "the Sangha should expel the novice monastic Kaṇṭaka … The Sangha then expelled Kaṇṭaka."26 Thanissaro, in BMC1 at this rule, has "the bhikkhus" instruct and then expel him, and in BMC2 makes expulsion, like other punishment, the responsibility of the novice's mentor, allowing another monk to punish him only with the preceptor's permission.27​ 28 The Manual takes the plural, used consistently — the novice is to be addressed bhikkhūhi, "by the monks" — to mean that at least two monks must do it, which "gives novices a certain level of protection in case of erratic behaviour by individual bhikkhus."29 The last two may not be far apart in practice, since a mentor who speaks the words with a second monk satisfies both the Manual and BMC2. The texts do not settle who may do it, and the convergence is my suggestion; the Manual's reason is a good one, that a novice's standing should not hang on the temper of one monk.

    Perception

    The grid is Pc 69's. A novice expelled and perceived as expelled is the pācittiya; if one is unsure, it is an offence of wrong conduct; if one does not perceive the expulsion, there is no offence. A novice not expelled but perceived as expelled, or doubted, is an offence of wrong conduct.30

    Effort

    To befriend him is defined by what a mentor offers: promising him a bowl or a robe, or to recite to him or to test him — the last being a teacher's questioning of a pupil to find out what he knows.31​ 32 Thanissaro's gloss catches the emphasis: overtures made "as a mentor would".33 To be attended on is defined by what a pupil does in the morning: "if he accepts bath powder, soap, a tooth cleaner, or water for rinsing the mouth from him."34 So the offence is committed by letting him hand one a tooth cleaner, which is a fair measure of how completely the rule means to sever the relationship. The other two acts are Pc 69's, with their offence counts, and the question of what ekacchanna covers comes with them; it is taken up at Pc 69.35

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    Of Pc 69's non-offence clauses, the second, the readmission, is missing here, since there is no formal act of the Saṅgha to restore a novice; the clause on giving up the view serves instead. Thanissaro notes that the first clause holds whatever the truth, and that the second, on giving up the view, would seem to as well.36

    The way back is as short as the way out. Thanissaro holds that when the novice gives up the view he is to be reinstated, and reasons that he should take the refuges from his teacher again, since the Commentary does not say how.37 Brahmāli, who called what happens to novices "actually very unclear", gave a simpler answer in class: "You just have to come back, say, I have given up my view, and then you are a novice again."38 The formula of expulsion is the harshest sentence in the chapter — no teacher, no shelter, "Go! Away with you!" — and it is spoken to someone who remains a novice, and who has only to let the view go to be taken back.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — where the novice has been expelled but the monk is unsure of it, and where he has not been expelled but the monk perceives that he has, or is unsure.39
    • Dukkaṭa (Parivāra) — for the effort of befriending, before it is done.40
    • The pācittiya multiplies as at Pc 69: one for each line of Dhamma recited to him, or each syllable; one each time they get up and lie down again.41

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence "if he knows that he has not been expelled; if he knows that he has given up that view; if he is insane; if he is the first offender."42

    Related Offences

    • Pc 68 — refusing to give up a bad view: the source of this rule's object.43
    • Pc 69 — living with an ejected monk: the source of two of this rule's verbs and their counts, and the place where ekacchanna is disputed.44
    • Pc 5 — lying down in the same dwelling as an unordained person for more than three nights: the allowance the expulsion formula withdraws.45
    • Kd 1:60 — the ten grounds for expelling a novice from his status.46

    Related Suttas

    • MN 22 — Ariṭṭha's view, which Kaṇṭaka took up, and the snake grasped the wrong way (see Pc 68).47

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register, in two parts with the expulsion between them; the Buddha's rebuke keeps the first and last of the ten similes with the source's ellipsis, and the interrogations and rebukes are otherwise in the standing summary form. The Manual, the recitation, the class transcript (session 030, 16 July 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1 and BMC2, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were all checked; the Concise BMC condenses BMC1 here. The commentaries are known here only as Thanissaro reports them. Ajahn Brahm's gradations of the ten grounds rest partly on the Vinaya-mukha, which is not used in this book, and are not followed. The class is cited for one practice ruling, how a novice returns. MN 22 is quoted from Bodhi's translation.
