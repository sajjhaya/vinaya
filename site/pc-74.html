    Pc 74

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. The monks from the group of six hit the monks from the group of seventeen in anger. They cried. Other monks asked them why, and they told them what had happened.1

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised them, "How could the monks from the group of six hit other monks in anger?" The Buddha questioned them, established that it was true, rebuked them, and stated the rule.2

    Synopsis — The monks of the group of six hit the monks of the group of seventeen in anger, and they cried. The rule that follows makes a blow in anger an offence whether or not it injures.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk hits a monk in anger, he commits an offense entailing confession."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu, motivated by anger, strikes a bhikkhu, he incurs a pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu, angered and displeased, give a blow to (another) bhikkhu, it is to be confessed."5

    Brahm

    "Being angry at a monk, deliberately giving him a blow is an offence of Pācittiya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu who is resentful [and] displeased should give a blow to a bhikkhu, [this is a case] involving expiation."7

    The renderings agree on the offence and differ only in how they give its verb. The Pāli is pahāraṁ dadeyya, "should give a blow": Thanissaro, Ajahn Brahm and Ñāṇatusita keep the noun, while Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga has "hits" and his recitation "strikes".8 Either word serves, though the noun is worth keeping, for the word-analysis goes on to ask with what the blow was given and never how hard. The intention clause, kupito anattamano, "angered and displeased", stands word for word in the rule-text of three other rules — NP 25, Pc 17 and Pc 75 — and it is at NP 25, where the anger is what the rule turns on, that its threshold is argued.9 That point is taken up below, under Intention.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule guards a monk's restraint of his own anger, at the point where anger reaches for the body, and with it the ease of monks who must live beside one another without fear. The Story's whole effect is that the monks of the group of seventeen cried. Nothing is said of injury, and the rule does not ask for any: what it grades is the mind behind the blow and the person it lands on, not the damage it does. The Buddha's rebuke adds the people's confidence, which does not survive the sight of monks hitting monks.

    Thanissaro gives the offence three factors: object, effort and intention.10

    Object

    The monk struck must be fully ordained. A blow in anger to anyone else is an offence of wrong conduct, and perception makes no difference in either direction: a monk who strikes a fellow monk while unsure of his ordination, or while taking him for unordained, still commits the full offence.11 Thanissaro refers the pattern to Pc 42.12

    Disputed — whether anupasampanna, "not fully ordained", reaches animals.

    The Commentary says it does, and Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm both report the reading without demur, so that a blow in anger to an animal is an offence of wrong conduct.13​ 14 Brahmāli rejects it: "non-ordained, in my understanding, must mean a person, it cannot mean an animal."15 The Vibhaṅga settles it for neither side. It defines the word four times, at Pc 4, Pc 5, Pc 8 and Pc 9, and each time as "the remainder" once the fully ordained are set aside, with no noun at all; the word "person" in Brahmāli's own rendering of those definitions is his translation's and not the Pāli's.16 Against that, a remainder can be narrowed by its context, and at Pc 4, Pc 8 and Pc 9 — teaching, claiming, telling — the context is one of persons. Something practical turns on it: on the Commentary's reading a dog struck in anger is an offence of wrong conduct, and on Brahmāli's it falls under no Pātimokkha rule short of killing, which is Pc 61. I do not think the texts settle it either way, and the four definitions show only that the Pāli leaves the question open, not which answer is right; a monk who strikes no creature in anger is safe on both.

    Effort

    The word-analysis gives three ways of giving a blow: with the body, with anything connected to the body, or with anything released, "even if just with a water-lily leaf".17 Thanissaro fills them out — a fist, an elbow or a foot; a stick or a knife in the hand; a thrown rock, an arrow or a gun — and reads the leaf as the rule's own statement that the blow "need not be painful".18 And so the scale runs from a leaf to a gun with no threshold along it. What of a hold that is not a blow — an arm twisted behind the back, a hand at the throat? Thanissaro notes that such acts are not mentioned under the rule, but that grabbing the arm or the neck beforehand "would fulfill the factor of effort"; Ajahn Brahm reaches the same penalty by analogy, holding that to cause pain in anger in some way other than a blow "seems to deserve the same penalty".19​ 20 On either reading, a monk who seizes another in anger to twist his arm answers at this rule's level.

    Intention

    The blow must be struck in anger. How little anger will do is argued at NP 25, whose rule carries the same two words and depends on nothing else. A blow struck for any other reason is outside the rule. Thanissaro's example is a thump on the back to dislodge something caught in a fellow monk's throat; Ajahn Brahm's are pushing someone out of the path of an oncoming vehicle and slapping a friend on the back to end his hiccups.21​ 22 One other motive moves the case to a different rule: a monk who strikes a woman from lust commits, on the Commentary's reading that Thanissaro gives, a saṅghādisesa under Sg 2.23

    Because the rule grades the mind and not the damage, what the blow does is beside the point. Whether the other monk is hurt, or how badly, does not affect the offence, and Thanissaro draws the hard consequence: if one means only to hurt and the victim happens to die of the blow, the case is treated under this rule, not under Pj 3.24 The Vinaya deals with the wish to kill under Pj 3 and does not deal with it again here; what is left for this rule is the temper, and a monk who breaks another's head in it answers at the same level as one who flicks him with a leaf. Ajahn Brahm states the boundary from the other side: a blow given with intent to kill may fall under Pj 3, Pc 61 or this rule, whichever carries the heavier penalty.25

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The exemption is wider than its English suggests. The Pāli, kenaci viheṭhīyamāno mokkhādhippāyo, is "being harassed by someone, aiming at release". Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga compresses it to "if he hits in self-defense", while his Pātimokkha Manual keeps the shape: "with the purpose of freeing oneself when oppressed".26​ 27​ 28 The order matters. The exemption is release, and self-defence is one case of it — not the other way round, which is what "self-defense" alone would leave a reader with. Thanissaro reports the Commentary as reading the clause to include what we would now call self-defence.29 A monk defending himself may well be angry, and that does not bring the offence back. Thanissaro reads this from the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī's analysis of the factors: even if anger or displeasure arises in such a case, "there is no penalty".30

    A nun is not a monk, and on three of the Vibhaṅga's four definitions she is not "not fully ordained" either; Thanissaro's note at Sg 2 puts a blow in anger to "a woman---or anyone else" under this rule; he does not address a nun, and the four definitions leave her case unclear.31 The reverse case did arise, and it got a ruling outside the Pātimokkha: a nun knocked a weak monk down in the street with her shoulder, and the Buddha made it an offence of wrong conduct for a nun to hit a monk.32 Brahmāli draws the lesson. The rule was laid down for the incident that arose, and a monk who struck a nun would do something just as bad, rule or no rule; the Vinaya answers the cases that came before the Buddha, and "the Vinaya itself is not enough to make sure you are a moral person."33

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — giving a blow in anger to anyone not fully ordained, however he is perceived.34
    • Dukkaṭa (Parivāra) — for the effort, while the blow is being given; the pācittiya follows when it lands.35
    • Dukkaṭa (the Commentary, in Thanissaro) — cutting off a fellow monk's nose or ear in order to disfigure him. Thanissaro, following the Vinaya-mukha, finds "no basis in the Vibhaṅga or in reason" for the lighter penalty, since the act is hard to imagine without anger and the cut is a blow with something connected to the body.36
    • Saṅghādisesa (the Commentary, in Thanissaro) — striking a woman from lust: Sg 2, not this rule.37
    • Dukkaṭa (Khandhaka) — for a nun who strikes a monk; Kd 20, a ruling outside the Pātimokkha.38

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if, being harassed by someone, he gives a blow aiming at release; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.39

    Related Offences

    • Pc 75 — raising a hand in anger against a monk: the same story, intention, object and exemption, differing only in the effort. This rule is its anchor.
    • Sg 2 — lustful bodily contact with a woman: where the blow is itself the lustful contact, the offence is that one.
    • Pj 3 — killing a human being: the boundary this rule sits under. A blow meant to hurt that kills is this rule; a blow meant to kill is that one.
    • Pc 61 — killing a living being: the boundary on the animal side.
    • NP 25, Pc 17 — snatching back a robe in anger, and evicting a monk from a dwelling in anger. With this rule and Pc 75, the rules whose text carries kupito anattamano; the anger is argued at NP 25.
    • Pc 52, 55 — tickling a monk, and frightening one. With this rule, the Pācittiya rules of physical contact and intimidation, distinguished by motive: fun, fear and anger.
    • Pc 42 — where Thanissaro sets out the principle that perception of the other's ordination does not mitigate.
    • Pc 17, 37, 52, 55, 60, 75, 77 — with this rule, the rules generated by the group of six's dealings with the group of seventeen, whose own ordination under twenty generates Pc 65. Not all of it is quarrelling: Pc 52's tickling and Pc 60's hidden bowls are horseplay that turned out badly, the first of them fatally.

    Related Suttas

    • MN 21 — not even bandits sawing a monk limb from limb justify a mind of hate: the mind this rule grades, rather than the injury, and its exemption looks to the aim, release, not to whether anger arose.40
    • Dhp 3–5 — "They abused me, they hurt me": the grievance that keeps hostility alive, and the non-hostility that alone ends it.41

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register, with the interrogation, rebuke and ruling given in the standing summary form. The Manual, the recitation, the class (session 031, 23 July 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1 and BMC2, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were all checked; the Concise BMC condenses BMC1 here, the Commentary's animal reading included, and adds nothing of its own. Ajahn Brahm's Story has the group of six ordering the seventeen "to do this and do that for them" and striking them when they did not obey; the orders are not the Vibhaṅga's and are left out of the Story. The Commentary and the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī are given as Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm report them. The Vinaya-mukha is known here only through Thanissaro's report of it, and Ajahn Brahm's two citations of it are reported as his. MN 21 is quoted from Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation, and Dhp 3–5 from Bhikkhu Suddhāso's.

For some reason, the Commentary says that if one cuts off the nose or ear of a fellow bhikkhu in order to disfigure him, one incurs only a dukkaṭa. As the Vinaya-mukha points out, though, there is no basis in the Vibhaṅga or in reason for this statement. It is hard to imagine anyone doing this unless impelled by anger, and the act of cutting another person would come under the factor of giving a blow with something connected with the body. … If one intends simply to hurt the other person, but he/she happens to die from one's blow, the case is treated under this rule, rather than under Pr 3."↩︎
