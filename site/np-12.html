    NP 12

    Story (Vatthu)

    While the Buddha was staying in the house with the peaked roof in the Great Wood near Vesālī, the monks from the group of six were having blankets made entirely of black wool. People who were walking about the dwellings complained and criticised them, "How could the Sakyan monastics have blankets made entirely of black wool? They're just like householders who indulge in worldly pleasures!"

    The monks heard the complaints of those people, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised those monks, "How could the monks from the group of six have blankets made entirely of black wool?" After rebuking those monks in many ways, they told the Buddha. He had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned the monks: "Is it true, monks, that you do this?" "It's true, sir." The Buddha rebuked them: "Foolish men, how could you do this? This will affect people's confidence …" And he laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — the group of six have blankets made entirely of black wool; people walking about the dwellings complain that the monks are like householders who indulge in worldly pleasures; the Buddha prohibits having a blanket made entirely of black wool.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk has a blanket made entirely of black wool, he commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu makes a blanket entirely of black wool, or has one made, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu have a felt (blanket/rug) made of pure black wool, it is to be forfeited and confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Having a santhata made entirely of black wool is a nissaggiya pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should have a rug made of pure black sheep's wool; [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture."6

    The renderings differ only as they did at NP 11, over santhata, which is discussed there. Ñāṇatusita alone names the animal, "sheep's wool", for the Pāli eḷakaloma; the rule does not depend on it.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    At stake here is a monk's contentment with plain things, which the laity can read from the colour of what he sits on. The people who walked about the dwellings saw the all-black blankets and drew the conclusion at once: "They're just like householders who indulge in worldly pleasures!"7 Thanissaro takes it that a pure black felt was stylish at the time, and observes that the Vinaya returns to the point again and again: stylish, luxurious or elegant articles do not fit a bhikkhu's way of life.8 Where NP 11's silk cost the lives of silkworms, the black wool here cost only an appearance, and the Buddha's rebuke shows that the appearance mattered: "This will affect people's confidence."9

    Everything but the object is NP 11's: the santhata and what counts as one today, the effort through every way of finishing, the result on getting it, the relinquishment, and the derived offences.10​ 11​ 12

    Object

    A santhata made entirely of black wool. The Vibhaṅga allows two kinds of black: "natural black and dyed black."13 The wool is sheep's wool, on Ñāṇatusita's reading of eḷakaloma, or a sheep's or a goat's in Ajahn Brahm's.14​ 15 A santhata with any wool of another colour in it is not "entirely" black and falls outside this rule, though not outside NP 13, which sets how much black it may hold.

    As at NP 11, the Vibhaṅga gives wrong conduct both for making one for someone else and for using one someone else has made, and Ajahn Brahm draws the same conclusion: an all-black santhata is unallowable for any monk, and even the offender who has relinquished it, confessed and received it back may not use it, but would give it away, discard it, or shred it for other uses.16​ 17 The non-offence clause is NP 11's, so black wool made into a canopy, a floor cover, a screen, a mattress or a pillow is no offence.18

    The people who walked about the dwellings judged the monks by their blankets. What a monk sits on is seen, and it speaks for him.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — for the effort of making an all-black santhata, or having it made, before it is got.19
    • Dukkaṭa — making one, or having one made, for someone else.20
    • Dukkaṭa — using one that someone else has made.21

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he makes a canopy, a floor cover, a cloth screen, a mattress, or a pillow; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.22

    Related Offences

    • NP 11 — the anchor of NP 11–15, where the santhata and the shared factors are set out.
    • NP 13 — the proportion of black wool a santhata may hold.
    • NP 16–17 — the chapter's rules on raw wool.

    Related Suttas

    • DN 1 §1.15 — the luxurious furnishings the ascetic Gotama is praised for refraining from, among them "fleecy or variegated coverlets" (see NP 11); the people read the same kind of comfort in an all-black blanket.23

    A note on sourcing

    The Story, definitions, permutations and non-offence clauses are from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation, and the shared machinery is carried from NP 11. Thanissaro's BMC1 gives the reading of black wool as stylish, Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes the animal and the unallowability of the all-black santhata, and Ñāṇatusita the reading of eḷakaloma as sheep's wool. The Concise BMC adds nothing, and the class on this rule is read but not cited. DN 1 is quoted from Walshe's translation.
