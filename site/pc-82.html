    Pc 82

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. An association had prepared a meal together with robe-cloth for the Saṅgha. They said, "After giving the food, we will offer the robe-cloth."1

    But the monks from the group of six went to that association and said, "Please give the robe-cloth to these monks."2

    "Venerables, we cannot do that. We have prepared our annual alms-offering together with robe-cloth for the Saṅgha."3

    "The Saṅgha has many donors and supporters. But since these monks are staying here, they are looking to you for support. If you do not give to them, who will? So give them the robe-cloth."4

    Being pressured by the monks from the group of six, that association gave the prepared robe-cloth to those monks and served the food to the Saṅgha.5

    The monks who knew that a meal together with robe-cloth had been prepared for the Saṅgha, but not that the robe-cloth had been given to those monks from the group of six, said, "You may dedicate the robe-cloth."6

    "There is not any. The robe-cloth we had prepared was diverted by the monks from the group of six."7

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised those monks, "How could the monks from the group of six divert to an individual things they knew were intended for the Saṅgha?" The Buddha questioned them, established that it was true, rebuked them, and stated the rule.8

    Synopsis — The same guild, the same annual offering and the same pressure as at NP 30, and the only change is who was asked for: for themselves there, for their fellows here — though the cloth, in the same words, reaches the group of six in both. The Saṅgha still got its meal, and found out what it had lost only when it tried to hand over the robes.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk diverts to an individual material support that he knows was intended for the Sangha, he commits an offense entailing confession."9

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu diverts to an individual an offering he knows was intended for the Saṅgha, he incurs a pācittiya."10

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu knowingly divert to an individual gains that had been allocated for a Community, it is to be confessed."11

    Brahm

    "Thinking that an offering is meant for the Sangha, causing it to be given to someone else is an offence of Pācittiya."12

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should knowingly allocate [already] allocated gain belonging to the community to a [lay-] person, [this is a case] involving expiation."13

    The renderings agree closely, and where they part it is over pariṇāmeti. Three of the five have "divert"; Ñāṇatusita has "allocate", and so must render the participle pariṇata by the same word, "allocate [already] allocated gain".14 His choice keeps what the Pāli is doing: what the donors have done and what the monk then does are the same act, bending a gift towards someone, done twice in different directions. Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga gives the participle as "intended", which is the most readable and hides the repetition.15 Ajahn Brahm's line alone makes the offence turn on the monk's belief, "Thinking that an offering is meant for the Sangha", and he adds "irrespective of the actual intention of the donor"; the Vibhaṅga's grid agrees that his belief decides whether he offends, though not quite what he offends, for where the gift was not in fact intended for the Saṅgha the offence is a lesser one.16​ 17 That grid is taken up below, under Perception.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A gift that donors have set aside for the Saṅgha is not a monk's to redirect, and the rule guards him from making himself the broker of other people's generosity, and the donors' confidence that what they meant for the Saṅgha will reach it. In the Story a guild had prepared its annual meal and robe-cloth for the Saṅgha. The group of six asked it to give the cloth not to themselves but to "these monks", who depended on the guild, and the guild, pressed, gave the cloth to the group of six and the food to the Saṅgha, which learnt what it had lost only when it asked for the robes.18 Thanissaro gives the reason for this rule and NP 30 together, from the Aṅguttara: one who prevents a gift from reaching its intended recipient creates three obstacles, to the donor's merit, to the recipient's gain, and to himself.19​ 20

    This is a lean rule. Its parent is NP 30, where the monk diverts the gift to himself, and Thanissaro's chapter here is a pointer to it.21 The factors, the perception variants between the editions, the diversions between Saṅghas, shrines and individuals, and the donor's freedom are treated there.22 Ajahn Brahm reads the Story as that rule's sequel: barred by NP 30 from asking for themselves, some of the group asked for others of the group.23 What is this rule's own is that the gift goes to someone else, so that there is nothing for the monk to forfeit and the offence is a plain pācittiya.

    Object

    A gift is intended for the Saṅgha, the Vibhaṅga says, when the donors have said "We'll give" or "We'll prepare".24 Thanissaro widens it to words or a gesture, and reports the Commentary's point that the donors may be anyone, lay people in general, fellow monks and relatives, "even one's own mother".25 Ajahn Brahm marks where the rule ends. It concerns offerings that have not yet become the Saṅgha's possession: those still to be given, and those already handed to a monk on the Saṅgha's behalf but not yet passed to the officer who looks after such things.26 The individual it is diverted to may be anyone, a monk, a novice or a lay person.27

    Perception

    The grid is the ordinary three: a gift intended for the Saṅgha and perceived as such, the pācittiya; one the monk is unsure of, an offence of wrong conduct; one he does not perceive as intended, no offence. Where the gift was not intended for the Saṅgha but he perceives or suspects that it was, the offence is one of wrong conduct.28 The editions agree here, as they do not at NP 30, and Thanissaro uses this rule's grid to settle that one's.29

    Effort

    The effort is whatever causes the donor to give to an individual instead of the Saṅgha.30 The Parivāra grades it: an offence of wrong conduct while he is diverting it, the pācittiya when it is diverted.31

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The exemption is a script. Asked "Where may we give?", a monk may answer: "Give where your gift will be useful," "… where it goes toward repairs," "… where it will last for a long time," or "… where you feel inspired."32 Not one of the four answers names a recipient, and the last, Thanissaro notes, echoes the Buddha's answer to King Pasenadi: "Wherever one's mind has confidence, great king."33 He draws the rule for a monk's speech from it: asked where to give, he may be no more specific than that; "When not asked, he has no business at all telling people where they should give their gifts, regardless of how noble his motives may seem in his eyes."34

    How easily a good motive does it is shown by a case Brahmāli recalled from a few years before. A lay person came to offer something to Ajahn Brahm, who told them to give it to Ajahn Brahmāli instead, knowing he would like it. Brahmāli judged it against the rule: the gift was coming to the Saṅgha through Ajahn Brahm, and once it was the Saṅgha's, passing it on was for the Saṅgha to agree, "not up to him to decide anymore". It was, he said, "just coming from kindness", and still not quite appropriate.35 No cell of the grid asks about motive, and a monk who catches himself steering a gift towards a friend, however kindly, has met this rule.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — where the gift was intended for the Saṅgha and the monk was unsure of it; and where it was not intended for the Saṅgha and he perceived that it was, or was unsure.36
    • Dukkaṭa — diverting a gift intended for one Saṅgha to another Saṅgha or a shrine; one intended for a shrine to another shrine, a Saṅgha or an individual; one intended for an individual to another individual, a Saṅgha or a shrine. For these, Thanissaro says, there is no preliminary offence for the effort; the offence falls only when the gift is received.37​ 38
    • Dukkaṭa (Parivāra) — for the effort, while diverting; the full offence when the diverting is done.39

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence "if being asked, 'Where may we give?' he says, 'Give where your gift will be useful,' '… where it goes toward repairs,' '… where it will last for a long time,' '… where you feel inspired;'" nor if he is insane, or the first offender.40

    Related Offences

    • NP 30 — knowingly diverting to oneself: the parent rule, whose factors, perception variants, cross-diversions and treatment of the donor's freedom this one shares.41
    • Pc 81 — complaining after a united Saṅgha has given robe-cloth: the chapter's other rule about the Saṅgha's gains. Where this one protects an intended gift, that one protects a completed distribution.
    • Pj 2 — taking what is not given, where Thanissaro notes that the Vibhaṅga mentions shrine property at NP 30 and at this rule but not there.42

    Related Suttas

    • MN 142 — no gift to an individual is "ever more fruitful than an offering made to the Sangha", which a monk who turns the Saṅgha's gift to individuals undoes.43
    • AN 3.57 — one who prevents a gift creates an obstruction for three, the donor's merit, the recipients' gain and himself, which Thanissaro gives as the reason for this rule and NP 30 together.44​ 45
    • SN 3.24 — asked where a gift should be given, the Buddha answers, "Wherever one's mind has confidence", which Thanissaro likens to the rule's exemption, "Give … where you feel inspired": the furthest a monk who is asked may go.46​ 47
