    Pc 3

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī, in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time the monks from the group of six were carrying tales between monks who were arguing. After hearing something on one side they reported it to the other, and after hearing something on that side they reported it back, in order to create division between them. In this way they started new quarrels and made existing ones worse. The monks of few desires complained and criticised them: "How can the monks from the group of six engage in malicious talebearing between monks who are arguing? How can they report to one side what they have heard on the other side, and vice versa, in order to create division, and in this way start new quarrels and make existing quarrels worse?" After rebuking them in many ways, they told the Buddha. Soon afterwards he had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned the monks: "Is it true, monks, that you do this?"

    "It's true, sir."

    The Buddha rebuked them: "Foolish men, how can you do this? This will affect people's confidence." Then he laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — The group of six carry reports back and forth between quarrelling monks to set them further at odds, and the rule against malicious tale-bearing follows.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk engages in malicious talebearing between monks, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "Malicious tale-bearing about a bhikkhu is a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Divisive tale-bearing among bhikkhus is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Malicious tale bearing is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "In the backbiting of a bhikkhu, [there is a case] involving expiation."6

    The renderings agree on the act, and the word that matters is one none of their rule lines uses, though Ñāṇatusita's title keeps it. Pesuñña has often been translated "slander", which implies a falsehood, but the Vibhaṅga's examples are all true reports: the tale-bearer passes on what really was said.7 Brahmāli's full rendering, "between monks", follows his endnote, which takes from the exposition that the offence needs a monk's words carried to another monk; to whom they must be carried is discussed below, under Effort.8

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A monk who carries tales becomes the channel by which quarrels spread, and this rule closes the channel, for the concord of the Saṅgha and the confidence of those who see it. The group of six did nothing but repeat what they had heard, and the Story shows what that alone could do: they "started new quarrels and made existing ones worse", and the Buddha's rebuke turns to what the quarrels would cost in the confidence of others.9 The rule borrows its matter from the two before it. What is carried is an insult of the kind Pc 2 defines, and a tale made up rather than carried is the lie of Pc 1. Its own contribution is the act of delivering a real insult to someone else, to drive a wedge or to win favour.

    Thanissaro gives the offence three factors, object, effort and intent, and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual names the same three in its own terms: having heard one monk abuse another, bringing it to the victim's attention, and doing so to ingratiate oneself or cause division.10​ 11 The two part on the second, to whom the tale is carried, and Thanissaro's factors serve here, with that question set out where it arises.

    Object

    For the full offence the monk whose words are carried and the one they are carried to must both be fully ordained, and the words must be a direct insult on one of Pc 2's ten topics, or would have been had they been said to the victim's face.12 If either of them, or both, is not fully ordained, the offence is a dukkaṭa.13 So too if the words carried were only an indirect insult, "There are outcasts right here", whoever is ordained.14​ 15 Where the tale is carried to someone other than the victim, Thanissaro holds that the victim need not be a monk.16

    Effort

    The tale-bearer reports the remark, by word or by gesture, as in a letter, and the report is a true one.17​ 18 That is what separates this rule from Pc 1: where a monk invents part or all of the tale, Ajahn Brahm notes, there is also a pācittiya for lying.19 What of a monk who has someone else carry the tale for him? Brahmāli thinks he is probably caught, as part of the sequence, as one who orders a killing is caught at Pj 3.20

    Disputed — whether the tale must be carried to the victim of the insult, or may be carried to anyone.

    The Vibhaṅga's examples all take one shape: the tale-bearer tells a monk, "So-and-so says this about you."21 The Manual holds the offence to that case. The rule concerns ingratiation and division, and "a bhikkhu who is not the victim of the abuse is unlikely to care much about what someone has said about someone else"; Brahmāli reads the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī the same way.22 Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm read the rule more widely. Thanissaro's tale-bearer may tell "either W or Y himself", a friend of the victim as readily as the victim, in hopes of a rift between that listener and the speaker, and Ajahn Brahm's worked cases count the report to any other listener as the full offence; since he grades the offence by whether the speaker and the victim are ordained, and not the listener, he departs from the Vibhaṅga on that point.23​ 24 The examples favour the narrower reading, and the rule's stated aim, division, seems to reach further than the victim. That balance is my own, and nothing in the texts weighs the two; on either reading a monk who carries an insult to its victim, to win favour or to divide, has done what the rule forbids.

    Intention

    The tale must be carried to win the listener's favour or to cause a rift between him and the speaker; the Vibhaṅga names the two, "for one wanting to endear himself and for one aiming at division", and exempts one who wants neither.25​ 26 As at Pc 2, the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī would make it a factor that the listener understands, and Thanissaro finds no basis for it in the Vibhaṅga.27 A true report carried for another reason is therefore no offence, and Thanissaro's examples show the reasons a monk will meet: telling a senior that one monk has accused another of a serious offence, so that an inquiry can be made for the sake of harmony, or telling a teacher that his student is making insulting remarks, so that he can stop it.28 The same words that would divide, carried to someone who can settle the matter, serve the concord this rule protects.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • A dukkaṭa where the monk heard from, or the one told, or both, is not fully ordained.29
    • A dukkaṭa where the words carried were only an indirect insult under Pc 2, whoever is ordained.30
    • A dukkaṭa, by the Sub-commentary in Thanissaro's report, for carrying tales about matters other than the ten topics of abuse to put a monk in a bad light, though some such cases fall under Pc 13.31

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence for one who neither wants to endear himself nor aims at division, nor for one who is insane, nor for the first offender.32

    Related Offences

    • Pc 1 — where an invented tale is handled: false tale-bearing is the deliberate lie of Pc 1.33
    • Pc 2 — the source of the ten topics of abuse that fix what counts as the insult carried here, and the rule for making such an insult oneself.
    • Pc 9 — telling an unordained person of a bhikkhu's serious offence without the Saṅgha's authorisation, a neighbouring restriction on damaging speech about a fellow monk.
    • Pc 13 — criticising a Community official, and the home of some tale-bearing about matters outside the ten topics of abuse.

    Related Suttas

    • AN 10.176 — divisive speech, the tale-bearer's fault, given up for its opposite: "Thus he is one who reunites those who are divided, a promoter of unity, who enjoys concord, rejoices in concord, delights in concord, a speaker of words that promote concord."34
