    NP 26

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Rājagaha in the Bamboo Grove. At that time the monks from the group of six were making robes, and they asked for a large amount of thread. But when their robes were finished, there was much thread left over. They said, "Well, let's ask for even more thread and get weavers to weave us robe-cloth." Yet even when that robe-cloth had been woven, there was much thread left over. A second time they asked for more thread and had weavers weave them robe-cloth. Once again there was much thread left over. A third time they asked for more thread and had weavers weave them robe-cloth.

    People complained and criticised them: "How could the Sakyan monastics ask for thread and then get weavers to weave them robe-cloth?"

    The monks heard the complaints of those people, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised those monks: "How could the monks from the group of six ask for thread and then get weavers to weave them robe-cloth?"

    After rebuking those monks in many ways, they told the Buddha. Soon afterwards he had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned those monks: "Is it true, monks, that you did this?"

    "It's true, sir."

    The Buddha rebuked them — "Foolish men, how could you do this? This will affect people's confidence" — and laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — The group of six ask for thread to make their robes, and three times over they ask for more and have weavers weave robe-cloth from it; people criticise them, and the Buddha makes having robe-cloth woven from thread one has asked for an offence entailing relinquishment.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk himself asks for thread, and then has weavers weave him robe-cloth, he commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu asks for thread and then has robe-cloth woven with it, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu, having requested thread, have robe-cloth woven by weavers, it is to be forfeited and confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Receiving robe-material, having had it woven from thread obtained by asking laypeople, who were not relations and who had not given invitation to ask, is a nissaggiya pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu, having himself requested the thread [to be used], should have a robe-cloth woven by cloth-weavers, [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture."6

    The renderings agree. Brahmāli's recitation leaves out the weavers, tantavāyehi.7 Ajahn Brahm builds the exemptions for relatives and those who have invited into the rule itself, and puts the offence at the receiving. Ñāṇatusita notes that cīvara here, as in the next rule, is robe-cloth and not a finished robe, since weavers make cloth and do not sew it, a reading that bears on how small a cloth comes under the rule, discussed below under Effort.8

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A surplus tempts a monk to ask again, and the rule stops him there. The group of six began with a need, thread to sew their robes, and each time thread was left over they took the remainder as a reason to ask for more, three rounds of it, until people asked how the Sakyan monastics could "ask for thread and then get weavers to weave them robe-cloth".9 The Buddha's rebuke names the cost, "This will affect people's confidence", and the rule leaves the first asking free, for thread to sew a robe is exempt, and falls on the asking that the surplus prompted.

    The offence has three factors in Thanissaro's division: object, effort and result.10 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual lists six, setting out the two exceptions and the robe's being for oneself on their own; I take Thanissaro's, with the robe's being for oneself under Result.11

    Object

    Thread of the six kinds, linen, cotton, silk, wool, sunn hemp and hemp, that the monk himself has asked for.12 The exemptions shape the factor: thread asked for to sew a robe, and thread from relatives or those who have invited him to ask, do not fulfil it, so it is thread asked for to make robe-cloth from people he may not ask.13 The Commentary reads the sewing exemption as allowing thread for sewing to be asked from anyone, and Thanissaro agrees from the clause's grammar; Ñāṇatusita gives the practical sense, since strong thread or thread of a matching colour may be needed.14​ 15 What the improper asking costs before any weaving, the texts do not say. Thanissaro suggests that it would perhaps be a dukkaṭa under the Khandhaka's catch-all rule on misbehaviour, and Ajahn Brahm states the dukkaṭa outright.16​ 17

    Effort

    The monk has weavers weave robe-cloth from the thread, weavers who are not his relatives and have not offered their services.18 When does the lesser offence fall? The Vibhaṅga puts it in the causative, "if he has it woven by weavers, then for every effort there is an act of wrong conduct", and in Thanissaro's report the Commentary reads the efforts as the weavers': from their first move toward the cloth, each one is a dukkaṭa for the monk.19​ 20 Thanissaro accepts that timing, because the Vibhaṅga to the next rule states it outright: a request no one acts on carries nothing. He takes it, tentatively, as the method for every rule in which a monk improperly gets something made, NP 11–15 and Pc 86–92: no offence in the request, a dukkaṭa when the maker acts on it, except where the Vibhaṅga puts the offence in the request itself, as at Pc 26.21 He would allow only one dukkaṭa however many efforts the weavers make, on the pattern of rules where one request carries one offence. The Vibhaṅga's "for every effort" seems to count each effort, and on the number the text sits more easily with the Commentary.22 Where only one of the two is improper, the thread or the weavers, the Commentary sets the penalty at a dukkaṭa on receiving the cloth, and where both are proper there is none; it then works through mixed warps and woofs and alternating strands, which, Thanissaro remarks, "if nothing else provides an insight into the commentators' minds."23

    Disputed — whether the rule is broken by any woven piece the size of the smallest cloth that can be assigned, or only by a wearable robe.

    The Vibhaṅga gives no minimum. In Thanissaro's report the Commentary takes the four by eight fingerbreadths of the other robe-cloth rules, and Ajahn Brahm gives the rule's robe-material the same lower limit, in his figures "longer than 8 inches, wider than 4 inches".24​ 25 Thanissaro answers that several of the things the exemptions allow, a belt, a bowl bag, a water filter, are larger than that, which sits awkwardly with so small a trigger, and he prefers to read cīvara as the Commentary itself reads it at Pc 58, where the Vibhaṅga is likewise silent on size: "the penalty is for getting the weavers to weave a wearable robe."26 Against him, we note, the next rule's Vibhaṅga defines robe-cloth by exactly the assignable minimum, a point he does not take up.27 The texts do not settle which the rule means, and the gap in practice, a small cloth woven from begged thread, is narrow; a monk who treats any piece of assignable size as within the rule is safe on both readings.

    Result

    The monk obtains the cloth, and it is for himself.28​ 29 The exemption "for the benefit of someone else" makes the same point from the other side: having cloth woven for another monk is outside the rule, provided the thread and the weavers come from those who may be asked for him, one's own relatives and inviters or his, as at NP 6.30​ 31 The offence falls when the cloth is received.32​ 33 The Commentary would count the cloth obtained once a piece of the smallest assignable size is woven, with a further offence for each further piece; Thanissaro sets against the first the Khandhaka's principle, met at NP 21, that cloth counts from when it reaches one's hand, and against the second the Vibhaṅga's single offence however large the cloth.34 Perception does not excuse: cloth woven at one's request brings the full offence whether the monk knows it, doubts it, or thinks otherwise, and cloth not so woven but taken to be is wrong conduct.35

    Left-over thread was the group of six's reason to ask again; the rule makes it the point at which to stop.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — each effort the weavers make on the monk's improper request, before he receives the cloth (one dukkaṭa only, on Thanissaro's reading).36​ 37
    • Dukkaṭa — receiving the cloth where one of the two, the thread or the weavers, is improper and the other proper (Commentary).38
    • Dukkaṭa — receiving cloth not woven at one's request while perceiving that it was, or unsure.39
    • Dukkaṭa — the improper request for thread itself: suggested by Thanissaro under the rule on misbehaviour, stated by Ajahn Brahm.40​ 41

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if it is to sew a robe; if it is for a back-and-knee strap, a belt, a shoulder strap, a bowl bag, or a water filter; if it is from relatives or those who have given an invitation; if it is for the benefit of someone else; if it is by means of one's own property; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.42 The back-and-knee strap is a cloth tied round the back and knees to support a squatting posture.43 In Thanissaro's report the Commentary reads the small items as allowing thread to be asked for them but not woven; Thanissaro, from the grammar of the clauses, reads them as allowing the weaving too, and Ajahn Brahm writes of thread "to be woven (or crocheted) into a bowl-bag".44​ 45 With one's own property, paying for thread and weavers, there is no offence here; when the ten days of NP 1 begin for cloth not yet in hand is the question NP 21 treats for a bowl.46

    Related Offences

    • NP 27 — the companion weaver rule: there the thread is the donor's and the offence is having the cloth improved; it states the timing of the dukkaṭa that this rule borrows.
    • NP 6 — the anchor for asking non-relatives, and for asking on another's behalf.
    • NP 11–15, Pc 86–92 — the rules on getting things made, which share this rule's reading of the causative: no offence in the request, a dukkaṭa when the maker complies.
    • Pc 26 — the stated exception: having a robe sewn for an unrelated nun, where the Vibhaṅga puts the offence in the request.
