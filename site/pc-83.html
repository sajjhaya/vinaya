    Pc 83

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. King Pasenadi of Kosala told his park-keeper, "Go and clean up the park; I will be going there."1

    "Yes, sir." While cleaning the park, he saw the Buddha seated at the foot of a tree. He then went to King Pasenadi and said, "The park is clean, sir, but the Buddha is sitting there."

    "Marvellous! I will visit him."

    The king went to the park and approached the Buddha, but just then a lay follower was seated there. When the king saw him, he became fearful and stopped. But he considered, "This man is not likely to be bad, since he is visiting the Buddha," and so he approached the Buddha, bowed, and sat down.

    But when that lay follower, because of his respect for the Buddha, neither bowed down to the king nor stood up for him, the king became annoyed. The Buddha realised what was happening and said to the king, "Great king, this lay follower is learned, a master of the tradition, and he is free from sensual desire."

    The king thought, "This lay follower does not deserve to be in an inferior position, since even the Buddha praises him." And he said to that lay follower, "Please say what you want."

    "Thank you, sir." 2

    The Buddha then instructed, inspired, and gladdened King Pasenadi with a teaching, after which the king got up from his seat, bowed down, circumambulated the Buddha with his right side toward him, and left.

    Soon afterwards King Pasenadi was up in his finest stilt house, when he saw that lay follower walking along the street, holding a sunshade. He summoned him and said, "You are a learned Buddhist, a master of the tradition. Please teach my harem."

    "Whatever I know, sir, I know because of the monks. They should teach the harem."

    Knowing that the lay follower was right, the king went to the Buddha, bowed, sat down, and said, "Sir, please ask a monk to teach my harem." The Buddha then instructed, inspired, and gladdened King Pasenadi with a teaching, after which the king got up from his seat, paid his respects as before, and left.

    Soon afterwards, the Buddha said to Venerable Ānanda, "Well then, Ānanda, teach the king's harem."

    "Yes, sir." And from time to time he would enter the harem and teach.

    Then, after robing up in the morning, Ānanda took his bowl and robe and went to King Pasenadi's house. On that occasion the king was in bed with Queen Mallikā. The queen saw Ānanda coming and she quickly got up, but her burnished golden dressing gown fell off. Ānanda turned around right there and returned to the monastery. And he told the monks what had happened.

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised him, "How could Venerable Ānanda enter the royal compound without first being announced?" The Buddha questioned him, established that it was true, and rebuked him.

    Having rebuked him, the Buddha gave a teaching and addressed the monks: "Monks, there are these ten dangers of entering a royal compound. What ten?

    "It may be that a monk enters where the king is sitting with his queen. The queen smiles when she sees the monk, or the monk smiles when he sees the queen. The king thinks, 'Surely they have done it, or they are going to.'

    "Again, because the king is very busy, he does not remember having slept with a certain woman, yet she becomes pregnant because of that. The king thinks, 'Only the monk enters here. Is he responsible for this?'

    "Again, a gem disappears from the royal compound. The king thinks, 'Only the monk enters here. Is he responsible for this?'

    "Again, secret discussions in the royal compound are spread outside. The king thinks, 'Only the monk enters here. Is he responsible for this?'

    "Again, in the royal compound a father longs for his son, or a son longs for his father. They think, 'Only the monk enters here. Is he responsible for this?' 3

    "Again, the king promotes someone. Those who dislike this think, 'The king is close to the monk. Is he responsible for this?' Again, the king demotes someone. Those who dislike this think, 'The king is close to the monk. Is he responsible for this?'

    "Again, the king sends out the army at an inappropriate time. Those who dislike this think, 'The king is close to the monk. Is he responsible for this?' Again, after sending out the army at an appropriate time, the king orders it to turn back while still en route. Those who dislike this think, 'The king is close to the monk. Is he responsible for this?'

    "Again, a royal compound is crowded with elephants, horses, and chariots, as well as enticing sights, sounds, smells, tastes, and tangibles that are not suitable for a monk.

    "Monks, these are the ten dangers of entering a royal compound." Then, after rebuking Ānanda in many ways, the Buddha spoke in dispraise of being difficult to support, and stated the rule.4

    Synopsis — A king who had been affronted by a layman's refusal to rise for him ended by asking that layman to teach his household, and was sent to the Buddha for a monk instead; and the monk the Buddha sent walked in one morning on the king and queen in bed. The rule is short and the teaching that precedes it is not: ten ways in which a monk who is known at the palace will be suspected of what happens there.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk, without first being announced, crosses the threshold to the bedroom of a consecrated aristocrat king, when both the king and the queen are present, he commits an offense entailing confession."5

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu, without being announced, enters the bedroom of a consecrated king when both the king and the queen are present, he incurs a pācittiya."6

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu, unannounced beforehand, cross the threshold of a consecrated noble king's (sleeping chamber) from which the king has not left, from which the valuable (the queen) has not withdrawn, it is to be confessed."7

    Brahm

    "Entering a Khattiya King's bedroom unannounced when the King and Queen are both within is an offence of Pācittiya."8

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu, without having been announced beforehand, should go beyond the boundary post of [the bed-room of] a king who is a consecrated noble, when the king has not departed, [and] the [queen-] jewel has not withdrawn, [this is a case] involving expiation."9

    Ñāṇatusita calls this "another obscure rule, just as Pāc 19 and 73, which probably got misunderstood and corrupted during transmission", and the renderings show why.10 A bedroom, a room or a sleeping chamber is in all five English versions and in none of the Pāli; Brahmāli says in a note that he added it "from the word analysis …", and Ñāṇatusita marks it with brackets.11 The rule's indakhīla is "the boundary post" in Ñāṇatusita, a threshold in Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga and Thanissaro, which follow the Vibhaṅga's own gloss, and simply entering in the other two; that is taken up below, under Effort.12 Ratana is the queen, as the Vibhaṅga's word-analysis has it, though in the origin story's third danger the same word means a valuable.13​ 14

    One difference changes what the rule requires. Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga has "a consecrated aristocrat king", but his recitation has only "a consecrated king".15​ 16 The dropped word is a condition of its own: the Vibhaṅga exempts "if it is not an aristocrat king" separately from "if the king has not been consecrated", so a monk working from the recitation alone would take any consecrated king to count.17​ 18 The two conditions are taken up under Object.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A monk keeps his distance from the private rooms of power, and the rule guards him there: his good name against suspicion, his practice against what such places offer, and the Saṅgha's standing with those who govern. The occasion was small. Ānanda, sent by the Buddha to teach the king's household, walked in one morning on King Pasenadi and Queen Mallikā in bed, and her gown fell as she rose.19 The Buddha answered with a teaching before he gave the rule: ten dangers in a monk's entering a royal compound. The first five are suspicions the king or his household may form about the monk himself: that he has been with a woman of the palace, that he fathered a child, that he stole a valuable, that he leaked a secret deliberation, that he is behind a father's longing for his son, or a son's for his father, a phrase the sources read two ways.20 The next four are suspicions others may form about the king's decisions, that he promoted or demoted someone, or sent out the army or recalled it, on the monk's advice. The tenth is not a suspicion at all: the compound is crowded with elephants, horses and chariots, and with sights, sounds, smells, tastes and touches "that are not suitable for a monk".21 Nine dangers are to his reputation and one to his practice. The same ten stand in the Aṅguttara as a sutta of their own, and Brahmāli calls the passage a warning against getting too close to power, "a very real problem for some monastics, both ancient and modern"; Thanissaro adds that some of the dangers still apply wherever a monk is on familiar terms with a person of influence, royal or not.22​ 23

    Thanissaro gives the offence two factors, object and effort.24

    Object

    The object is a king in his bedroom with his queen. The king must be a khattiya of pure descent, seven generations back in the Pāli's count, and consecrated with the khattiya consecration.25 The two are separate conditions. The Vibhaṅga exempts each on its own, and Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm both name both, so a consecrated king of another lineage, or a khattiya who was never consecrated, is not this rule's object.26​ 27​ 28 The bedroom is anywhere the king's bed is prepared, even outdoors behind a curtain or screen.29 Both king and queen must be in it; if either has left, there is no offence.30

    Effort

    The Vibhaṅga defines the indakhīla as the threshold of the bedroom, and measures the offence there: crossing it with the first foot is an offence of wrong conduct, with the second the pācittiya.31 That gloss governs, but it is a gloss: Ñāṇatusita renders the word "the boundary post" and finds its exact meaning "debatable", and at Pj 2, the only other rule in the bhikkhus' Vibhaṅga whose text carries it, it marks the gateway to a village, where Brahmāli renders it "threshold" too.32​ 33

    The offence falls only on one "not first announced", which the Vibhaṅga defines as not having first informed the occupants.34 Ajahn Brahm gives the practical form: "one does not announce oneself first, by a cough for example".35 The cough is his own gloss and a good one, for the point is that the people inside should know someone is coming.

    Perception

    Perception does not mitigate. Where the monk has not been announced, he offends whether he knows it, doubts it, or believes he has been announced; where he has been announced but perceives that he has not, or is unsure, it is an offence of wrong conduct.36​ 37

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The seven substantive clauses negate the rule's conditions one by one, and only the first, that he was announced, is one a monk can act on.

    The rule can hardly be broken now, and Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm both say so.38​ 39 Brahmāli drew out what it leaves standing: the general idea is "not to … burst into people's private quarters", a standard worth keeping "whether you are in a king's house or anybody else's house".40 Pc 43 keeps that principle in force for a householder's bedroom, with the same word for the room and the same condition that both occupants are present.41 Ānanda turned around at the door and went back to the monastery, and the rule asks no more of a monk than that he knock first.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — on crossing the threshold with the first foot; the pācittiya follows on the second.42
    • Dukkaṭa — where the monk had been announced but perceived that he had not, or was unsure.43

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence "if he has been announced; if it is not an aristocrat king; if the king has not been consecrated with the aristocratic consecration; if the king has left the bedroom; if the queen has left the bedroom; if they both have left; if it is not a bedroom; if he is insane; if he is the first offender."44

    Related Offences

    • Pc 43 — sitting down intruding on a couple in a bedroom: the living form of this rule's principle, with a householder's bedroom for a king's, and no allowance for being announced.45
    • Pc 84 — picking up a valuable: the next rule, which shares this one's vocabulary of ratana.
    • Pj 2 — taking what is not given: the only other rule in the bhikkhus' Vibhaṅga whose text carries indakhīla, there at the gateway to a village.46
    • Pc 4 — the rule at which Thanissaro sets out the pattern of a perception that does not mitigate.47

    Related Suttas

    • AN 10.45 — the ten dangers of entering the king's inner palace, the origin story's own teaching, each closed by its refrain.48
