    Aniyata 1

    Story (Vatthu)

    At one time the Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time Venerable Udāyī was associating with and visiting a number of families in Sāvatthī. One of the families that supported him gave their daughter in marriage to the son of another family, which also supported him. Soon afterwards Udāyī went to the first family and asked where the daughter was, and was told that she had been given to another family. He went there and again asked where the girl was. They said, "She's sitting in her room." He went up to her, and the two of them sat down alone in private on a concealed seat suitable for the deed. When they were able, they chatted; otherwise he gave her a teaching.

    At that time Visākhā Migāramātā had many healthy children and grandchildren, and so she was considered auspicious; at sacrifices, ceremonies and celebrations people would feed her first. She had been invited to that same family. When she arrived, she saw Udāyī sitting alone with the girl, and she said to him, "Venerable, it's not appropriate for you to sit down in private alone with a woman on a concealed seat suitable for the deed. You may not be aiming at that act, but it's hard to convince people with little confidence." But Udāyī did not listen.

    After leaving, Visākhā told the monks what had happened. The monks of few desires complained and criticised him: "How could Venerable Udāyī sit down in private alone with a woman on a concealed seat suitable for the deed?" They told the Buddha, who had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned Udāyī: "Is it true, Udāyī, that you did this?" "It's true, sir." The Buddha rebuked him, "This will affect people's confidence", and laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — Visākhā finds Udāyī alone with a young married woman on a concealed seat and warns him, and he does not listen; the Buddha lays down a rule under which a trustworthy laywoman's charge in such a case is dealt with.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk sits down in private alone with a woman on a concealed seat suitable for the deed, and a trustworthy female lay follower sees him and accuses him of an offense entailing expulsion, an offense entailing suspension, or an offense entailing confession, then, if he admits to the sitting, he is to be dealt with according to one of these three or according to what that trustworthy female lay follower has said. This rule is undetermined."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "It may be that a bhikkhu sits alone in private with a woman in a secluded place and is seen by a trustworthy female lay disciple. If she accuses him of a pārājika, a saṅghādisesa, or a pācittiya, and if he admits that charge, he should be dealt with accordingly."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu sit in private, alone with a woman on a seat secluded enough to lend itself (to sexual intercourse), so that a female lay follower whose word can be trusted, having seen (them), might describe it as constituting any of three cases—entailing defeat, communal meetings, or confession—then the bhikkhu, acknowledging having sat (there), may be dealt with in line with any of the three cases—entailing defeat, communal meetings, or confession—or he may be dealt with in line with whichever case the female lay follower whose word can be trusted described. This case is indefinite."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Should any monk sit in private with a woman, just the monk and her, on a seat sufficiently screened to be suitable (for sexual intercourse), so that a trustworthy female lay follower having seen (them) might describe it as constituting one of three cases: defeat, initial and subsequent meeting of the community, or confession , (then) the monk acknowledging having been seated may be dealt with for one of the three cases: defeat, initial and subsequent meeting of the community, or confession , or he may be dealt with for whichever (case) the female lay follower described. This case is undetermined."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should take seat with a woman, one [man] with one [woman], privately, on a concealed seat [that is] sufficiently fit for doing it, [and then if] a female lay-follower whose words can be trusted having seen that, should speak in accordance with one of three cases: in accordance with disqualification, in accordance with [a case] involving the community in the beginning and in the rest, or in accordance with expiation, [then] the bhikkhu who is admitting the sitting down should be made to do [what is] in accordance with one of three cases: in accordance with disqualification, or in accordance with [a case] involving the community in the beginning and in the rest, or in accordance with expiation, or in accordance with whatever that female lay-follower whose words can be trusted should say, in according with that the bhikkhu is to be made to do. This is an uncertain case."6

    The four full renderings agree, down to the clause that matters most: the monk who admits the sitting may be dealt with for one of the three offences "or according to what that trustworthy female lay follower has said." The recitation leaves that clause out. In its place it says that if he "admits that charge", he "should be dealt with accordingly", which is how the Vibhaṅga itself applies the rule. That the Vibhaṅga here departs from the rule's wording is Thanissaro's reading, set out under Procedure. Ñāṇatusita's "one [man] with one [woman]" keeps the shape of the Pāli eko ekāya, which the others render "alone" or, in Ajahn Brahm's, "just the monk and her", and the difference bears on a question discussed under Circumstance.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    Visākhā's words to Udāyī give the rule its reason: "You may not be aiming at that act, but it's hard to convince people with little confidence."7 A monk's conduct is judged by those who see it, and a monk alone with a woman out of sight and hearing has given them no way to judge it well. The rule's answer is to give the one who sees a voice: it opens with what a trustworthy laywoman sees and the charge she brings. Thanissaro finds it "remarkable", given the position of women in Indian society at the time, that the Buddha was at one point willing to let the monks give her word more weight than an accused monk's.8 Ajahn Brahm sees the same: the rule was needed so that a virtuous woman's charge could not be dismissed because a woman made it.9 Brahmāli observes in class that the point has, if anything, grown stronger: today a woman's report that a monk has been misbehaving is the first evidence one would weigh, not the last.10

    The rule assigns no penalty of its own. It is a procedure by which the Saṅgha deals with a charge, and the penalty it reaches is another rule's: a pārājika, a saṅghādisesa or a pācittiya, according to what is established.11​ 12 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual gives it eight factors, which fall into two groups: the circumstance that grounds the charge, and the procedure that resolves it.13

    Circumstance

    The Vibhaṅga defines each element. The woman is any human female, even a girl born that day. They are alone, just the monk and the woman, and in private, both to the eye, where no one can see them wink, raise an eyebrow or nod, and to the ear, where no one can hear ordinary speech. The seat is concealed by a wall, a screen, a door, a tree or anything else, and suitable for the deed, meaning sexual intercourse would be possible there. Sitting includes lying down, and it makes no difference who sat first.14 Such a place may itself be a breach of Pc 44, and it gives the opportunity for Pj 1 and Sg 1–4, which is why the case is undetermined.15

    Disputed — whether the presence of other women takes the case outside the rule.

    On the commentaries' side the answer is no: the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī and the Samantapāsādikā, as the Manual reports them, hold that the presence of other women does not absolve the monk. The Manual rejects this on the rule's own words: it says eko ekāya, "one with one", and the Vibhaṅga explains "alone" as "just a bhikkhu and a woman", so the commentarial reading "must be rejected."16 The question is narrower than it looks. At Pc 44, whose rule does not carry the words eko ekāya, Thanissaro and the Manual both hold that many women are no exemption, and Ajahn Brahm agrees; it is only here, where "one with one" meets a concealed seat, that the Manual parts from the commentaries.17 The Manual's reason is the rule and its word-analysis. The commentaries' reading comes to us through the Manual without its reason; the nearest a modern source offers is Ajahn Brahm's guess at Pc 44, a rule without eko ekāya, that a second woman was presumably considered no guarantee against malicious gossip.18 The sources argue this without settling it; and either way the monk among several women in a concealed place has Pc 44 to answer.

    Procedure

    The witness is a female lay follower, one who has gone for refuge to the Buddha, the Teaching and the Saṅgha, and she is trustworthy if she has attained the fruit: she is at least a stream-enterer. If she is, the Saṅgha must investigate her charge; if she is not, it may still do so, and Ajahn Brahm points out that the Buddha questioned Dabba on the charge of a nun who would certainly not have been a stream-enterer (Sg 8).19​ 20​ 21 One detail of the rule remains in force when all else has been absorbed into the later guidelines. Ordinarily a monk who brings a charge must first ask the accused's leave, and the accused may refuse it; where a trustworthy lay follower brings the charge, no leave is needed, one of the monks must take it up, and the accused must answer, since the matter has already spread among the laity and the Saṅgha must act.22

    On Thanissaro's reading, which Ñāṇatusita follows, the rule's wording lets the Saṅgha deal with the monk either by what he admits or by what she said, and the Vibhaṅga allows only the first: every case in its long series is decided by the monk's admission. If she says she saw him seated, having intercourse with a woman, and he admits it, he is dealt with for the offence; if he admits the sitting but not the intercourse, for the sitting; if he says he was lying down, for the lying down; if he says he was standing, he is not to be dealt with.23 Thanissaro explains the departure, with a hedge: apparently these two rules were laid down early, before the principle of judging a monk only by his own admission (the fourth of the Adhikaraṇasamatha), and before the general guidelines for accusations, which the Buddha hedged with checks after the group of six abused the system against monks they disliked. The Vibhaṅga brings the rule into line with them.24

    Under those guidelines the accused and the accuser are both present and both accept the meeting that hears the case; a monk takes up a layperson's charge. The accused gives his account and is questioned, past his first word, until all are satisfied, and only three verdicts are possible: that he is innocent, that he was insane at the time, or that he is guilty and, for drawing out his confession, deserving of a further act of the Saṅgha. The decision must be unanimous, or the case is left unsettled. If he admits an act that is an offence but will not see it as one, the Saṅgha may suspend him.25​ 26

    Disputed — whether a monk who admits nothing can be dealt with when the evidence against him is overwhelming.

    Thanissaro allows no such exception to the Vibhaṅga's grid, in which a monk who admits nothing "is not to be dealt with": the guidelines exist above all to keep an innocent monk from being unfairly penalised; a verdict later found wrong may be reopened; and as for the guilty monk who escapes, "the laws of kamma guarantee that in the long run he is not getting away with anything at all."27 Ajahn Brahm agrees that an accused monk is innocent until he admits guilt, but makes one exception: where the evidence proves beyond reasonable doubt that he is guilty and he still will not admit it, the Saṅgha should expel him if the charge is a pārājika, forcibly if need be, citing the Vinayamukha and the Cullavagga's account of the impure monk whom Mahāmoggallāna led out of the Uposatha, and should suspend him for not seeing an offence if it is anything less.28 The Vibhaṅga's grid weighs admissions and says nothing of evidence; the Cullavagga's expulsion followed on what the Buddha and Mahāmoggallāna knew, not on evidence a Community weighed. Whether that case licenses a Community to act on evidence alone is a question the texts do not answer; a Community that would act on it should know it is acting on Ajahn Brahm's reading, not the Vibhaṅga's.

    Thanissaro's last word on the rule is addressed to the monk, not the Saṅgha. Under Pc 44 a monk may in some states of mind sit alone with a woman in a secluded place without penalty, but even if his motives are pure his actions may not look pure to whoever comes upon him; he could face a formal hearing, waste the Community's time and his own, and, if acquitted for want of proof, still be thought guilty by some. He would be wise to avoid such situations altogether, "remembering what Lady Visākhā told Ven. Udāyin".29

    Resolution Grid (Pariccheda)

    The Vibhaṅga's permutations set out how the case resolves from what the monk admits:30

    • He admits the offence, whether or not he admits going there or sitting: dealt with for the offence.
    • He admits sitting, but not the offence: dealt with for the sitting.
    • He admits neither sitting nor the offence, whether or not he admits going there: not dealt with.

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    The rule carries no non-offence clause, since it assigns no penalty; a monk who admits only going there, or nothing the rule describes, "is not to be dealt with."31

    Related Offences

    • Aniyata 2 — the same procedure for a seat that is private but not concealed.
    • Pc 44, Pc 45 — sitting in private with a woman on a concealed seat, and in private alone with a woman: the pācittiyas most often beneath a charge under these rules.
    • Pj 1, Sg 1–4 — the heavier offences such a place affords the opportunity for.
    • Sg 8 — the accusation, and the Buddha's questioning of Dabba on a nun's charge (Ajahn Brahm's example of a witness who was not a stream-enterer).

    Related Suttas

    • SN 35.127 — how young monks keep the celibate life, setting up toward women "the idea that they are your mother", sister or daughter: the guard within, where this rule's guard is what others can see.32
    • SN 47.6 — the hawk and the quail: the domain of others, where Māra gets a hold, "is the five cords of sensual pleasure"; a monk's own resort "is the four establishments of mindfulness". A concealed seat with a woman lies in the first.33
    • AN 5.55 — a mother and son, a nun and a monk: "Because they often saw one another, a bond was formed; because a bond formed, intimacy arose; because there was intimacy, lust found an opening"; the danger a concealed seat holds even for a monk not aiming at the act.34
