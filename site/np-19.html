    NP 19

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī, in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time the monks from the group of six engaged in various kinds of trades involving money. People complained and criticised them: "How can the Sakyan monastics engage in trades that involve money? They're just like householders who indulge in worldly pleasures!"

    The monks heard the complaints, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised the group of six: "How can the monks from the group of six do this?" After rebuking them in many ways, they told the Buddha. Soon afterwards he had the Saṅgha gathered, questioned the monks, and rebuked them: "Foolish men, how can you do this? This will affect people's confidence." Then he laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — The group of six deal in money, and the rule against a monk gaining money through trade follows.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk engages in various kinds of trades involving money, he commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu becomes the owner of money through trading, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu engage in various types of monetary exchange, it (the income) is to be forfeited and confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Exchanging anything of one's own for gold or money is a nissaggiya pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should engage in the various kinds of trading in money, [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture."6

    The renderings divide over the rule's reach. The full translation's "various kinds of trades involving money" and Ñāṇatusita's "trading in money" render rūpiyasaṃvohāra without saying which way the money runs. The recitation's "becomes the owner of money through trading" and Ajahn Brahm's "anything of one's own for gold or money" take the rule to cover a trade that ends with money in the monk's hands. Thanissaro's "monetary exchange" is open in itself, but his bracketed "(the income)" makes what is forfeited the proceeds of the trade, and he argues below that these are money. Ñāṇatusita's note reads the rule more narrowly still, as the exchange of "(precious) metals and/or money … for (precious) metals and/or money".7 Which trades the rule reaches is the rule's one dispute, and it is argued below, under Effort.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule keeps a monk out of trade for money, the line that sets his life apart from the householder's. Those who saw the group of six dealing in money said so: "They're just like householders who indulge in worldly pleasures!"; and the Buddha's rebuke was that "this will affect people's confidence."8 Thanissaro shows why a second rule was needed after NP 18. That rule and the next allow a monk to point out a steward and, if he words it rightly, to propose a trade; on those two alone a steward could speculate in currency for him without offence. This rule allows no such wording, and so it closes the gap.9

    The offence has two factors, object and effort.10

    Object

    Money, defined as at NP 18: gold, whether shaped into ornaments for the head, neck, hands, feet or waist, or not shaped, a lump; and coins of gold, copper, wood or resin, "whatever is used in commerce."11 The marks of money and the line between money and a cheque or card are NP 18's.

    Effort

    Engaging in an exchange. The Vibhaṅga's table runs through every exchange of shaped gold, unshaped gold, or both, for the same, and its perception series then adds the case of something that is not money exchanged for money.12​ 13 Brahmāli's endnote reads that case, with the Commentary he quotes, as selling: he exchanges his own goods and ends up with money.14 So the rule covers changing one currency for another, and also selling anything for money. Ajahn Brahm's examples make it concrete: changing a hundred dollars for rupees, selling one's bowl for fifteen pounds, cashing a travellers' cheque.15 It does not matter whether he trades himself or has another do it for him; he still ends up the owner of the money, and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual makes that ownership the factor.16​ 17

    Thanissaro draws the practical consequence. A monk may not tell his steward that he would like something of his sold, or the funds held for him invested for a return; a monk going abroad must leave it to his steward to see that money held for him may need changing into the local currency.18 The Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī, Thanissaro reports, requires the goods traded to be the monk's own, but the non-offence clauses make no exception for goods belonging to others, and Thanissaro concludes that a monk who arranges the sale of his family's goods must forfeit any of the proceeds they give him.19 Perception does not excuse: the Vibhaṅga gives the full offence for money got by exchange whether he perceives it as money, is unsure, or does not.20

    Disputed — whether the rule also covers buying with money.

    The widest reading takes in any trade in which money is on either side, so that a monk who pays money for a robe falls here. It is the Commentary's, in Thanissaro's report, and Buddhaghosa, granting that the passage this rests on is not in the Vibhaṅga, holds that logically it should be.21 Thanissaro answers from the Vibhaṅga itself: its table lists only exchanges that end in money, its relinquishment speaks only of the "one who purchased money" and a money-disposer, and its procedure for having a steward buy allowable things with money stands under the next rule, not this one.22 Ajahn Brahm takes the same view, that the rule "only extends to transactions where the end product is gold or money", and the Manual's factor, any trade in which the monk becomes the owner of money, agrees.23​ 24 I follow the Vibhaṅga's evidence and place buying with money under NP 20; but the Commentary's reading is, as Thanissaro says, widely followed, and which rule a purchase falls under is a judgment the texts leave open. On either reading buying with money is an offence; what differs is the rule it falls under, and so how the goods bought are treated.

    Procedure

    As at NP 18: the money is relinquished in the midst of the Saṅgha and not returned; a lay follower may be asked to look into it and shown what is allowable, and what he brings may be used by everyone except the one who did the exchange; failing him, an appointed money-discarder throws it away without noting where.25 Ajahn Brahm adds that money already lost or stolen cannot be relinquished, and the monk informs the Saṅgha and confesses.26

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    Only the insane and the first offender.27 The rule has no exemption, and that is its point: there is no form of words by which a monk may turn what is his into money.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — exchanging for something that is not money while perceiving it as money or unsure.28
    • Dukkaṭa — the money-discarder who takes note of where he throws it.29

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he is insane; if he is the first offender.30

    Related Offences

    • NP 18 — receiving money, whose definitions, relinquishment and disposal this rule shares.
    • NP 20 — buying and selling, where the purchase of goods with money falls on the Vibhaṅga's reading.
    • NP 10 — the steward and the fund, whose wording-right allowances this rule does not extend.

    Related Suttas

    • SN 42.10 — the Buddha's answer to the headman Maṇicūḷaka: whoever agrees to gold or money also agrees to the five strands of sensual pleasure; a monk who trades in money has agreed to it in act as well as word.31
