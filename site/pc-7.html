    Pc 7

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī, in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time Venerable Udāyī was visiting a number of families in Sāvatthī. One morning he went to a certain family, where the housewife was sitting at the door to the house and her daughter-in-law at the door to the guesthouse. Udāyī went up to the housewife and gave her a teaching, whispering in her ear. The daughter-in-law thought, "Is this monastic my mother-in-law's lover, or is he speaking indecently?" Udāyī then went up to the daughter-in-law and taught her the same way, and the housewife thought, "Is this monastic my daughter-in-law's lover, or is he speaking indecently?" When Udāyī had left, the two women compared what he had said, found it was a teaching in both cases, and complained: "How can Venerable Udāyī give teachings by whispering in the ear? Should not teachings be given audibly and openly?" The monks of few desires complained too, and told the Buddha, who rebuked Udāyī and laid down a first rule: a monk was not to give a teaching to a woman at all.

    Soon afterwards some female lay followers asked monks for a teaching. "It is not allowable for us to teach women." "Just teach five or six sentences; that might be enough for us to understand." But the monks, afraid of doing wrong, still would not, and the women complained that they were refused when they asked. The monks told the Buddha, who relaxed the rule: "Monks, I allow you to teach five or six sentences to a woman."

    When the monks from the group of six heard of the allowance, they taught women more than five or six sentences with a man who did not understand sitting nearby. The monks of few desires complained, and told the Buddha, who laid down the rule in its final form.1

    Synopsis — Udāyī's whispered teachings to women rouse suspicion, and after a first ruling forbidding teaching women and a second allowing five or six sentences, the rule settles on more than that requiring a man who understands to be present.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk gives a teaching of more than five or six sentences to a woman, except in the presence of a man who understands, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu, without being asked, teaches a woman more than six sentences of the word of the Buddha, except if a comprehending man is present, he incurs a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu teach more than five or six sentences of Dhamma to a woman, unless a knowledgeable man is present, it is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Engaging in a long conversation with a woman out of hearing of another man is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should teach the Dhamma to a woman by [means of] more than five or six sentences, except [when being together] with a discerning male human being, [this is a case] involving expiation."6

    The renderings agree on the measure, "sentences", and part on two points that matter. The recitation writes in "without being asked", folding the non-offence for a woman's question into the rule. And Ajahn Brahm's "long conversation" reads the rule by its purpose rather than its word: where the others keep "teaching" or "Dhamma", he would count talk on any subject. What "Dhamma" covers is disputed, and is discussed below, under Effort.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A monk teaches women in the open under this rule, for his own protection and for the confidence of those who see him teach. Udāyī whispered his teaching into each woman's ear, each woman suspected him of being the other's lover, and their complaint names what was wanted: "Should not teachings be given audibly and openly?"7 The Buddha first forbade teaching women at all, then, when women who asked were refused, allowed five or six sentences, and finally required a man who understands. Thanissaro reads the rule's aim from the allowance for a woman's question: if suspicion alone were the concern, a question would not lift the rule, so its target is more likely a monk "using his knowledge of Dhamma as a come-on", since "there are women who find such knowledge irresistible".8

    Thanissaro analyses the offence into two factors, object and effort.9 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual lists four elements, the man who understands, the speaking, the single sitting, and the absence of a question, and each finds its place inside Thanissaro's two.10

    Object

    The object is a human female who "understands and is capable of discerning good and bad speech, what is decent and what is indecent".11 The word covers women in the plural: with two or more and no man present, the limit holds.12 Teaching a female spirit, ghost or animal in the form of a woman, or a paṇḍaka, is a dukkaṭa, and perception does not excuse, on the pattern set out at Pc 4.13

    Effort

    Six sentences. What is the measure? It is the vācā, which can mean a word or a saying; the Commentary makes it about a line of verse, and Brahmāli, Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm all take it as a sentence, the origin story showing that a point can be taught in five or six.14 Teaching by the line is an offence for every line, by the syllable for every syllable, and the count runs within one sitting.15​ 16

    A man who understands. He must, like the woman, be able to tell decent speech from indecent. A man who cannot follow the talk does not count, which was the group of six's device.17​ 18

    Where and how. Unlike Pc 44 and Pc 45, the rule covers a monk and a woman standing as well as sitting. It applies to the telephone, but not to letters or other writing, since it concerns the spoken word.19​ 20 Brahmāli counts a video call as nearer a meeting face to face, and the more hazardous: better to have a man present, or to use the voice alone.21

    The Dhamma. The Vibhaṅga defines it as at Pc 4: what was spoken by the Buddha, by disciples, by sages, by gods, connected with the beneficial and with the Teaching.22 What that covers is disputed.

    Disputed — what counts as "Dhamma" for this rule: the Canon in Pāli alone, its passages in any language, or any talk at all.

    Thanissaro reports the Commentary as taking it to be the Pāli Canon and the Mahā Aṭṭhakathā, the second "highly unlikely" to him, since that commentary did not yet exist.23 He sets out two other readings: any statement about the Dhamma, in any language, quoted or one's own; or, better fitted to the Pāli syntax, passages of the Canon in any language, translations included, on the strength of a Cullavagga passage that Brahmāli's own translation reads differently.24​ 25 The Manual confines the Dhamma to passages of the four main Nikāyas and the four main books of the Vinaya, yet counts translations of them: "the Buddha clearly said the Dhamma should be spoken in the local language", and the commentarial idea that only Pāli texts are included "seems strange".26 In class Brahmāli agrees that a modern Dhamma talk falls outside the rule.27 Thanissaro's reading thus finds support in the Manual, though not in Brahmāli's own rendering of the Cullavagga passage it rests on. Ajahn Brahm reads by the purpose and counts "conversing on any subject for more than six sentences".28 The texts leave it open, and Thanissaro's counsel is to follow one's own Community's reading.29

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The change of posture the exemptions allow marks a separate conversation, Thanissaro explains, so that a monk "is not condemned to silence" toward a woman he has once taught; and a woman's question allows him a talk at whatever length he needs.30 That allowance "honors a woman's desire to understand the Dhamma", but Thanissaro's counsel is restraint even there, for "the relationship of male teacher to female student has a long, well-known history of getting out of hand", and Ajahn Brahm puts the practice simply: a monk who is to say more than a few sentences to a woman "should always try to call another monk, novice or layman to come within hearing."31​ 32

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • A dukkaṭa for teaching more than five or six sentences to a female spirit, ghost or animal in the form of a woman, or to a paṇḍaka, with no man who understands present.33
    • A dukkaṭa where the person taught is not a woman but the monk perceives one, or is unsure.34

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if a man who understands is present; if he teaches five or six sentences, or fewer; if he gets up and sits down again and then teaches; if the woman gets up and sits down again and he then teaches her; if he teaches another woman; if he asks a question; if he is asked a question and then speaks; if he is speaking for someone else's benefit and a woman listens in; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.35 In Thanissaro's report the Commentary lets a monk addressing a hundred women teach six hundred sentences, aiming six at each, and gives as its example of a talk in answer to a question the whole Dīgha Nikāya.36

    Related Offences

    • Pc 4 — the other rule turning on the definition of "Dhamma", which defers its argument to this rule.
    • Pc 44, 45, Aniyata 1, 2 — sitting alone with a woman; a monk alone with a woman in talk on other subjects is caught there, though this rule alone reaches the standing case.37
    • Pc 21, 22 — the rules on instructing nuns, which carry the same allowance for a question.38
    • Sg 3 — lewd speech to a woman, the graver offence the definitions of "a woman" and "a man who understands" look toward.

    Related Suttas

    • AN 5.159 — a monk named Udāyī, as in the origin story, teaching a large lay assembly, and the Buddha's words that teaching others is not easy: "It isn't easy, Ānanda, to teach the Dhamma to others. One who teaches the Dhamma to others should first set up five qualities internally."39
    • SN 35.127 — the reflection on women as mother, sister and daughter, the guard against the motive Thanissaro reads as the rule's target.40
