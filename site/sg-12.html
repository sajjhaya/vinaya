    Sg 12

    Story (Vatthu)

    At one time when the Buddha was staying at Kosambī in Ghosita's Monastery, Venerable Channa was misbehaving. The monks would tell him, "Don't do that; it's not allowable," and he would reply, "Who are you to correct me? I should correct you! The Buddha is mine; the Teaching is mine. The Master realized the Truth because of me. Just as grass, sticks, and fallen leaves are whirled up by a strong wind all at once, just as various water plants are whirled up by a mountain stream all at once, so too have you–after going forth with various names, various families, various castes, various households–been lifted up all at once. So, who are you to correct me? I should correct you! The Buddha is mine; the Teaching is mine. The Master realized the Truth because of me."

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised him: "How can Venerable Channa make himself incorrigible when he's legitimately corrected by the monks?" They rebuked Channa and told the Buddha. He had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned Channa: "Is it true, Channa, that you do this?" "It's true, sir." The Buddha rebuked him and laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — Channa answers every correction by claiming the Buddha and the Teaching as his own, and the Buddha lays down the rule that a monk who makes himself incorrigible is to be corrected, and offends only if he will not stop.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk is difficult to correct, and he makes himself incorrigible when legitimately corrected by the monks concerning the training rules that are recited, saying, 'Venerables, don't say anything to me, either good or bad, and I won't say anything to you, either good or bad. Please refrain from correcting me,' then the monks should correct him like this: 'Be easy to correct, venerable, not incorrigible. And please give legitimate correction to the monks, and the monks will do the same to you. For it's in this way that the Buddha's community has grown, that is, through mutual correction and mutual clearing of offenses.' If that monk continues as before, the monks should press him up to three times to make him stop. If he then stops, all is well. If he does not stop, he commits an offense entailing suspension."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu refuses to be corrected about breaches of the pātimokkha rules, he should be admonished up to three times to make him stop. If he does not stop, he incurs a saṅghādisesa."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "In case a bhikkhu is by nature difficult to admonish—who, when being legitimately admonished by the bhikkhus with reference to the training rules included in the (Pāṭimokkha) recitation, makes himself unadmonishable, (saying,) 'Do not, venerable ones, say anything to me, good or bad; and I won't say anything to the venerable ones, good or bad. Refrain, venerable ones, from admonishing me'—the bhikkhus are to admonish him thus: 'Let the venerable one not make himself unadmonishable. Let the venerable one make himself admonishable. Let the venerable one admonish the bhikkhus in accordance with what is right, and the bhikkhus will admonish the venerable one in accordance with what is right; for it is thus that the Blessed One's following is nurtured: through mutual admonition, through mutual rehabilitation.' And should that bhikkhu, thus admonished by the bhikkhus, persist as before, the bhikkhus are to rebuke him up to three times for the sake of relinquishing that. If while being rebuked up to three times he relinquishes that, that is good. If he does not relinquish (that), it entails initial and subsequent meetings of the Community."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "In case a monk is by nature difficult to admonish -- who when being legitimately admonished by the monks with reference to the training rules included in the recitation, makes himself unadmonishable (by saying) 'Do not, Venerable Sirs, say anything to me, good or bad, and I will not say anything to the Venerable Sirs, good or bad. Refrain, Venerable Sirs, from admonishing me' -- that monk should be admonished thus by the monks: 'Let the Venerable One not make himself unadmonishable. Let the Venerable One admonish the monks in accordance with righteousness, and the monks will admonish the Venerable One in accordance with righteousness; for it is thus that the exalted one's following is nurtured: through mutual admonition, through mutual rehabilitation'. And should that monk, thus admonished by the monks, persist as before, he is to be formally admonished up to three times by the monks so as to desist. If he does not desist it entails initial and subsequent meetings of the Saṅgha."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "Now, a bhikkhu is of a nature difficult to be spoken to, [and when] being righteously spoken to by the bhikkhus about the training precepts included in the recitation, he makes himself [one] who cannot be spoken to [saying]: 'Venerables, don't say anything good or bad to me, and I too shall not say anything good or bad to the venerables! Venerables, refrain from speaking to me!' [Then] that bhikkhu should be spoken to thus by the bhikkhus: 'Venerable, do not make yourself [one] who cannot be spoken to. Let the venerable one make himself [one] who can be spoken to. Let the venerable one speak to the bhikkhus with righteousness and the bhikkhus too will speak to the venerable one with righteousness. For the Blessed One's assembly has grown thus, that is, by the speaking of one to another, by the rehabilitating of one another,' and [if] that bhikkhu being spoken to thus by the bhikkhus should persist in the same way [as before], [then] that bhikkhu is to be argued with up to three times by the bhikkhus for the relinquishing of that [course], [and if that bhikkhu,] being argued with up to three times, should relinquish that [course], then this is good, [but] if he should not relinquish [it]: [this is a case] involving the community in the beginning and in the rest."6

    The renderings agree, and the verb for the Saṅgha's act divides them as at Sg 10. Two differences matter here. The recitation writes into the rule that the correction concerns "breaches of the pātimokkha rules", where the full rule has "the training rules that are recited"; the edge this sets is drawn under Effort. And the correction's last words are rendered "mutual clearing of offenses" by Brahmāli and "mutual rehabilitation" or "the rehabilitating of one another" by the others. The Pāli word, Ñāṇatusita notes, is often used of a monk's emerging from an offence by confession, so the community is said to grow not only by monks pointing out each other's faults but by their helping each other out of them.7

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule keeps every monk inside the mutual correction by which the Saṅgha grows. Channa's claim was closeness: the Buddha and the Teaching were his, and the other monks were grass and leaves the wind had gathered. The rule's answer is given in the words the monks are to say to such a monk: "please give legitimate correction to the monks, and the monks will do the same to you. For it's in this way that the Buddha's community has grown, that is, through mutual correction and mutual clearing of offenses."8 No one in the Saṅgha stands above that exchange, and a monk who steps out of it cannot be helped out of his own offences. The rule does not punish a single refusal or a moment's pride. It meets the settled refusal with the same patient procedure as Sg 10, and the offence falls only if he will not stop.

    The procedure is Sg 10's, stage for stage, with the same offences on the way and the same validity grid.9​ 10 Thanissaro's analysis has a single factor, effort: making oneself unadmonishable, even when rebuked three times in a properly performed act of the Saṅgha.11 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual adds one point of its own to the factors it shares with Sg 10: being difficult to correct means refusing "to accept admonishment in regard to breaches of the pātimokkha rules."12

    Effort

    The Vibhaṅga describes the monk as hard to correct, resistant, not receiving instructions respectfully; Thanissaro adds the Commentary's gloss, one impossible to speak to, who cannot stand criticism or does not mend his ways when his faults are pointed out.13​ 14 He makes himself incorrigible in the words the rule gives him, asking the monks to say nothing to him, good or bad.

    We now come to the edge, and two limits mark it. The first is the subject of the correction. The training rules concerned are those of the Monastic Code, and "legitimately" means by the training rules the Buddha laid down, so the rule reaches a monk who will not be corrected on his keeping of the Pātimokkha, not one who declines advice on other matters.15 The second is degree. A monk evasive or uncooperative under admonition falls under Pc 12, one who shows disrespect under Pc 54, one who makes excuses not to train in the rule under Pc 71; only when he "will accept criticism from no one at all", in Thanissaro's words, is he treated under this rule.16 Being corrected also has its own etiquette, which the one corrected is part of. The monk who corrects should ask leave first, as set out under Sg 8; the one asked should give it, Ajahn Brahm says, except to a shameless monk or one under a penalty of the Saṅgha, and should accept the correction "with gratitude and humility."17

    What, then, of a monk who is corrected and honestly does not see the offence?

    Disputed — whether a monk who does not believe he has committed an offence should confess it on the word of a respected senior.

    Ajahn Brahm advises that where the charge is not a heavy offence and the one making it is a respected elder, the monk should confess, even if only out of faith in the elder's judgement; where he disagrees, above all where the charge is a saṅghādisesa or pārājika, he should take it to the senior elder for investigation and, if need be, a hearing before the Saṅgha.18 Brahmāli, in class, counsels otherwise: normally a monk should say that he does not see it and ask to discuss it further or take it to a third person who knows the Vinaya, since a senior monk can be wrong and a monk should not confess an offence he has not committed; only in extreme cases, a senior who cannot be reasoned with or a threat of serious disharmony, might he confess anyway.19 Ajahn Brahm cites two passages, and they bear on the two sides differently. In the Kosambī dispute the Buddha tells a monk who does not see his offence to confess it "even out of confidence in the others", but where the others are learned and conscientious and his refusal would lead to schism: that is close to Brahmāli's exception. At the First Council the senior monks charged Ānanda with five acts of wrong conduct, and each time he answered, "I can't see that I have committed any wrong conduct, but I'll confess it out of faith in the venerables": light offences, senior monks and no threat of schism, which is Ajahn Brahm's advice.20 Yet Ānanda did not deny the acts, only that they were offences, while Brahmāli's objection is to confessing what one has not done; the two may be speaking of different cases. That reading of the two passages is ours; both teachers agree that what a monk may never do is what Channa did: refuse to hear.

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The Vibhaṅga's list of exemptions here is shorter than Sg 10's.21 A monk who carries on after the full offence can be banished for speaking in dispraise of the Community; one who will not see the offence or undergo the penalty can be excluded from the Pāṭimokkha and the Invitation, or suspended.22

    Channa's own story ends in the Khandhakas. After the Buddha's passing, Ānanda told the senior monks that at the time of his final extinguishment the Buddha had said to him, "After my passing away, Ānanda, the Sangha should impose the supreme penalty on the monk Channa", and that, asked what the supreme penalty was, he had replied, "Whatever Channa says, the monks shouldn't correct him, instruct him, or teach him." The monk who had told the others they had no business correcting him was given exactly that. When Ānanda told him, he fainted; then, troubled and ashamed, he lived alone, heedful and diligent, and became one of the perfected ones. When he asked for the penalty to be lifted, Ānanda told him it had been lifted the moment he realised perfection.23

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — for a monk who hears of it and says nothing; for the monk who does not stop after the private corrections, after the corrections in the Saṅgha, or after the motion; and for one who does not stop when the procedure pressing him is illegitimate.24​ 25
    • Thullaccaya — after each of the first two announcements of a legitimate procedure; with the dukkaṭa after the motion, annulled when the saṅghādisesa falls.26

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he has not been pressed; if he stops; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.27

    Related Offences

    • Sg 10 — the procedure, set out in full.
    • Pc 12, Pc 54, Pc 71 — evasion, disrespect and excuses under admonition, short of a refusal to be corrected at all.
    • Sg 8 — how a monk is to go about correcting another.
    • Sg 11, Sg 13, Pc 68 — the same pressing, for other refusals.

    Related Suttas

    • MN 15 — the qualities that make a monk difficult to admonish, which the Commentary, as Thanissaro reports it, quotes for this rule, among them "a bhikkhu is reproved, and he resists the reprover" and "a bhikkhu is obstinate and arrogant".28
    • AN 4.122 — the peril of waves, "a designation for anger and irritation": a monk who thinks, "Formerly, when I was a layman, I exhorted and instructed others. But now these [monks], who are young enough to be my sons or grandsons, presume to exhort and instruct me", gives up the training.29
    • AN 5.167 — the one reproved should be established in two things, "in truth and non-anger", the half of mutual correction that falls to the monk this rule addresses.30

After each of the first two announcements, he commits a serious offense. When the last announcement is finished, he commits an offense entailing suspension. For one who commits the offense entailing suspension, the offense of wrong conduct and the serious offenses are annulled."↩︎
