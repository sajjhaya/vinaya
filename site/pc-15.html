    Pc 15

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī, in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time the monks of the group of seventeen were friends: they lived together, and when they went somewhere they left together. On one occasion they put out bedding in a dwelling belonging to the Saṅgha and then departed without putting it away, having it put away, or informing anyone, and the bedding was eaten by termites.

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised them: "How could those monks from the group of seventeen put out bedding in a dwelling belonging to the Sangha, and then depart without putting it away, getting it put away, or informing anyone? The furniture was eaten by termites." They told the Buddha, who questioned the monks, confirmed that it was so, and — observing that this would affect people's confidence — laid down the training rule.

    Synopsis — The group-of-seventeen monks, who did everything together, lay out bedding in a Saṅgha dwelling and leave together without putting it away or telling anyone, and termites eat it; the Buddha forbids abandoning bedding one has laid out in a Saṅgha dwelling.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk puts out bedding in a dwelling belonging to the Sangha, or has it put out, and he then departs without putting it away, having it put away, or informing anyone, he commits an offense entailing confession."1

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu lays out bedding in a dwelling belonging to the Saṅgha, and then abandons it, he incurs a pācittiya."2

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu set out bedding in a dwelling belonging to the Community—or have it set out—and then on departing neither put it away nor have it put away, or should he go without taking leave, it is to be confessed."3

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Leaving a monastery abandoning the bedding which one was using in a dwelling belonging to the Sangha is an offence of Pācittiya."4

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu, having [himself] laid out or having [someone else] lay out, bedding in a dwelling belonging to the community, [and] then, when departing, should not take [it] away or should not have [it] taken away, or should go without asking [someone to put it back], [this is a case] involving expiation."5

    The five renderings sit close, and the recitation's "abandons" again compresses the whole effort clause into one word. The word that moves is "departs", and it moves away from Pc 14. There it meant passing a stone's throw from the furniture; here the Vibhaṅga defines it as crossing the boundary of an enclosed monastery or the vicinity of an unenclosed one, and Ajahn Brahm writes "leaving a monastery" into his rule line.6​ 7 One word sits underneath it: Brahmāli renders vihāra "dwelling", since in its early use the word means a single dwelling, "like a house", as the Commentary he cites puts it, and only later the whole monastery.8 The rule's place, in other words, is the room where the bedding lies; its departure is from the monastery around it. The difference is taken up under Effort.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    Under this rule a monk leaves the Saṅgha's dwellings fit for the monks who come after him, for the sake of the Saṅgha whose rooms they are. The friends of the group of seventeen did everything together, and together they walked away from the bedding they had spread, until the termites had it. Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm both draw the purpose from that story, a dwelling left so long that its bedding draws pests, and both add a second, which Thanissaro takes from the Vinaya-mukha: a monk long away, with his belongings still spread about a dwelling, keeps the Saṅgha from giving it to anyone else in the meantime.9​ 10

    This rule is the mirror of Pc 14. It shares that rule's three factors, object, effort and intention, and most of its machinery: "having it put away" and "taking leave" are defined as there, but for one exception noted below, and perception of ownership is again no mitigating factor.11​ 12 What follows gives its weight to the three points where the two rules part.

    Object

    The object is bedding: in the Vibhaṅga's list, a mattress, a mat underlay, a bedspread, a floor cover, a straw mat, a hide, a sitting mat, a sheet, a spread of grass or of leaves, but not the bed or bench it lies on.13 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual doubts that the list was meant to be complete, "particularly considering the changes in bedding equipment that have taken place since the time of the Buddha", and takes bedding as "any article used for sleeping on or under, apart from the bed itself".14

    Here the first difference appears: whose bedding it is does not matter. The place decides the offence. Bedding left in a dwelling belonging to the Saṅgha is grounds for the pācittiya; in another individual's dwelling, around a dwelling, in an assembly hall, under a roof cover or at the foot of a tree, for a dukkaṭa; in one's own dwelling, for nothing.15 A bed or bench carried from its place and left in any of these is a dukkaṭa, and, since this rule's departing is a different act from Pc 14's, the penalty holds even in the dry months when Pc 14 allows furniture to be kept under trees.16

    The rule reaches only bedding a monk has set out himself or had set out, not bedding he finds already spread, even as a courtesy to him. Thanissaro reports the Commentary tying the responsibility to the claim on the dwelling: a visitor staying in a dwelling another monk has claimed is not answerable for its bedding until he claims it himself. Thanissaro draws the practical lesson, that a monastery keeping a dwelling for visiting elders does well to have a resident lay claim to it, so that its guests are not made answerable for bedding laid out to honour them.17

    Effort

    The second difference is departing. Here it means leaving the monastery grounds: beyond the wall if it is walled, beyond its vicinity if it is not, which, by Thanissaro's report, the Commentary reckons at two stone's throws, about thirty-six metres from the buildings.18​ 19 The Manual finds "leaves the monastery" clear enough for practical purposes.20 A short trip outside does not count. The protocols before almsround make no mention of this rule, and Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm both read that silence as showing that temporary excursions are not departing.21​ 22

    "Putting away" is fuller than at Pc 14. Thanissaro gives the Commentary's sense of it: returning the bedding to the safe place it was kept before it was spread, the bundle to its clothesline, the mattress to the room it came from. And a monk who has another spread the bedding stays answerable for it even when the other is ordained; the passing of responsibility to an ordained helper that Pc 14 allows does not operate here.23

    Intention

    The third difference is the sharpest: here the intention to return does mitigate, the reverse of Pc 14.24 A monk who leaves his bedding spread, goes out meaning to come back within the allowable time, and comes back as planned has done nothing wrong. The Vibhaṅga's non-offence clauses carry two further cases inside this one: "if he abandons his intention to return, and at that spot informs anyone"; and "if he is obstructed".25 In Thanissaro's account the Commentary reads them as two exemptions governed by the intention to return. A monk who changes his mind on the road can still avoid the offence by sending word back that he is taking leave, and one kept from returning by a flooded river, a king or robbers is exempt by that fact alone.26

    How long may a temporary absence be? The Vibhaṅga sets no limit, and the Commentary only illustrates the clause with a monk who leaves "thinking, 'I will return today'". Thanissaro leaves it to each Community, weighing the two purposes above.27 Ajahn Brahm gives the rule of thumb a monk can carry with him: "Going overnight would then be such a temporary excursion while going for a week would not."28

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • A dukkaṭa for abandoning bedding in a dwelling belonging to an individual, or around a dwelling, in an assembly hall, under a roof cover or at the foot of a tree.29
    • A dukkaṭa for carrying a bed or bench from its place and leaving it in a dwelling or any of those places, even in the dry months when Pc 14 allows furniture to be kept under trees.30​ 31
    • A dukkaṭa where the dwelling belongs to an individual but he perceives it as the Saṅgha's or is unsure, or where it belongs to another individual and he knows it.32

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he departs after putting the bedding away, after having it put away, or after informing a monk, a novice or a monastery worker; if the bedding is obstructed; if he gives up his intention to return and informs someone from where he is; if he is obstructed; if there is an emergency; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.33 Where there is no one to take leave of, the protocol for leaving a monastery supplies the remedy: set the bed on four stones, stack bed on bed and bench on bench, heap the bedding on top, put away the wooden and clay goods, and close the windows and doors.34

    Related Offences

    • Pc 14 — the anchor of this pair, which carries the shared machinery. This rule differs on three points: bedding in a dwelling, not furniture in the open; departing as leaving the monastery, not passing a stone's throw from the item; and the intention to return, which mitigates here and not there.
    • Pc 16, 17 — the two rules that follow turn from the care of the Saṅgha's lodgings to conflict over them: crowding a monk out of his place, and throwing a monk out of a dwelling in anger.

    A note on sourcing

    The Story, the rule, the definitions of bedding and of departing, the permutations and the non-offence clauses are from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation, with his endnote on vihāra, and the recitation line from his Pātimokkha recitation translation. The Manual gives the wider reading of bedding and the practical sense of departing. Thanissaro's BMC1 supplies the three factors, the place-based grading, the reading of departing and of temporary excursions, the Commentary's points on putting away, the vicinity and the claim on a dwelling, all cited through him, the two exemptions inside the intention clause, the question of how long an absence may be with the Vinaya-mukha's point, taken from him, and the protocol for leaving a monastery. Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes gives the purpose and the overnight rule of thumb. The Concise BMC was checked and condenses BMC1 without adding to it. Ñāṇatusita gives the rule translation. According to the purpose of this rule, a temporary excursion would not be so long as to endanger the vacated lodging, such as from termites or rats, nor so long as to inconvenience the resident Sangha… Going overnight would then be such a temporary excursion while going for a week would not."↩︎

    31. Thanissaro, BMC1, Pc 15: "Here again the three factors for a full offense are object, effort, and intention." "Object": "the question of whom the bedding belongs to is not an issue"; a bed or bench left in those places is grounds for a dukkaṭa, and "this penalty applies even during the periods when one is allowed to keep such things under trees"; the rule does not reach "bedding already set out there, even when set out as a courtesy for him"; where a dwelling is kept for visiting elders, "it would be a wise policy for one of the resident bhikkhus to lay claim to it". Perception "is not a mitigating factor here (see Pc 4)."↩︎
