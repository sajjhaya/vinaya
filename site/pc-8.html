    Pc 8

    Story (Vatthu)

    A number of monks who were friends kept the rains residence together on the banks of the river Vaggumudā. That year Vajjī was short of food, the crops affected by a blight that left the heads white and empty, and almsfood was not easy to come by. The monks considered their difficulty and asked themselves how they might live together in peace, keep a comfortable rains, and get almsfood without trouble.

    Some proposed working for the householders in return for support. Others proposed running errands and carrying messages instead. A third group proposed something else again: that they talk up one another's superhuman qualities to the householders — that monk has the first absorption, that one the second, the third, the fourth; that one is a stream-enterer, that one a once-returner, a non-returner, a perfected one; that one has the three true insights, that one the six direct knowledges. In this way, they reasoned, they would have a comfortable rains, live together in harmony, and get almsfood without trouble. This was the plan they settled on.

    The monks did as they had agreed, and it worked exactly as they had hoped. The people of the district thought themselves fortunate to have such virtuous monks keeping the rains among them — no monks so accomplished, they said, had ever stayed with them before — and gave them food and drink in such quantity that they did not keep enough back for themselves, their parents, their wives and children, their servants, or their relatives. Before long the Vaggumudā monks had a good colour, bright faces, clear skin, and sharp senses.

    When the three months were over, the monks put their dwellings in order and set out, as was the custom, to visit the Buddha at the hall with the peaked roof in the Great Wood near Vesālī. The monks arriving from the districts afflicted by the famine were thin, haggard, and pale, with their veins showing; the Vaggumudā monks, by contrast, were well-coloured and bright-faced. The Buddha greeted the new arrivals and asked whether they had kept well, whether their rains had been comfortable and harmonious, and whether they had managed for almsfood without trouble. They told him it had been so, and he asked how. They told him what they had done.

    "But did you really have those superhuman qualities?"

    "Yes, sir."

    The Buddha rebuked them regardless. "How could you, for the sake of your stomachs, talk up one another's superhuman qualities to householders? This will affect people's confidence…" He then laid down the rule.

    Synopsis — Facing a lean rains residence, a group of monks agree to praise one another's genuine spiritual attainments to the laypeople supporting them, prosper on the resulting generosity, and are rebuked by the Buddha even though the attainments were real.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk truthfully tells a person who is not fully ordained of a superhuman quality, he commits an offense entailing confession."1

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu tells an unordained person about his own extraordinary spiritual attainment, he incurs a pācittiya."2

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu report (his own) superior human state to an unordained person, when it is factual, it is to be confessed."3

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Claiming a supernormal attainment to laypeople or novices, thinking it is true, is an offence of Pācittiya."4

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should declare a superhuman state to one who has not been fully admitted [into the bhikkhu-community], when it is a fact, [this is a case] involving expiation."5

    The five renderings sit close together. The recitation's inserted "own" and "extraordinary" gloss what the fuller translations make explicit through the rule's own definitions, and Thanissaro's parenthetical "(his own)" marks whose attainment it is, as the recitation's "own" does. They diverge, quietly, over how far they commit on one word: Ajahn Brahm's "thinking it is true" builds into the rule what Thanissaro argues ("probably") and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual adopts for practicality, that "factual", bhūtasmiṃ, means factual from the bhikkhu's own point of view, not factual in some way nobody could ever verify. That reading is discussed below, under Object.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule keeps a monk's real attainments out of what he tells anyone not fully ordained, so that the path is never made a means of support, for his own practice and the confidence of those who support him. It is the true counterpart to the false claim of Pj 4, and it shares that rule's origin story, so the two rules are best read as a pair. Where the Vaggumudā monks of Pj 4 lied about attainments they did not have, the monks of this rule's own telling are asked directly, "But did you really have those superhuman qualities?", and answer, "Yes, sir." The claims were true. The Buddha rebuked them anyway, and laid down a lesser penalty for the lesser wrong: a pācittiya, not a pārājika. Truth is a defence against Pj 4; it is not a defence against this rule, whose whole concern is that a monk who has genuinely attained something has still done wrong in telling an unordained person so.

    Thanissaro gives the offence two factors: effort, reporting one's own actual attainment of a superior human state, and object, an unordained person, any human being who is not a bhikkhu or bhikkhunī.6 The Manual slices the same ground into three, folding "tells of one's own attainment" and "in a direct manner" together under a single Tells heading and treating Real, that the claim is factually accurate or at least believed to be, as its own separate factor.7 The content is the same either way. I follow Thanissaro's two, the leaner division, and take the Real question under Object, where the word's interpretive weight sits.

    Object

    The claimed attainment must be a genuine superior human state, the same term, and the same list, defined in full at Pj 4: absorption, release, stillness and attainment, knowledge and vision, development of the path, realisation of the fruits, the abandoning of the defilements, a mind without hindrances, and delight in solitude.8 The unordained person addressed is, likewise, anyone who is not a fully ordained bhikkhu or bhikkhunī.9

    Factual is the word carrying the rule's real difficulty, because a bhikkhu's attainment is not the kind of thing anyone else can verify. Thanissaro notes that the texts do not explain it, but his resolution is the only workable one, and the Manual reaches the same practical answer by its own route: bhūtasmiṃ, "factual," must mean factual from the reporting bhikkhu's own point of view: he has genuinely attained the state, or genuinely believes he has.10​ 11 The Manual is candid that this reading is not strictly what the word says, only what practicality requires of it. Thanissaro, and Ajahn Brahm with him, draw the consequence: a bhikkhu who thinks he lacks a state he has actually attained, and knowingly claims it anyway, is not telling the truth as he understands it; he is lying, and the far heavier Pj 4 applies.12

    Effort

    To report means to speak directly of one's own attainment, naming the state outright ("I have attained the first absorption"); whether an accepted idiom counts as a direct claim is disputed, and the dispute belongs to Pj 4.13 An indirect claim ("the monk who lives in this dwelling enters jhāna at will") is a lesser dukkaṭa, not the full offence. The Commentary extends the rule to gestures, so that a bhikkhu who merely nods when a layperson asks whether he has any noble attainment has made the claim itself.14 The Vibhaṅga's permutation series raises the same "whose understanding?" question that runs through Pj 4: the case of a monk who means to claim one attainment but, by a slip of the tongue, names another. Here as there, the sources divide: Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga and his endnote read the decisive understanding as the listener's (a monk who names the wrong state incurs the full pācittiya if the listener grasps that a claim was made, a dukkaṭa if not), while Thanissaro reads it as the speaker's (a pācittiya if the monk realises his own slip, a dukkaṭa if he does not).15​ 16 The penalty ladder is identical either way, and the fuller argument belongs to Pj 4, where the same participle is the rule's central and deepest dispute. What is settled at Pc 8, and unlike Pj 4, is the neighbouring case of an indirect claim: the bhikkhu's own understanding of what his words imply is not at issue, and the lesser dukkaṭa applies whether he grasps the implication or not.17 Intention plays no part in any of this: whether a bhikkhu's motive for mentioning a genuine attainment is skilful or not, the offence is the same.18

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The Vibhaṅga allows only two exemptions: reporting one's attainment truthfully to another bhikkhu or bhikkhunī, and the original instigators of the rule.19 There is no exemption here for insanity, an omission the Commentary notices and tries, not very persuasively, to explain: a person who has attained any noble path can never become insane, it argues, and one who has attained jhāna can go insane only after losing it, and so has nothing "present in himself" to claim. Thanissaro points out that this sits awkwardly against the Vibhaṅga's own examples, which include claims made in the past tense ("I have attained the first jhāna"), and offers the simpler explanation that the standard exemptions were not repeated here because they are already given in full under Pj 4.20

    The first exemption, that telling another bhikkhu one's genuine attainment carries no offence, needs beside it a case from the Vinītavatthu attached to Pj 4, since it shows that "no offence" is not the same as "always wise." Venerable Mahā Moggallāna, descending Vulture Peak, smiled at something he had seen. Asked why, he told his companion Lakkhaṇa that this was not the moment to answer, and that the question should be put to him in the Buddha's presence. Only there did he explain: he had seen a being suffering the fruit of a past cruelty, immersed in a pit of excrement and feeding on it. Bhikkhus complained that Moggallāna was boasting of a superior state. The Buddha confirmed the vision was genuine and that Moggallāna had committed no offence. But Moggallāna's own instinct, to hold the report until his teacher was present to frame it, is the more useful lesson than the bare fact of the exemption. There are situations, as the bhikkhus' first reaction shows, where relating even a permitted truth serves no purpose at all.21

    A related dukkaṭa outside this rule attaches to displaying rather than reporting an attainment: the Buddha compared a bhikkhu who shows off psychic power to laypeople to a woman exposing herself for a wretched coin, and a rule in the Khandhaka accordingly makes such a display, to a layperson specifically, a dukkaṭa in its own right.22

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • A dukkaṭa for an indirect claim to a genuine attainment, naming the state but referring to oneself only implicitly, rather than a direct one.23
    • A dukkaṭa where a bhikkhu means to claim one state but names another by mistake, and the listener does not understand him to have made a claim (on Thanissaro's reading, where the monk does not notice his slip); the full pācittiya applies where the listener does understand.24

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence in reporting a genuine attainment to another bhikkhu or bhikkhunī, or for the rule's original instigators.25

    Related Offences

    • Pj 4 — the false counterpart of this rule and the source of its shared origin story, and its definition of "superior human state." A specified false claim is a pārājika; a genuine one, told to the unordained, is this pācittiya. The mismatch between that shared origin story (where the monks praised one another's attainments) and both rules' narrower reach to a claim about oneself is discussed there.
    • Pc 1 — a monk who praises another bhikkhu's genuine attainment to an unordained person commits no offence under this rule, which reaches only a claim about oneself; if the praise is invented rather than genuine, it is a deliberate lie and falls under Pc 1 instead.

    Related Suttas

    • MN 105 — some monks declare final knowledge "because they overestimate themselves": a claim honestly believed may still be wrong, and "factual" is read, under Object, from the monk's own understanding.26
    • SN 17.5 — the gain the monks of the Story prospered on, and the monk "whose mind is overcome and obsessed by gain, honour, and praise", likened to a dung beetle proud of its dunghill.27
    • DN 11 — "seeing the danger of such miracles, I dislike, reject and despise them": a superhuman power is not a means to win lay confidence, the display the Khandhaka penalises beside this rule.28

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone. The rule text, the definitions of "monk," "a person who is not fully ordained," and "a superhuman quality" (with its full sub-list), the permutation series (including the meaning-to-say-one-thing cases and the gross-hinting dukkaṭas), and the non-offence clause are from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga; the condensed recitation line from his Pātimokkha recitation translation. The two-factor analysis, the "factual means factual from the bhikkhu's own point of view" resolution, the direct/indirect distinction and its dukkaṭa, the Commentary's gesture extension, Thanissaro's speaker-reading of the slip-of-the-tongue permutation (set against Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation and endnote 306, which read the same case via the listener, as at Pj 4), the settled indirect-claim point (understanding not an issue, unlike Pj 4), the "intention is not a factor" point, the Commentary's insanity puzzle and Thanissaro's resolution of it, the Moggallāna Vinītavatthu case (drawn from the Vinītavatthu attached to Pj 4, not to this rule's own), and the Cv.V.8.2 display-of-powers cross-reference are from Thanissaro's BMC1. The Manual supplies the three-factor division and its own independent path to the same "factual from one's own point of view" reading, debated openly as such. Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes, vol. 2 confirms the shared origin story, the summary contrast with Pj 4 and the case of telling a fellow monastic, and the Cv.V.8.2 comparison to a woman exposing herself for a coin, without adding independently. The Concise BMC was checked and confirms the factor analysis and non-offence clauses without adding. Ñāṇatusita supplies the fifth rendering and was checked for the rule's own Pāli (bhūtasmiṃ, āroceyya), raising no fresh grammatical question. MN 105 and SN 17.5 are quoted from Bhikkhu Bodhi's translations, and DN 11 from Walshe's. The class transcript for this rule (session 021, 26 April 2012) was checked and adds no material beyond what the Manual and BMC1 already supply — the class discussion closely follows the Vibhaṅga's own definitions without extending them.

Displaying psychic powers to anyone who is not a lay person is no offense.↩︎

    23. Thanissaro, BMC1, Pc 8, "Effort": "To report, says the Vibhaṅga, means to speak directly of one's own attainments … i.e., to claim that the state is present in oneself or that one is present in the state. To speak indirectly of one's own attainments — e.g., 'The bhikkhu who lives in this dwelling enters jhāna at will' — entails a dukkaṭa. According to the Commentary, gestures fall under this rule as well. Thus, if a bhikkhu who has attained stream-entry nods when asked by a lay person if he has any noble attainments, his nod would fulfill the factor of effort here."↩︎

Brahmāli's endnote to the passage (Pc 8, endnote 306) defends the listener-reading of paṭivijānanta on the same logic as his Pj 4 endnote: grammatically the participle "could refer to either the speaker or the listener," but since a monk who knowingly makes a claim already understands his own overall meaning, the understanding the rule makes decisive "must refer to the listener," a reading he supports with Sp 1.219 and parallels at Pj 1 and Sg 3.↩︎
