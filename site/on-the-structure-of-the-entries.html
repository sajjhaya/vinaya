    On the Structure of the Entries

    Every entry in this book takes its rule through the same sequence of sections, set out here so that any entry can be followed at a glance. The entries run from the four Pārājikas to the ninety-two Pācittiyas; the Pāṭidesanīya, Sekhiya and Adhikaraṇasamatha rules are not drafted here, and a reference to one (Pd, Sk, As) points to the rule itself, not to an entry. The order is the tradition's own, the order in which it approaches a rule: from the occasion of its origin, to the wording in which it was laid down, to the analysis that settles how it applies, and, throughout, to a plain accounting of the sources. The sequence is uniform, and where an entry departs from it the departure is deliberate and marked. A heading that answers to a part of the Vibhaṅga gives that part's name in brackets, in the terms the Samantapāsādikā uses for the parts of the text: the vatthu, the occasion; the mātikā, the rule itself; the padabhājanīya, the word-by-word analysis; the āpattibheda, the grades of offence; the anāpatti, the cases of no offence; and, at the two Aniyata rules, the pariccheda, the determination.

    Story. Each rule was laid down in response to a particular occasion, and the Story gives that occasion as the Vibhaṅga records it, in the level, reported manner of the canonical narrative. The origin account often shows a rule's purpose more clearly than the later analysis does: where a rule reaches far, or stops short, the reason can often be seen already in the occasion that prompted it, and so the Story comes first. A single Synopsis sentence follows, stating the substance of the rule.

    Translation. The rule is then given in more than one rendering: Brahmāli's translation of the full Vibhaṅga formulation and of the condensed recitation, Ṭhānissaro's, and, where it exists, Ajahn Brahm's. They do not always agree, and the points where they differ are instructive. A short paragraph takes up those differences: a term one translator construes as a noun and another as an adjective, a clause dropped from the recitation, a technical word whose sense is disputed. Here the Pāli itself is opened. Where the divergence is one of grammar or philology, Ñāṇatusita is cited, since his treatment of such matters is the most detailed available. Behind these paragraphs stands the Digital Pali Dictionary, which separates a word's senses by the canonical passages in which each is attested, instead of giving one flat gloss for them all.1

    Explanation. This is the heart of the entry. It opens with a single paragraph on what the rule is for, drawn from the Story and from the reasons the Vinaya gives for laying down its training rules. The rest is organised around the factors of the offence, the conditions that must all be present for the full offence to be incurred. Most rules have two or three: an object, an effort, and in some rules a matter of perception or intention. The Explanation takes each factor in turn, giving first the Vibhaṅga's definition and then the tradition's determination of the hard cases. The practical force of a rule lies in its factors: what it does and does not cover, and where its line falls, is settled there and seldom anywhere else.

    Disputed passages. Where the sources disagree, I have neither hidden the disagreement nor settled it by preference. Such a passage is set apart, introduced by the word Disputed, and states the question at issue. The positions then follow, each with the reasoning it rests on and, where such ground exists, the textual basis on which the question might be decided. These passages record the tradition's reasoning in full, so that the reader can weigh the arguments and not simply receive a conclusion. Holding two well-founded readings at once, and seeing why each is held, teaches more in the long run than a single answer would.

    Derived Offences and Exemptions. After the main analysis, two further sections gather the lesser penalties attaching to the rule (a dukkaṭa or a thullaccaya incurred in the partial or mistaken case) and the non-offence clauses, which set out the circumstances in which the act is done but no offence is incurred: the first offender, the one who is insane, and whatever particular exemptions the rule allows. In certain procedural rules a Resolution Grid stands in place of the Derived Offences section, a table being the clearer form there.

    Related Offences. No rule stands alone. This section draws the connections between a rule and its neighbours: the rule it takes its procedure from, the rule it hands over to where its own scope ends, and the rule it divides a common subject with. The aim is to show the Pātimokkha as a connected structure, each rule clarified by those beside it.

    Related Suttas. Where a discourse bears on the rule, because the origin story names or echoes it, because it teaches the danger or the virtue the rule guards, or because a modern source links the two, it follows, with a line on what it teaches a monk about this rule.

    Threads. Where a rule belongs to one of the characters or themes traced in the appendices, a line names them, so that the rule can be read with the others in its thread.

    A note on sourcing. Each entry closes with a paragraph stating which source supplied which part of it (the Story, the rule text, the analysis of the factors, the several positions in a disputed passage) and marking where the treatment is my own synthesis and not the conclusion of any single source. Accuracy in these matters is a first commitment of the book, and this note is how that commitment is kept open to inspection.

    Footnotes. Every substantive statement is supported by a footnote. The footnotes name the source and the section, and often give the operative words directly, so that the primary text can be consulted without guesswork. A key of the form np18-thanissaro-object names the rule, the source, and the point at issue. Two conventions hold throughout: quoted Pāli, and the decisive wording of a source, are given verbatim, not paraphrased; and where a position is the conclusion of a single source and not the settled view of the tradition, the footnote says so.

    A single entry can be consulted on its own, and the book will serve that use. But the rules were not laid down as a list, and they are best read as what they are: one training, determined case by case, each rule standing in a definite relation to the rest. Read that way, the Pātimokkha shows the care with which its provisions were framed.
