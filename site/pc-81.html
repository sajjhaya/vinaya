    Pc 81

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Rājagaha in the Bamboo Grove. Venerable Dabba the Mallian, who was the assigner of dwellings and the designator of meals, had a robe that was worn out. Just then the Saṅgha had obtained a robe, which it gave to Dabba.1

    The monks from the group of six complained and criticised it, "The monks are diverting the Saṅgha's material support according to friendship."2

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised them, "How could the monks from the group of six give out a robe as part of a unanimous Saṅgha and then criticise it afterwards?" The Buddha questioned them, established that it was true, rebuked them, and stated the rule.3

    Synopsis — The Saṅgha met, decided together to give its one robe to the monk who had been doing its work, and the group of six took part in the decision and then said outside it that the robe had gone to a friend. The rule reaches the second act and not the first.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk gives out a robe as part of a unanimous Sangha and then criticizes it afterwards, saying, 'The monks are diverting the Sangha's material support according to friendship,' he commits an offense entailing confession."4

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu, as part of a united Saṅgha, gives a robe to a saṅgha official, but then criticises the act, he incurs a pācittiya."5

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu, (acting as part of) a united Community, give robe-cloth (to an individual bhikkhu) and later complain, 'The bhikkhus allocate the Community's gains according to friendship,' it is to be confessed."6

    Brahm

    "Having participated in a Sanghakamma which decided to give a robe to an officer of the Sangha, and later criticising the decision is an offence of Pācittiya."7

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu, having given a robe [-cloth] [together] with a united community, should afterwards engage in criticising [saying]: 'The bhikkhus allocate gain belonging to the community according to familiarity,' [this is a case] involving expiation."8

    The renderings part over who receives the robe. The Pāli says only that it was given, cīvaraṁ datvā; the recipient's office enters through the word-analysis, which defines complaining afterwards as complaining when robe-cloth has been given to a fully ordained monk the Saṅgha has appointed to one of its offices.9 Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga keeps the Pāli's silence; his recitation builds the office into the rule line, "gives a robe to a saṅgha official", and Ajahn Brahm does the same.10​ 11 The offence as the Vibhaṅga states it needs an appointed recipient, and that is taken up under Object. The accusation turns on yathāsanthutaṁ, "according to friendship" in Brahmāli and Thanissaro, "according to familiarity" in Ñāṇatusita.12 The Vibhaṅga defines it by five kinds of personal tie, from friendship to being a co-pupil, so the charge is not that the Saṅgha favoured one crony but that it gave along personal lines of any kind.13

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A monk who takes part in a Saṅgha's decision stands behind it afterwards, and the rule guards that: the harmony of a Saṅgha that decides together, and the confidence of the lay people whose gifts it is deciding about. In the Story the Saṅgha received a robe and gave it to Venerable Dabba the Mallian, whose own robe was worn out and who did the Saṅgha's work of assigning dwellings and designating meals. The group of six were part of the Saṅgha that gave it, and afterwards said the monks were diverting the Saṅgha's support according to friendship; the Buddha's rebuke begins, "This will affect people's confidence."14 A monk who thought the gift wrong had the meeting to say so in. Thanissaro notes that a protest during the announcement stops such a gift, and that the rule exists to keep a monk from calling it favouritism after he has taken part in it.15

    Thanissaro gives the offence two factors, object and effort.16

    Object

    The Saṅgha must be unanimous: "belonging to the same Buddhist sect and staying within the same monastery zone."17 For the monk this means, in Thanissaro's gloss, that he is in affiliation with the Saṅgha that gave the cloth, and was either in the meeting or had given his consent to it.18 The robe is robe-cloth of one of the six kinds, no smaller than the size that can be assigned to another, which Brahmāli puts at eight by four standard fingerbreadths.19

    The recipient must be a fully ordained monk the Saṅgha has appointed to an office: assigner of dwellings, designator of meals, or distributor of congee, fruit, fresh foods or minor requisites. A requisite other than cloth, or a gift to a monk not appointed, or to one not fully ordained who holds such an office, appointed or not, makes the complaint an offence of wrong conduct.20 Such offices are seldom filled by formal act today, and Ajahn Brahm suggests, as he does at Pc 13, that a monk informally agreed upon for the work be treated as appointed for this rule.21

    Behind the rule stands what a Saṅgha may give away at all. Its heavy goods, monasteries and their land, dwellings, furniture, metal vessels and tools, building materials, may not be given away by anyone; a gift of them does not take effect, and the giver commits a serious offence.22 Light articles may go to individuals, normally through an appointed official, and Thanissaro reads this rule as showing that the Saṅgha as a whole may also give one to a single member, as a way of rewarding an official who has been especially helpful. Apart from the kaṭhina, his example is a fine piece of cloth presented whole instead of being cut up and shared.23 That is the origin story read as a precedent, and it is the right reading of it.

    Perception

    On a legitimate act the grid gives the full offence however he perceives it: as legitimate, doubtfully, or as illegitimate. On an illegitimate act it gives an offence of wrong conduct where he takes it for legitimate or is unsure.24 The Vibhaṅga does not say which act it means, the appointment of the official or the gift to him, and the sources divide over the last cell.25

    Disputed — whether a monk who perceives an illegitimate act as illegitimate, and complains, incurs an offence of wrong conduct or none.

    The Mahāsaṅgīti and Oldenberg's PTS print no offence, and Brahmāli translates it so; his Pātimokkha Manual makes it the rule's exception, no offence where the act was invalid and he perceives it so.26​ 27 Ajahn Brahm puts the exemption in terms of belief: no offence in criticising an act "believing that the act was performed incorrectly according to the Vinaya, or that the community was indeed acting out of favouritism", without saying whether the act must in fact have been invalid.28 Thanissaro reads an offence of wrong conduct. He takes the act to be the appointment, following the Commentary, which he reports as referring this rule's grid to Pc 13, and the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī, which he reports as making the validity of the appointment a factor; and he notes that the Thai and Sri Lankan editions of the Canon, and the PTS edition of the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī, read a dukkaṭa in this cell.29 The Concise BMC follows him.30 The Mahāsaṅgīti and the PTS print the reading Thanissaro sets aside, and I leave the choice open: a monk following the Manual will find no offence where Thanissaro finds a lesser one, and neither reading touches the course both teachers commend, which is to object at the meeting.

    Effort

    The effort is the complaint made afterwards, in the words of the origin story: that the monks are diverting the Saṅgha's support according to friendship.31 Thanissaro reads it as any expression of personal displeasure with the Saṅgha's distribution, and draws a boundary the Vibhaṅga does not: to accuse the Saṅgha of having acted improperly, against the rule or with an incomplete assembly, belongs under Pc 63, not here.32

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The exemption, for criticising one who regularly acts out of favouritism, ill will, confusion or fear, concerns the recipient, not the act, and it is why the rule does not silence an honest objection. Thanissaro draws out the timing: before the act, the objection under Pc 13 halts it; after it, the objection is that the act was invalid from the start, which Pc 63's exemption covers.33 Where a monk now meets this question is less often a meeting than a store-room. Communities seldom give a robe by formal act today, the kaṭhina apart, and the store-man distributes. Brahmāli placed criticism of the store-man under Pc 13, where a monk may criticise an officer who acts from greed, hatred, delusion or fear; but "if you simply criticise him because you don't like him … that's when you have a problem."34 Where an act has been made, the counsel to keep is Ajahn Brahm's: it would have been better "to have raised one's objections at the meeting before the Sanghakamma had been concluded."35 The robe went to Dabba with the group of six in the room, and that was the time to speak.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — complaining where another requisite has been given to an appointed official.36
    • Dukkaṭa — complaining where robe-cloth or another requisite has been given to a fully ordained monk not appointed by the Saṅgha, or to one not fully ordained who holds such an office, appointed or not.37
    • Dukkaṭa — where the act was illegitimate and he perceived it as legitimate, or was unsure; and, on Thanissaro's reading, where he perceived it as illegitimate.38​ 39
    • Dukkaṭa (Parivāra) — for the effort, while criticising; the pācittiya follows when it is done.40
    • Thullaccaya (Khandhaka) — for giving away the Saṅgha's heavy goods at all, which is not this rule.41

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence "if he criticizes one who regularly acts out of favoritism, ill will, confusion, or fear, saying, 'What's the point of giving it to him — he'll ruin it or use it inappropriately;'" nor if he is insane, or the first offender.42

    Related Offences

    • Pc 13 — complaining about a Saṅgha-appointed official: where, Thanissaro reports, the Commentary refers this rule's grid; the source of its exemption; and where criticism of a store-man belongs.43​ 44
    • Pc 79, 80 — complaining after giving consent, and leaving a meeting without giving it: the two rules before this one, with the same concern for a Saṅgha's completed decision.
    • Pc 63 — reopening a settled legal issue: where, for Thanissaro, a complaint that the act was improperly done belongs.45
    • Pc 82 — diverting to an individual what was intended for the Saṅgha: the next rule, also about the Saṅgha's gains.
    • Kd 16:15 — the five kinds of heavy goods a Saṅgha may not give away.46

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register, with the complaint, interrogation and rebuke in the standing summary form; Ajahn Brahm's retelling supplies a grudge and a reason for the gift that the Vibhaṅga does not state, and they are not merged. The Manual, the recitation, the class (session 031, 23 July 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were all checked. The Commentary and the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī are given as Thanissaro reports them. Ajahn Brahm cites the Vinaya-mukha at this rule; nothing here rests on it.
