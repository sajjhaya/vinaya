    Pc 56

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying in the Bhagga country at Susumāragira in the Bhesakaḷā Grove, the deer park. During winter, the monks were warming themselves after setting fire to a hollow log. Heated by the fire, a black snake came out of the log and attacked the monks. The monks ran here and there.

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised them, "How could those monks light a fire to warm themselves?" The Buddha questioned them, established that it was true, rebuked them, and laid down the first ruling: a monk who lights a fire to warm himself, or has one lit, commits an offence entailing confession.

    At one time a number of monks were sick. The monks looking after them asked, "I hope you're bearing up? I hope you're getting better?" They answered, "Previously we lit a fire to warm ourselves, and then we were comfortable. But now that the Buddha has prohibited this, we don't warm ourselves because we're afraid of wrongdoing. Because of that we're not comfortable." The Buddha allowed a sick monk to light a fire to warm himself, or to have one lit, and amended the rule.

    Soon afterwards the monks did not light lamps, small fires, or saunas because they were afraid of wrongdoing. The Buddha allowed a fire to be lit if there was a suitable reason, and amended the rule a second time.1

    Synopsis — The rule is laid down against a fire that produced a snake, and is then amended twice by monks who were afraid to light anything at all. What survives is narrow: a fire lit for warmth by a monk who does not need the warmth.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk who is not sick lights a fire to warm himself, or has one lit, except if there is a suitable reason, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a physically comfortable bhikkhu lights a fire to warm himself, or has it lit, except with an appropriate reason, he incurs a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu who is not ill, seeking to warm himself, kindle a fire or have one kindled—unless there is a suitable reason—it is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Not being ill, lighting a fire for warmth is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu who is not ill, desiring to warm [himself], should light a fire or should have [it] lit, except with an appropriate reason, [this is a case] involving expiation."6

    The renderings agree, and two words in them need a note. The rule's first word is agilāno, "not ill", and the Vibhaṅga defines it at once as one "who is comfortable without a fire".7​ 8 Brahmāli's recitation line builds the definition into the rule, "a physically comfortable bhikkhu", where the other renderings, his own Vibhaṅga translation among them, keep "not sick" or "not ill". It is the more useful line. A reader given "not ill" looks for a diagnosis; a reader given "physically comfortable" looks at how cold he is, which is what the word-analysis asks. The other word is joti, which the Vibhaṅga glosses as aggi, fire, Ñāṇatusita's "a fire"; Brahmāli renders the gloss "flames are what is meant".9​ 10​ 11 On his English the gloss matches Thanissaro's ruling, under Effort, that a heater without a flame is outside the rule.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A fire lit for comfort gathers monks into an easy circle, and this rule protects a monk's endurance, and the quiet of the monks around him, from the pleasures of the fireside. Thanissaro finds the purpose in the sutta on the five dangers of fire, the last two of which, in Bodhi's rendering, are that "it promotes fondness of company; and it conduces to pointless talk".12​ 13 The rule was laid down against a fire lit in a hollow log, which drove out a black snake, and was amended twice by monks too afraid of wrongdoing to light anything at all.14

    Thanissaro gives three factors: object, that one is not ill; effort, lighting a fire or having one lit; and intention, to warm oneself.15 The suitable reasons wait for Non-offences, where the Vibhaṅga lists them.

    Object

    To be "not ill" is to be comfortable without the fire, and what one believes about one's own state does not soften the offence: a monk who is in fact comfortable, and lights a fire for warmth, commits the pācittiya under all three perceptions.16 Thanissaro draws the consequence that on a chilly day "one should be very sure that extra warmth is necessary for one's health".17 Ajahn Brahm turns it into geography. A monk in a cold country needs heating against colds and even pneumonia, while monks in India or Thailand can usually bear a chilly morning, so that a fire there is lit for pleasure.18 Brahmāli reads "comfortable" generously, so that a monk who feels cold while sitting in meditation is not comfortable, and then hands the judgment back to the monk: if he needs the heat to be comfortable it is fine, and if it is "pure indulgence" it is not.19

    Effort

    For a modern heater, Thanissaro rules on the letter: turning on the flame of a gas heater is lighting a fire, and a solar or electric system, with no flame, is not.20 Brahmāli met the question in a monastery whose huts had gas heaters with a pilot light, and answered it from the object, not the effort, as a matter of whether the monk needs the heat; he would not distinguish an electric heater either, since a monk turns either on when he is too cold, and not otherwise.21​ 22 The two answers do not conflict. Thanissaro is answering whether an offence is incurred, and Brahmāli what a monk should do.

    Tending a fire already lit is a different matter. The Vibhaṅga gives an offence of wrong conduct for putting back a burning piece of wood that has fallen out.23

    Disputed — whether adding new fuel to a fire that is already burning is lighting it.

    Thanissaro holds that "adding new fuel to a fire—according to the Commentary—is grounds for a pācittiya", and the Concise BMC repeats him.24​ 25 Ajahn Brahm reproduces that sentence in a passage he takes from Thanissaro, but in his own words gives only an offence of wrong conduct for stoking a fire somebody else has built, citing the Vibhaṅga's clause on the fallen brand.26 The Vibhaṅga itself names only the brand put back, and reaching new fuel either way is a step beyond it: Thanissaro's step rests on the Commentary he cites, and Ajahn Brahm's on an analogy with the brand. The texts do not settle it, and my counsel is the safer course: a monk adds no fuel to a fire he could not have lit for himself.

    Having a fire lit means asking another to light it, and on Brahmāli's rendering one request is one pācittiya, however many fires the other lights.27 Thanissaro reads a pācittiya for the order and another when the fire is lit, and one manuscript in Oldenberg's edition reads only a dukkaṭa at the clause.28​ 29 Because the clause is about asking, Thanissaro sees "apparently room for kappiya-vohāra", a suggestion worded so that it is not an express command.30

    Intention

    The fire must be lit to warm oneself, "wanting to heat himself".31 A fire lit for any other purpose is outside the rule: a lamp, or a fire to boil water, burn dead leaves or fire an alms bowl, in Thanissaro's examples, though Pc 10 and Pc 11 may still reach the soil and the plants it burns.32

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    Beside sickness, the Vibhaṅga allows warming oneself at a fire lit by another or over flameless coals; lighting a lamp, a small fire or a sauna when there is a suitable reason; and an emergency.33 The small fire, Brahmāli's endnote gives from the Commentary, is one made "to fire a bowl, to make sweat, etc."34 The sauna has a story behind it. Jīvaka, the Buddha's physician, asked for saunas because the monks of Vesālī were often sick, and the Buddha allowed them.35 In Thanissaro's report of the Commentary, the emergency is a snake-bite, being surrounded by robbers, or being disturbed by non-human beings or beasts of prey.36

    Disputed — what the coals clause allows, and so whether a monk may warm himself at a fire without flame.

    Ajahn Brahm reports, from the Vinaya-mukha, a reading of the rule as fire safety, on which a fire in a "safe" fireplace or a flameless charcoal fire would be no offence, resting on the sauna and on this clause, and he rejects both supports.37 The sauna was allowed for health and not for warmth, which is what the Khandhaka says; and the clause, he holds, means warming oneself "over raked-out embers", an old fire made by another, since "even a coal fire, if left unattended, can burn down a monk's wooden hut".38​ 39 His second argument turns on a rendering. Vītaccitaṅgāraṁ is "raked-out coals" in Thanissaro's translation, which supports him, and "flameless coals" in Brahmāli's, which does not: on Brahmāli's reading the clause concerns the coals' having no flame, not who raked them.40​ 41 Thanissaro's and Brahmāli's renderings agree that lighting a fire for warmth is the offence and that warming oneself at another's dying fire is not. They part over coals left from a fire a monk lit himself for some other purpose, and the texts do not settle that, nor can I; a monk who warms himself only at coals from another's fire is safe on both.

    The rule was laid down over a fire that drove a snake out of a hollow log, but what it guards is quieter than safety: a monk's willingness to be a little cold. A heater with no flame in it is no fire in the rule's sense, and a monk who turns one on only when the cold is real, and otherwise puts on another layer, keeps what the rule was made to keep.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — putting back a burning piece of wood that has fallen out.42
    • Dukkaṭa — lighting a fire for warmth when sick, but not perceiving oneself as sick, or unsure of it.43
    • Dukkaṭa — setting fire to a forest (Kd 15:32.1); the same passage allows a counter-fire when a forest fire threatens the dwellings.44

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he is sick; if he warms himself over a fire lit by another; if he warms himself over flameless coals; if he lights a lamp, a small fire, or a sauna, when there is a suitable reason; if there is an emergency; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.45

    Related Offences

    • Pc 10, 11 — digging soil and damaging plants: Thanissaro notes that a fire lit for an allowable reason may still incur these.46
    • Pc 57 — bathing too often: the neighbouring rule, amended in the same way for monks afraid of wrongdoing, and with the same definition of illness as not being comfortable without.

    Related Suttas

    • AN 5.219 — the five dangers of fire, the last two that "it promotes fondness of company; and it conduces to pointless talk": the fireside circle a fire lit for warmth gathers, which this rule keeps a monk from kindling.47

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; the three substories are given in full and the two superseded rulings are summarised. The snake is "a black snake" in Brahmāli, where Thanissaro has "a cobra" and Ajahn Brahm "a dark poisonous snake"; the Story keeps Brahmāli's. Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual, the recitation, his two endnotes, the class transcript (session 028, 12 June 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1 and BMC2, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were checked, with the Khandhaka passages on saunas and forest fires and AN 5.219 in Bodhi's translation. Ajahn Brahm cites the Vinaya-mukha; what is carried is his own argument against it. Two class uses are kept: Brahmāli's generous reading of "comfortable", and his treating gas and electric heaters alike. The Commentary is known here only through Thanissaro and Brahmāli's endnote. Transcript quotations are in the house spelling, with disfluencies removed.
