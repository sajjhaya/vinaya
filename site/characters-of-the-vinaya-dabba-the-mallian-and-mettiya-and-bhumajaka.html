    Dabba the Mallian, and Mettiya and Bhūmajaka

    Dabba the Mallian realised perfection at the age of seven, and, having nothing further to do, wondered how he could be of service to the Saṅgha. He offered to assign the dwellings and designate the meals, and the Saṅgha appointed him by a formal motion.1 The Vibhaṅga shows how he did it. He put the experts on the discourses together so that they could recite to one another, the experts on the Monastic Law together so that they could discuss it, the meditators together so that they would not disturb one another, and the gossips and the bodybuilders together too, "thinking, 'In this way even these venerables will be happy.'" Monks who arrived after dark he led to their dwellings with his finger glowing, and some arrived late on purpose to see it.2 Brahmāli calls it "the entertaining story of Dabba the Mallian", and it is hard to read otherwise.3

    The darker side of the story belongs to two newly ordained monks, Mettiya and Bhūmajaka, who "had little merit" and so got the inferior dwellings and meals.4 One day a householder who served fine food learned they were to be his guests, seated them in the gatehouse and had them served broken rice and porridge, though they had been too excited to sleep properly the night before. They decided Dabba was to blame, and when the nun Mettiyā came and greeted them three times, they told her what to say to the Buddha: "Venerable Dabba the Mallian has raped me."5 When the charge failed, they named two goats they saw mating on their way down from the Vulture Peak after Dabba and the nun, and reported that they had now seen it for themselves.6 Those are Sg 8 and Sg 9.

    At Pc 13 the same two complained to other monks that Dabba assigned dwellings and meals "based on favoritism", and when complaining was forbidden, said it within the hearing of other monks instead.7 Brahmāli counts them among the group of six.8 At Pc 81 it is the group of six who took part when the Saṅgha gave Dabba a robe because his own was worn out, and afterwards complained that the robe had gone by friendship.9

    Three times the Buddha asked Dabba before the Saṅgha whether he remembered doing what the nun said, and three times Dabba answered, "Sir, you know what I'm like." The Buddha would not accept it: "Dabba, the Dabbas don't give such evasive answers. If it was done by you, say so; if it wasn't, then say that." Only then did Dabba say plainly, "Since I was born, sir, I don't recall having sexual intercourse even in a dream, let alone when awake."10 The Buddha did not clear an arahant on his own knowledge of him. He made him answer, and that answer, Brahmāli notes, is how "resolution through recollection" became one of the seven ways of settling a legal issue.11 How an accusation is heard has its own piece in Themes; here it is enough that an arahant was held to the same procedure as anyone else.

    The four rules together make a ladder going down. A charge of a pārājika with no grounds (Sg 8), a charge of a pārājika on a pretext (Sg 9), complaint about a Saṅgha official's work (Pc 13) and criticism of a Saṅgha decision in his favour (Pc 81): the grievance is the same each time, and only the weapon gets smaller. It seems reasonable to read in this what serving the Saṅgha costs. Whoever assigns its dwellings, divides its meals or receives its gifts will at some time be resented by someone who got less, and the Vinaya does not leave him to bear it alone. Each step of the ladder is an offence. What Mettiya and Bhūmajaka called favouritism was the work of a monk who assigned dwellings "according to their character", and took such care over it that even the gossips and the bodybuilders were put where they would be happy. A monk who is given work for the Saṅgha has Dabba to measure it by: he gave each monk what suited him, and when he was accused and told to answer plainly, he did.

    1. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Sg 8, origin story.↩︎

    2. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Sg 8, origin story: "Dabba assigned dwellings to the monks according to their character"; "and for the gossips and the bodybuilders, thinking, 'In this way even these venerables will be happy.'"↩︎

    3. Brahmāli, Introduction to the Monks' Analysis, part I, on Ss 8: "Bu Ss 8 starts with the entertaining story of Dabba the Mallian who is said to have become an arahant at the age of seven and then spent his life in service to the Sangha."↩︎

    4. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Sg 8, origin story: "At that time the monks Mettiya and Bhūmajaka were only newly ordained. They had little merit, getting inferior dwellings and meals."↩︎

    5. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Sg 8, origin story.↩︎

    6. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Sg 9, origin story: "Previously we spoke of what we had heard, but now we've seen Dabba copulating with the nun Mettiyā."↩︎

    7. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 13, origin story.↩︎

    8. Brahmāli, Introduction to the Monks' Analysis, part I, on Ss 8: "the two bad monks Mettiya and Bhūmajaka, who were members of the notorious group of six monks, developed a grudge against Dabba." The sources differ on the pair. Thanissaro, BMC1, Sg 8, has the "followers of Mettiya and Bhummaja" and calls the two "among the leaders of the group-of-six bhikkhus"; Ajahn Brahm, Vinaya Notes, vol. 1, Saṅghādisesa 8, The Story, does not name them and has "four of the 'Group of Six Monks'".↩︎

    9. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 81, origin story: "The monks are diverting the Sangha's material support according to friendship."↩︎

    10. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Sg 8, origin story; the exchange is repeated at Sg 9.↩︎

    11. Brahmāli, Introduction to the Monks' Analysis, part I, on Ss 8: "The Buddha asks Dabba whether this is true, which he denies, and the Buddha then exonerates him on the basis of his perfect memory. This is how 'resolution through recollection', sativinaya, becomes established as the second of the seven 'principles for settling legal issues'."↩︎
