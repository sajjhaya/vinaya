    Pc 80

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. The Saṅgha had gathered on some business. The monks from the group of six were busy making robes and so they gave their consent to one among them. When the Saṅgha was ready to do the legal procedure for which it had gathered, it put forward a motion.1

    That monk from the group of six thought, "This is just how they do legal procedures against us one by one, but against who will you do this one?" — and without giving his consent, he got up from his seat and left.2

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised him, "When the Saṅgha is in the middle of a discussion, how could that monk get up from his seat and leave without giving his consent?" The Buddha questioned him, established that it was true, rebuked him, and stated the rule.3

    Synopsis — The same afternoon as the rule before, and the same monk. Having learnt that complaining afterwards is an offence, he tries the one move still open to him: he removes himself, and with himself the assembly's completeness. The rule closes the last door.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "When the Sangha is in the middle of a discussion, if a monk gets up from his seat and leaves without first giving his consent, he commits an offense entailing confession."4

    Condensed recitation translation — "When the Saṅgha is deciding an issue, if a bhikkhu, aiming to stop an official act, departs without giving his consent, he incurs a pācittiya."5

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu, when deliberation is being carried on in the Community, get up from his seat and leave without having given consent, it is to be confessed."6

    Brahm

    "Leaving a meeting of the Sangha during a Sanghakamma, not giving one's Chanda, is an offence of Pācittiya."7

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu, when an investigatory discussion is going on in the community, not having given [his] consent, having got up from [his] seat, should depart, [this is a case] involving expiation."8

    The renderings differ most over vinicchayakathā: "a discussion" in Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga, "deciding an issue" in his recitation, "deliberation" in Thanissaro, "an investigatory discussion" in Ñāṇatusita, and "during a Sanghakamma" in Ajahn Brahm, the narrowest of them.9​ 10 The spread matters, because it is the question of when the rule applies, and the Vibhaṅga's own definition, which runs from the announcing of the matter to the end of the announcement, favours the wide renderings; it is taken up under Object.11​ 12 The recitation alone writes the intention into the rule line, "aiming to stop an official act", importing it rightly from the word-analysis.13 And chanda, "consent", is the word of the rule before in a different use; that is taken up under Effort.14

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    Once a Saṅgha act has begun, a monk in the meeting belongs to its decision until it is made, and if he must go he leaves his consent behind him; that loyalty to his assembly is the rule's concern. The Story shows a monk walking out mid-act. The one monk of the group of six who came, seeing that a motion was under way and guessing whom it was for, got up and left, and took the assembly's completeness with him. Lay people would not long trust a Saṅgha that cannot finish its own business, and the rebuke names that confidence.

    The rule is the second half of the afternoon told at Pc 79. Thanissaro explains why the move worked: the monk had no right to protest an act against himself, but a Saṅgha may not act against a monk who is not in its midst, and any act is invalid while a monk within the territory is neither in the meeting nor has given his consent.15 Ajahn Brahm puts the result in a sentence: one monk was now inside the zone but outside the meeting, without having given his consent, and the assembly was incomplete.16

    Thanissaro gives the offence four factors: object, perception, intention and effort.17

    Object

    The object is a legitimate Saṅgha act in progress. The Vibhaṅga fixes the period: from the moment the matter has been announced and is still undecided, or the motion has been put forward, or the announcement is under way.18 For an accusation, Thanissaro reports, the Commentary sets a later start, the matter counting as announced only when accuser and accused have each stated their case and an examiner has been appointed, and Ajahn Brahm gives its first half, both sides having stated their case, for disputes as well as accusations, as the Vibhaṅga's meaning.19​ 20 Thanissaro sees the consequence — an accused monk could escape by leaving after the charge and before stating his case, which would "open a loophole" — and he follows the Vibhaṅga.21 He is right to, and the monk of the Story, who left after the motion had been put forward, is caught on either start.22

    Perception

    The grading is Pc 79's, cell for cell. A legitimate act perceived as legitimate is the pācittiya; an act about which he is unsure, or an illegitimate act he takes for legitimate, an offence of wrong conduct; an act he perceives as illegitimate, whether it was or not, no offence.23​ 24

    Intention

    The monk leaves in order to break the act. The Vibhaṅga gives his thought three ways — how may this procedure be disturbed, how may it be done by an incomplete assembly, how may it not be done.25 A monk who leaves for any other purpose is outside the rule, and the non-offence clause, below, names the purposes.26

    Effort

    The Vibhaṅga allots the penalties by distance. Leaving with that thought is an offence of wrong conduct; being in the process of going beyond arm's reach of the gathering is another; once he has gone beyond it, the pācittiya.27​ 28 Arm's reach, hatthapāsa, is given as about 1.25 metres by Thanissaro, Ajahn Brahm and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual.29​ 30​ 31 It is the same distance that makes a monk present at a meeting, read from the other side:32 to pass beyond it is to take oneself out of the assembly.

    What he fails to give is consent. The consent here, as Ñāṇatusita notes, is consent to the outcome, given by a monk who has to leave, not the consent an absent monk sends.33 Thanissaro reports that the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī adds a further condition, that the monk remain within the territory, and rejects it: the Vibhaṅga does not mention it, and on it a monk facing an act could escape the offence by leaving the territory as well as the meeting.34 Thanissaro's effort factor, arm's reach passed whatever happens after, is the working rule, and I follow it.

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The seven exemptions fall into three about the act, three about the body and one about coming back, that he leaves meaning to return and not in order to invalidate the procedure.35 That last clause needs reading to its end, for a monk who steps out meaning to return once the motion has failed for want of him is inside the rule. The first three are the striking ones, since Thanissaro reads them as exempting even a monk who leaves precisely in order to stop the act.36 A monk who walks out of an act he believes to be wrong is not this rule's concern. For the monk who simply has to go, both modern teachers give the same small courtesy: Thanissaro's "if possible, it is best to give one's consent before going", and Ajahn Brahm's picture of him handing it quickly to the monk sitting next to him, "so that the Sangha is not inconvenienced".37​ 38

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — leaving with the intention of disturbing the act; another while going beyond arm's reach; the pācittiya once arm's reach is passed.39
    • Dukkaṭa — leaving where the act was legitimate but he was unsure of it, or was illegitimate but he took it for legitimate or was unsure.40

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he leaves because he thinks there will be quarrels or disputes in the Saṅgha; because he thinks there will be a fracture or schism; because he thinks the procedure will be illegitimate, done by an incomplete assembly, or done against one who does not deserve it; because he is sick; because he has to care for someone sick; because he needs to relieve himself; or with the intention to return, and not because he wants to invalidate the procedure; or if he is insane, or the first offender.41 The perception grid adds a monk who perceives the act as illegitimate, whether it was or not.42

    Related Offences

    • Pc 79 — complaining after giving consent: the first half of the same episode, with the same perception grid and the same three grounds concerning the act. The machinery of Saṅgha acts and of a complete assembly is set out there.43
    • Pc 81 — complaining after a unanimous Saṅgha has given robe-cloth: where the Vibhaṅga defines a unanimous Saṅgha.44
    • Pc 63 — reopening a settled legal issue: another rule whose perception grid, like this one's and Pc 79's, grades down to no offence.
    • Pc 35 — eating after being satisfied: where Ajahn Brahm treats the measure of arm's reach.45
    • Adhikaraṇasamatha 1 — the face-to-face verdict: where Thanissaro points for the principle that a Saṅgha may not act against a monk who is not in its midst.46
    • Cancellation of the Pātimokkha (Khandhaka) — not an offence but the Saṅgha's available response: under Cv.IX.3 a monk who commits this offence may have his Pātimokkha cancelled, which, Thanissaro notes, gives the Community the opportunity "to look into his attitude and to take further disciplinary actions if it sees fit".47

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register, with the monk's thought quoted and the complaint, interrogation and rebuke in the standing summary form; the interrogation is in the singular, the rule being about the one monk who came. Ajahn Brahm's retelling calls the monk "outraged", which the Vibhaṅga does not; it is not carried.
