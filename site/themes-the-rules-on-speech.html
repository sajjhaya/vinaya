    The Rules on Speech

    When the group of six argued with the good monks and insulted them "about their caste, name, family, occupation, profession, illnesses, physical traits, defilements, and offenses", the Buddha rebuked them and told a story. A brahmin of Takkasilā bet a thousand coins that his ox Nandivisāla could pull a hundred carts tied together, and when the carts were yoked he said to the ox, "Go, you fraud! Pull, you liar!" The ox did not move. The brahmin lost his money, and the ox asked him, "But why did you disgrace me by calling me a fraud when I'm not?" The second time, for two thousand coins, the brahmin said, "Go, good ox! Pull, good ox!", and the hundred carts moved. The verse that ends the story begins, "One should say what's pleasant, / Never what's unpleasant", and the Buddha drew from it a line about himself: "Even at that time, monks, reviling and insulting was unpleasant to me. How then could reviling and insulting be pleasant now?"1 That is Pc 2.

    Several of the things the group of six insulted the monks about may well have been true. A monk does have a caste, a family and an illness, and he may have an offence. The insult lay in what the words were used for. The same holds through the rules on speech, and it is clearest where two rules share one story. In a year of famine in Vajjī the monks spending the rains on the Vaggumudā River praised one another's attainments to the householders, who fed them so well that they kept back too little for their own families. When the monks came to the Buddha after the rains, well fed among thin monks from the famine districts, he asked them, "But did you really have those superhuman qualities?" At Pj 4 the monks answered, "No, sir," and the Buddha told them it would be better for their bellies to be cut open with a butcher's knife.2 At Pc 8 the monks answered, "Yes, sir," and he rebuked them in nearly the same words: "How could you for the sake of your stomachs talk up one another's superhuman qualities to householders?"3 A false claim for oneself is an offence entailing expulsion; a true one, told to the unordained, is still an offence entailing confession: "If a monk truthfully tells a person who is not fully ordained of a superhuman quality."

    Pc 9 carries the same pattern to another monk's fault. Upananda the Sakyan had committed a grave offence, asked the Saṅgha for probation and received it, and so sat on the last seat at a meal given by lay supporters. The group of six told the lay followers why he sat there.4 What they said was true, and the rule forbids it: a monk may not tell a person who is not fully ordained about a monk's grave offence, "except if the monks have agreed".5 Upananda's story is told in Characters of the Vinaya. At Pc 3 the same six carried tales between quarrelling monks to divide them, and that rule belongs to The harmony of the Saṅgha.

    None of this lowers the standing of truth. Pc 1 begins with Hatthaka the Sakyan, who in debate with the followers of other religions asserted what he had denied and denied what he had asserted, "evaded the issues, lied, and made sham appointments", and who defended himself to the monks: "These monastics of other religions should be beaten, whatever it takes! They shouldn't be allowed to win."6 The rule that followed makes a deliberate lie an offence entailing confession. The Buddha's teaching to Rāhula puts the same thing as an image. He showed his son the little water left in a vessel and said, "Even so little, Rāhula, is the recluseship of those who are not ashamed to tell a deliberate lie."7

    Taken one at a time, the rules seem to pull in two directions. Pc 1 forbids saying what is false, and Pc 8 and Pc 9 forbid saying certain things that are true. Read together, they ask two questions of what a monk says, whether it is true and what it will do, and a monk's words have to pass both. The suttas put the two questions in one place. In MN 58 the Buddha sorts speech by whether it is true, whether it is beneficial and whether it is welcome. Speech that is untrue he does not utter; speech "true and correct but unbeneficial" he does not utter either; and of speech "true, correct, and beneficial, but which is unwelcome and disagreeable to others", he says, "the Tathāgata knows the time to use such speech."8 Pc 8 and Pc 9 mark two places where true words do harm: an attainment spoken of to the unordained, and another monk's offence carried to the laity. Pc 2, it seems fair to say, is the third. A true word, said to hurt, is an insult.

    In MN 21 the Buddha lists the ways others may speak to a monk, "timely or untimely, true or untrue, gentle or harsh, connected with good or with harm, spoken with a mind of loving-kindness or with inner hate", and asks him to meet all of them with the same mind: "we shall utter no evil words; we shall abide compassionate for their welfare, with a mind of loving-kindness, without inner hate."9 The rules on speech set the floor under that training. Nandivisāla had been called a liar falsely, and he would not pull. He pulled when he was spoken to truly and kindly.
