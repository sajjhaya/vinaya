    Pc 48

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. King Pasenadi of Kosala was marching out with the army, and the monks from the group of six went to see it. When King Pasenadi saw the monks coming, he summoned them and said, "Venerables, why have you come here?"

    "We wish to see the great king."

    "What's the use of seeing me finding pleasure in battle? Shouldn't you see the Buddha?"

    And people complained and criticised them, "How can the Sakyan monastics go to see the army? It's our misfortune that we must go out with the army for the sake of our livelihoods and because of our wives and children."

    The monks heard the complaints of those people, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised those monks. The Buddha questioned them, established that it was true, rebuked them, and laid down a preliminary ruling: a monk who goes to see an army commits an offence entailing confession.

    Soon afterwards a certain monk had a sick uncle in the army. The uncle sent a message to that monk: "I'm with the army and I'm sick. Please come, venerable. I want you to come." Knowing that the Buddha had laid down a rule against going to see an army, that monk thought, "I have a sick uncle in the army. What should I do now?" And he told the Buddha. Soon afterwards the Buddha gave a teaching and addressed the monks: "Monks, I allow you to go to the army when there's a suitable reason." He then restated the rule with the exception in it.1

    Synopsis — Six monks walk out to watch a king march to war, and the king himself rebukes them: there is no use in looking at a man who delights in battle when the Buddha is there to be looked at. The people who march because they have families to feed say the same thing from below. Then a monk with a sick uncle in the camp finds he cannot go, and the rule is amended to allow a visit made for a suitable reason.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk goes to see an army, except if there is a suitable reason, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu goes to see an army in action, except with an appropriate reason, he incurs a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu go to see an army on active duty, unless there is a suitable reason, it is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Going to see a large military force on active duty, or in camp, unless there is a suitable reason, is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should go to visit an army in action; except with an appropriate reason, [this is a case] involving expiation."6

    The renderings differ over two words. Ñāṇatusita alone has the monk go "to visit" an army, since dassanāya, "for seeing", often carries that sense; it falls the same way as the rule's "go to see", and the offence still lies in the seeing, as discussed under Effort.7 The other is uyyutta, the participle that qualifies the army. Its literal sense is "set out", and the renderings run from Thanissaro's "on active duty" through "in action" to Ajahn Brahm's "on active duty, or in camp".8 The word-analysis decides between them: the army "has left the inhabited area and is either encamped or marching", so an army in camp satisfies the word, and the participle carries having gone out, not fighting.9 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual says so directly: "in action" "includes an army in camp".10 His full translation drops the qualifier altogether and says only "an army", and his endnote gives the reason: armies in the Buddha's day were reserves, mobilised for war, whereas professional armies are "always more or less mobilized".11 What the word means for a modern army belongs with the object.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule keeps a monk from taking war as his entertainment, for the sake of his own path and of the regard of those who must fight. Battle, real and practice, was a spectacle in the Buddha's day, watched by citizens "much as people at present watch football games", Thanissaro observes.12 The king himself rebuked the group of six: "What's the use of seeing me finding pleasure in battle? Shouldn't you see the Buddha?" And the people who marched said it from below, for they went out with the army only for their livelihoods and for their wives and children.13

    Thanissaro gives three factors: object, effort and intention, the last being the absence of a suitable reason.14 The object needs the most care, since a modern army is not the Vibhaṅga's.

    Object

    The object is an army that has gone out. The Vibhaṅga defines army as the classical fourfold array of elephants, horses, chariots and infantry, and gives the manning of each unit: twelve men to an elephant, three to a horse, four to a chariot, and four men with arrows in hand to an infantry unit.15 One division of the fourfold army is grounds for an offence of wrong conduct only, down, in Thanissaro's report of the Commentary, to "one armed archer"; an army not on duty, which the Commentary illustrates with a king's pleasure trip, is no ground at all.16​ 17

    On how small a force the rule reaches, the sources agree that the army is a large one. Thanissaro argues that the Vibhaṅga's fourfold army comes close to a modern field army with its full array of divisions, and by the Great Standards a similar large force of navy, marines or air force counts as well; so a full field army or its equivalent is grounds for a pācittiya, and "a regiment, a division, or even one armed soldier" for an offence of wrong conduct, matching the permutations' one division. The Concise BMC follows him.18​ 19​ 20 The Manual reads the army as "any large military force", and Ajahn Brahm's rule line has "a large military force", adding that the rule is now extended to "all military forces".21​ 22​ 23 In class Brahmāli took the Vibhaṅga's army to be "a substantial army", though he set no clear-cut limit; whether a smaller unit brings a monk under the rule "is debatable, maybe not", but he advised against going to see any army unit for amusement all the same.24

    An army on active duty now does not always leave the city. Thanissaro therefore counts soldiers at work on base or off as on duty, but not the housing on a base where their families live, since the Vibhaṅga defines the army as away from home; Ajahn Brahm reaches the same line.25​ 26

    Effort

    The offence is in the seeing, and the going must be purposeful. Nothing in the definitions says so; it is read off the first three non-offence clauses, each a case of seeing without going: from within a monastery, when the army comes to where one is, when one meets it on the road.27​ 28 The Vibhaṅga grades it in three steps: an offence of wrong conduct on the way, a pācittiya where he stands and sees, and a fresh pācittiya each time he passes beyond the range of sight and sees it again.29​ 30 Perception does not mitigate, the pattern of Pc 4.31

    Intention

    The exception for a suitable reason is given no content by the word-analysis, and Ajahn Brahm concludes that "one must judge for oneself".32 The origin story gives one reason, the sick uncle, and the Manual takes it as its instance: "visiting an ill relative". Thanissaro adds an invitation to receive alms or to give a Dhamma talk.33​ 34 It seems reasonable, then, to read the exception as wide in content and narrow in spirit, since the one thing it will not cover is the thing the rule was laid down for.

    What the rule reaches now is the question a modern monk actually meets, and Brahmāli worked through it in class. Going down to the port to look at warships, or to an air show, falls under it, though nothing is being fought. An army that appears on the news one is already watching is "the army coming to you rather than you going to the army", the rule's own exemption, while a war film watched for its war action, he judged, comes under the rule.35 Thanissaro reaches the same line from the Khandhaka's rule against going to see shows, which he extends to screens by the Great Standards: an offence to turn on a device for entertainment, or to go "out of his way" to watch one already on.36​ 37 The list of shows in the Brahmajāla Sutta's section on virtue ends with the military displays of Pc 50.38

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — for each step on the way to see an army; the pācittiya falls where he stands and sees, and again at each renewed seeing.39​ 40
    • Dukkaṭa — on the way, on seeing and on each renewed seeing, where the object is one division of the fourfold army only.41
    • Dukkaṭa — where it is not an army but he perceives it as one, or is unsure of it. Where it is an army, all three perceptions carry the full pācittiya.42

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he sees it while standing in a monastery; if the army comes to where the monk is standing, sitting, or lying down; if he sees it while walking in the opposite direction; if he has a suitable reason; if there is an emergency; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.43

    Thanissaro gives the Commentary's definition of the emergency, danger to one's life or celibacy, and he and Ajahn Brahm both read the clause as permission to take shelter with an army.44​ 45

    Related Offences

    • Pc 49 — staying more than three nights with an army: the rule for the monk who has a suitable reason and has gone.
    • Pc 50 — going to a battlefield, a troop review, a massing of troops or a troop inspection while staying with an army: this rule's mirror.
    • Kd 15:2.6 — going to see dancing, singing and music: a dukkaṭa, the general rule against shows of which this one is a special case.46

    Related Suttas

    • DN 1 §1.13 — among the shows the Buddha refrains from attending, "sham-fights, parades, manoeuvres and military reviews".47

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; King Pasenadi's speeches, the people's complaint and the uncle's message are verbatim. Ajahn Brahm's telling differs in three places (a battle in progress, an excuse imputed to the group of six, and soldiers as complainants), and the Story follows the Vibhaṅga. Brahmāli's recitation, the Manual and the class transcript (session 028, 12 June 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1 and BMC2, Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were checked, and the Khandhaka's rule against shows and the Brahmajāla Sutta, in Walshe's translation. One class use is kept, Brahmāli's modern applications and his view on how small a force the rule reaches; his other points are carried by the written sources. The Commentary is known here only through Thanissaro.
