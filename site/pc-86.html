    Pc 86

    Story (Vatthu)

    At one time the Buddha was staying in the Sakyan country in the Banyan Tree Monastery at Kapilavatthu. An ivory-worker had invited the monks who needed needle cases to ask for one. And the monks asked for many needle cases. Those who had small needle cases asked for large ones, and those who had large needle cases asked for small ones.1

    The ivory-worker was so busy making needle cases for the monks that he was unable to make goods for sale. He could not make a living for himself, and his wives and children suffered. People complained and criticised the monks, asking how the Sakyan monastics could have no sense of moderation and ask for many needle cases, when the ivory-worker was so busy making them that he could not make goods for sale, could not support himself, and his wives and children were suffering.

    The monks heard the complaints, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised those monks. The Buddha rebuked them and laid down the rule.2

    Synopsis — An ivory-worker offers the monks needle cases, they ask for so many, and exchange them so freely, that he can no longer make anything to sell and his family goes without; the rule that follows forbids a needle case of bone, ivory or horn and orders any such case destroyed.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk has a needle case made from bone, ivory, or horn, it is to be destroyed, and he commits an offense entailing confession."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu makes a needle-case of bone, ivory, or horn, or has one made, it should be destroyed and he incurs a pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu have a needle box made of bone, ivory, or horn, it is to be broken and confessed."5

    Brahm

    "Having a needle case made out of bone, ivory or horn is an offence of Pācittiya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should have a needle-case made, which is made of bone, or made of ivory, or made of horn, [this is a case] involving expiation with breaking up [the needle-case]."7

    The word the renderings handle differently is bhedanaka, which is not part of the offence but an adjective attached to the penalty: the pācittiya here is a breaking kind of pācittiya. Brahmāli and the recitation turn it into a separate sentence — "it is to be destroyed, and he commits an offense entailing confession" — Thanissaro keeps it adjectival and paired, "it is to be broken and confessed", and Ñāṇatusita renders the grammar visibly, "involving expiation with breaking up". Ajahn Brahm drops it from the rule line and states it below instead; the penalty is the same in all of them.8

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A monk's requisites are to be humble and asked for in moderation, and the rule guards both: his own contentment, and the livelihood and goodwill of the people who make what he uses. An ivory-worker invited the monks to ask for needle cases, and they asked for so many, and exchanged small for large and large for small so freely, that he could make nothing to sell and his family went without; people complained that the monks had no sense of moderation.9 The rule that followed says nothing about quantity and everything about material, and two readings of the gap are on offer that do not conflict. Brahmāli's is that a needle case of these materials simply is a luxury, and a monk is meant to carry something humbler, wood or metal perhaps.10 Thanissaro, quoting the Vinaya-mukha, reports a broader one: that the Buddha was stopping a fad, the kind that arises when some requisite becomes fashionable among monks to the point of inconveniencing the people who supply it, and that senior monks should stop the equivalent fads of their own day.11 The second reading makes the story and the rule agree, and it is the part of the rule that transfers: the material is obsolete and the behaviour is not.

    This is the first of seven rules (Pc 86–92) built on the same machinery: a monk makes a requisite, or has one made, in a form he is not allowed; the making is a lesser offence, the getting is the full one, and the object must be put right before the offence can be confessed. The machinery is set out here in full and restated in a sentence at Pc 87–92. Thanissaro gives three factors, object, effort and intention, and two of them carry permutations.12

    Object

    A needle case made of bone, ivory, or horn. The Vibhaṅga's definitions are as short as definitions get: bone is any kind of bone, horn any kind of horn, and ivory is elephant tooth.13 A needle case as such is perfectly allowable: the Khandhakas allow a needle tube against rust, which Ajahn Brahm takes as the allowance for a needle case, and Pc 60 counts a needle case among the requisites a monk has, so the rule is about the material alone.14​ 15

    Effort

    Making it, or having it made, is an offence of wrong conduct for the effort; the full offence falls when the monk receives the finished case, and the case must be destroyed before he confesses.16 The Vibhaṅga then runs the four combinations that decide nothing and are worth stating once, since they recur at Pc 87–92: he finishes what he began himself, he has others finish what he began, he finishes what others began, he has others finish what others began. All four are the full offence.17 The series closes the gap a monk might otherwise find between his own hands and someone else's. The wrong conduct for having one made falls when the maker acts, not at the request, which Thanissaro, tentatively, extends at NP 26 to every rule of this kind.18

    Intention

    For his own use. Making such a case for someone else, or having one made for someone else, is wrong conduct, not the full offence, and so is receiving one that another person made and then using it.19​ 20

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The non-offence clause does something unusual and easy to misread. Instead of naming materials from which a needle case may be made, it names seven objects that may be made of bone, ivory or horn: a toggle, a fire kindler, a buckle, an ointment box, an ointment stick, a machete handle, and a water wiper.21 Read as a closed list it would suggest that these seven things and nothing else may be made of these materials, which is the wrong way round. Thanissaro reads it as illustrative, because the Khandhakas allow many other items of bone, ivory and horn besides, and he adds that for three of the seven, the fire kindler, the machete handle and the water wiper, this clause is the only passage in the Canon that says so.22 Ajahn Brahm gives the Khandhaka list: a knife handle, a thimble, an earwax remover, a waistband buckle, robe tags, ointment boxes and spatulas, nose spoons and steam tubes.23 Only the needle case is barred.

    The complaint did not begin with the monks, and it was not made about the Dhamma. It began with people who saw a craftsman who had stopped being able to sell anything, and his wives and children going without, and the Saṅgha heard about its own conduct from them.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • An offence of wrong conduct for the effort of making such a needle case or having one made, incurred before and separately from the full offence, which falls on receiving it.24
    • An offence of wrong conduct where a monk makes such a case, or has one made, for the benefit of someone else.25
    • An offence of wrong conduct where a monk obtains a case made by someone else, not at his instigation, and uses it.26 Ajahn Brahm notes that the Vibhaṅga does not say the case must be destroyed in this last instance, and thinks it appropriate that it should be; the suggestion is his own.27

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if it is a toggle; if it is a fire kindler; if it is a buckle; if it is an ointment box; if it is an ointment stick; if it is a machete handle; if it is a water wiper; if he is insane; if he is the first offender.28

    Related Offences

    • Pc 87 — a bed or bench with over-long legs; the same make-obtain-and-put-right machinery, applied to a measurement instead of a material.
    • Pc 60 — hiding another monk's requisites, which names the needle case among them and so shows that an allowable needle case is a normal possession.29
    • NP 22 — asking for a new bowl when the old one is barely worn; Thanissaro notes that the origin story here echoes it, and the fault in both is immoderate asking that falls on a layman.30
    • Pc 47, NP 6 — the other rules in which an invitation to ask for requisites is abused or exceeded, the shape of this rule's origin story.
    • NP 26 — getting cloth woven by an unrelated weaver: where Thanissaro sets out when an offence for having something made falls, for this rule and the rest of its kind.31
    • Pc 88, Pc 89, Pc 90, Pc 91, Pc 92 — the rest of this chapter's requisite rules, each pairing a limit with an order to put the object right.

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; the complaint is given twice in the source, once by the laypeople and once by the monks of few desires, and both are kept because the second is what brought the matter to the Buddha. Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual, the recitation, the class (session 032, 30 July 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were all checked. The principle about fads reaches this book only through Thanissaro, who quotes the Vinaya-mukha, which this book does not use directly.
