    Pc 66

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time a certain group of travellers was about to go west from Rājagaha.1 A monk said to those people, "Let me travel with you."

    "But we're smuggling goods."

    "That's your business."2

    The customs officers heard about that group of travellers. They then blocked the road, caught the group, confiscated the goods, and asked that monk, "Venerable, why are you knowingly travelling with a group of thieves?" And they detained him.

    After being released, that monk went to Sāvatthī, where he told the monks what had happened. The monks of few desires complained and criticised him: "How could a monk knowingly travel by arrangement with a group of thieves?" They told the Buddha, who questioned him and rebuked him: "Foolish man, how could you do this? This will affect people's confidence." A monk who knowingly travels by arrangement with a group of thieves, even just to the next inhabited area, commits an offence entailing confession.3

    Synopsis — A monk asked to travel with a caravan, was told it was smuggling, said that was their affair, and was detained with it when the customs officers blocked the road. The rule that follows is the third of the four travelling rules and the only one whose companions are defined by what they are doing rather than who they are.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk knowingly travels by arrangement with a group of thieves, even just to the next inhabited area, he commits an offense entailing confession."4

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu travels on a country road by arrangement with a group of travellers he knows includes thieves, he incurs a pācittiya."5

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu knowingly and by arrangement travel together with a caravan of thieves, even for the interval between one village and the next, it is to be confessed."6

    Brahm

    "By arrangement, going on a journey with people knowing them to be thieves is an offence of Pācittiya."7

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu, having made an arrangement, should knowingly travel together on the same main road with a company of thieves, even [if] just the distance between villages, [this is a case] involving expiation."8

    The renderings divide on whether the thieves are the company or are in it. Theyyasattha joins theyya, theft, to sattha, a travelling company, and Ñāṇatusita gives it as "a caravan of thieves, … a caravan (of travellers intent on) theft".9 Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm make the whole party the object; Brahmāli's recitation makes it a party "he knows includes thieves".10 The difference does change what the rule asks, for on the narrower reading a monk could travel by arrangement with a party he knows to be smuggling so long as some of its members are not; the question is taken up below, under Object. The rest of the rule line is Pc 27's vocabulary, as Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual says in so many words, and the recitation drops antamaso gāmantarampi, "even just to the next inhabited area", as it does at Pc 67; the reading of that phrase belongs to Pc 27.11​ 12

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule guards a monk's good name, and with it the confidence of the people who see whom he travels with. The monk of the origin story stole nothing and carried nothing, but when the road was blocked he was taken with the smugglers, and the officers' question — why was he "knowingly travelling with a group of thieves?" — is the question anyone would ask. The Buddha's rebuke names the same cost: "This will affect people's confidence."13 The monk lacked judgement about company, not honesty, and he was told what the party was doing before he answered "That's your business."

    This is a lean mirror, and the anchor is Pc 27. The family is four rules — travelling by arrangement with a nun (Pc 27), in a boat with a nun (Pc 28), with a group of thieves (here) and with a woman (Pc 67) — and the Vibhaṅga gives them the same machinery: the specimen dialogues that define an arrangement, the requirement that both parties speak, the dukkaṭa attached to the arrangement itself, the travel as arranged, and the count by inhabited areas or, in the wilderness, by distance. All of it is set out at Pc 27.14 Thanissaro gives the offence three factors: object, perception and effort.15 Effort is Pc 27's; the other two are this rule's own.

    Object

    The Vibhaṅga defines the thieves by what they are doing: they may have done their deed or not yet done it, and they are "stealing from the king or smuggling".16 Thanissaro gives the Commentary's gloss on robbing the king, cheating the government in one way or another, and draws the modern line: "any person or group of people smuggling or trading in contraband goods."17

    How many thieves make a group, none of the texts say. Thanissaro argues that since the Vibhaṅga consistently speaks of them in the plural, "it would appear that at least two thieves are needed".18 Brahmāli, meeting the argument in class, allowed that a Pāli plural need not always be read as one, and declined to rest anything on the count: he could not imagine that the company of one thief was "all that much better" than the company of two, and even if it fell outside the rule, it was "kind of dodgy."19 His own renderings point the same way. His Vibhaṅga has "a group of thieves", but his recitation has a party that includes them, and his endnote on the ways of knowing speaks of "the thieves or one of them" telling the monk.20​ 21 I follow the recitation in reading the rule to cover a party one knows contains thieves as well as a party of thieves, since the word-analysis defines the thieves and not the mixture and so excludes neither; the argument is mine, and he gives the rendering without arguing it.

    The company need not be on foot. Brahmāli applied the rule to travel by car or by plane.22 The rule's own "knowingly" still sets the limit: the monk must know that the party includes a thief. Of friends who deal in drugs, he took the rule's purpose rather than its definition as the guide: where a group "might get caught by police for doing something illegal", a monk should not be part of it.23 That goes beyond the Vibhaṅga's two property crimes, and it is offered as his advice, not as the rule.

    Perception

    A monk must know, and the grid makes the knowing decisive. He may know it himself, be told by others, or be told by the party; Thanissaro reads the last as one of the thieves telling him.24 A thieving party perceived as such is the pācittiya; if he is unsure, it is an offence of wrong conduct; and if he does not perceive it as thieving, there is no offence at all, however the journey turns out. A party that is not thieving but is perceived as such, or doubted, is an offence of wrong conduct.25 The origin story is careful on this point: the monk is told what the party is doing before he agrees.

    Effort

    The arrangement, the going and the count are Pc 27's. Thanissaro carries over the one distinction a monk needs on the road: a party that starts earlier or later than arranged releases him, but leaving from a different spot or going by a different route does not.26 What excuses is a change in when, not in where.

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The first three clauses are the family's, and Ajahn Brahm gives the first, no arrangement, in the form a monk will meet it: the thieves decide by themselves to follow him, or he happens to be going the same way at the same time.27​ 28 The fourth is the family's too, a single word, āpadāsu, "if there is an emergency".29 Thanissaro fills it out with the case it must mean, "if there are dangers (and the bhikkhu must join the caravan for his safety)", and Brahmāli's Appendix I finds in the word "an immediate danger".30​ 31 A monk who joins a criminal party because the road is otherwise unsafe is inside the clause; one who joins it because it is convenient is not.

    The rule needs so many conditions — a journey, an arrangement, a road between villages — that a monk may go his whole life without meeting it. What he will meet is the origin story's moment: he is told, plainly, what his companions are doing, and has to decide whether "That's your business" is an answer a monk can give.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — for the arrangement itself, before any travelling is done, as at Pc 27.32
    • Dukkaṭa — where the monk proposes and the travellers do not express their agreement, even if they then travel together as he proposed.33​ 34
    • Dukkaṭa — where the party is thieving and he is unsure of it, and where it is not thieving and he perceives it as thieving or is unsure.35
    • Dukkaṭa (Parivāra) — for the effort, while travelling; the pācittiya follows when the travelling is done.36

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence "if he goes, but not by arrangement; if the group has made an arrangement, but he has not expressed his agreement; if he goes, but not according to the arrangement; if there is an emergency; if he is insane; if he is the first offender."37

    Related Offences

    • Pc 27 — travelling by arrangement on a country road with a nun: the family's anchor, which carries the arrangement, the going as arranged, the reading of antamaso gāmantarampi and the distance count.38
    • Pc 28, 67 — travelling by arrangement in a boat with a nun, and on a country road with a woman: the family's other two members.
    • Pj 2 — taking what is not given: the offence the party is committing and the monk is not. His fault is the travelling; he neither takes nor handles anything.39

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register, with the exchange and the officers' question verbatim and the interrogation and rebuke in the standing summary form. One word departs from his translation: he has the party going "south" from Rājagaha, and the Story reads "west" with the Digital Pali Dictionary and Ajahn Brahm (see the footnote at the word). Ajahn Brahm's retelling adds that the monk escaped arrest only because he was a monk, which the Vibhaṅga does not say, and the addition is not carried. The Manual, the recitation, the class transcript (session 030, 16 July 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were all checked; the Concise BMC condenses BMC1 here. The commentaries are known here only as Thanissaro and Brahmāli's endnote report them. The class is cited for Brahmāli's view on a single thief and for two modern applications.

    37. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 66, non-offence clauses, as quoted under Exemptions. The fourth is āpadāsu.↩︎

    38. Thanissaro, BMC1, Pc 66, "Making an arrangement" and "Going as arranged", which cross-refer to Pc 27 throughout. On the going: "If they happen to start out earlier or later than arranged, the bhikkhu incurs no penalty… However, if they leave from a different spot than the one they had arranged or go by a different route, that does not absolve the bhikkhu from the offense."↩︎

    39. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 66, final ruling and definitions. "A group of thieves: thieves who have done their deed or thieves who have not. They are stealing from the king or smuggling." "By arrangement: if he makes an arrangement like this: the monk says, 'Let's go,' and they reply, 'Yes, let's go, venerable;' … then he commits an offense of wrong conduct." His endnote on the third way of knowing suggests "that it refers to the group of travelers (i.e. the thieves or one of them) telling the monk directly."↩︎
