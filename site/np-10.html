    NP 10

    Story (Vatthu)

    While the Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery, a certain government official was a supporter of Venerable Upananda the Sakyan. On one occasion that official sent a robe fund by messenger, saying, "Buy robe-cloth with this fund and give it to Venerable Upananda."

    The messenger went to Upananda and said, "Venerable, I've brought a robe fund for you. Please receive it."

    "We don't receive robe funds, but we do receive allowable robe-cloth at the right time."

    "Is there anyone who provides services for you?"

    Just then a lay follower had come to the monastery on some business. Upananda told the messenger, "This lay follower provides services for the monks." The messenger instructed that lay follower and then returned to Upananda, saying, "I've instructed the lay follower you pointed out to me. Please go to them at the right time and they'll give you robe-cloth."

    Later on that government official sent a message to Upananda, saying, "Please use the robe-cloth. I would like you to use the robe-cloth." When Upananda did not say anything to that lay follower, the official sent a second message saying the same thing. When Upananda still did not say anything, the official sent a third.

    At that time the householder association had made an agreement that whoever came late to a meeting would be fined fifty coins. And now they were having a meeting. Just then Upananda went to that lay follower and said, "I need robe-cloth."

    "Please wait one day, venerable, for today there's a meeting of the householder association. They've made an agreement that whoever comes late gets fined fifty coins."

    Saying, "Give me the robe-cloth today," he grabbed him by the belt. Being pressured by Upananda, the lay follower bought him robe-cloth, and as a consequence he was late for the meeting. People asked him, "Sir, why are you late? You've just lost fifty coins." When that lay follower told them what had happened, they complained and criticized Upananda, "These Sakyan monastics have great desires. They're not content. It's no easy matter to provide them with a service. How could Venerable Upananda not agree when asked by a lay follower to wait for a day?"

    The monks heard the complaints of those people, and the monks of few desires complained and criticized him. After rebuking Upananda in many ways, they told the Buddha, who had the Saṅgha gathered, questioned him, and — when Upananda admitted the deed — rebuked him: "Foolish man, how could you do this? This will affect people's confidence …" He then laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — Upananda points an official's messenger to a lay follower to hold a robe fund, and after the official has three times asked him to use the cloth, grabs the man by the belt on the day of a meeting whose latecomers are fined fifty coins; the man buys the cloth and pays the fine, and the Buddha lays down the rule.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a king, a king's employee, a brahmin, or a householder sends a robe fund for a monk by messenger, saying, 'Buy robe-cloth with this robe fund and give it to monk so-and-so,' and the messenger goes to that monk and says, 'Venerable, I have brought a robe fund for you. Please receive it,' then that monk should reply, 'We don't receive robe funds, but we do receive allowable robe-cloth at the right time.' If that messenger says, 'Is there anyone who provides services for you?' the monk, if he needs robe-cloth, should point out a monastery worker or a lay follower and say, 'They provide services for the monks.' If the messenger instructs that service provider and then returns to the monk and says, 'Venerable, I have instructed the service provider you pointed out. Please go to them at the right time and they'll give you robe-cloth,' then, if that monk needs robe-cloth, he should go to that service provider and prompt them and remind them two or three times, saying, 'I need robe-cloth.' If he then gets robe-cloth, all is well. If he does not get it, he should stand in silence for it at most six times. If he then gets robe-cloth, all is well. If he makes any further effort and then gets robe-cloth, he commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession. If he does not get robe-cloth, he should go to the owners of that robe fund, or send a message, saying, 'That monk hasn't received any benefit from the robe fund you sent for him. Please recover what's yours, or it might perish.' This is the proper procedure."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If money has been placed with a steward to buy a robe for a bhikkhu, that bhikkhu may hint to the steward three times by speech and six times by standing in silence. If he does not get a robe in this way, but he does get one by making further effort, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "In case a king, a royal official, a brahman, or a householder sends a robe fund for the sake of a bhikkhu via a messenger … the bhikkhu … may prompt and remind him two or three times … If he should not produce the robe, (the bhikkhu) should stand in silence four times, five times, six times at most for that purpose … Should he then produce the robe after (the bhikkhu) has endeavored further than that, it is to be forfeited and confessed … 'May you be united with what is yours. May what is yours not be lost.' This is the proper course here."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "When a lay-steward has been entrusted with a fund to be used for a monk's benefit, receiving a requisite from that lay steward through excessive prompting is a nissaggiya pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "Now, if a king or a kings' official or a brahmin or a male householder should convey by messenger a robe-fund for a bhikkhu [saying]: 'Having traded this robe-fund for a robe, clothe the bhikkhu named so and so with a robe,' and if that messenger, having approached that bhikkhu, should say so: 'Venerable Sir, this robe-fund has been brought for the venerable one. Let the venerable one accept the robe-fund!' [then] that messenger should be spoken to thus by that bhikkhu: 'Friend, we do not accept a robe-fund, but we do accept a robe at the right time [when it is] allowable.' … [If through] standing silently for [it] four times, five times, six times at the most, he should have [him] bring forth that robe, it is good; if [through] making effort more than that, he should have [him] produce that robe, [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture. If he should not have [him] produce [it], [then] from wherever the robe-fund may have been brought, there [he] himself can go, or a messenger can be sent [saying]: 'Sirs, that robe-fund which you conveyed for the bhikkhu does not fulfil any need of that bhikkhu. Let the sirs endeavour for [what is their] own. Do not lose what is your own.' This is the proper procedure here."6

    The renderings of the longest rule in the Pātimokkha agree on its procedure, and the condensed ones keep only its core. The recitation reduces it to the steward, the three spoken and six silent promptings and the offence for pressing further, and its "hint" is gentler than Brahmāli's own "prompt", which his appendix ties to a robe already promised; Ajahn Brahm's rendering keeps only the excessive prompting. The full rule's other parts, how the fund is set up and the duty to tell the donor when nothing comes, are not in the condensed lines but bind all the same, and the word the full rule rests on, "prompt", is discussed below, under Effort.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule keeps a monk at a distance from money held for him, money that is not his, and from pressing those who hold it. A government official sent a robe fund for Upananda; Upananda refused the money and pointed out a lay follower who served the monks; and when the official had three times asked him to use the robe-cloth, he went to the lay follower on the day of a meeting whose latecomers were fined fifty coins, refused his request to wait one day, and "grabbed him by the belt". The man bought the cloth and lost the fifty coins, and people complained: "It's no easy matter to provide them with a service."7 Thanissaro gives the purpose of the whole arrangement: its protocols exist to free a monk from the worries that come with buying, selling and money.8

    This is the procedural model of the class. The offence has three factors, object, effort and result, but the first two each carry a procedure: how a fund is properly left with a steward, and how far a monk may press the steward for what the fund is for.9 The penalty waits at the far end of the procedure.

    Object

    A fund for a monk's robe-cloth left with a steward; the Commentary extends it to a fund for any of his requisites.10 The fund is "money, gold, a pearl, or a gem".11

    Setting up the fund. The rule presupposes the Meṇḍaka allowance: people of faith may place gold with a steward to give a monk whatever is allowable, and he may accept what comes of it, though gold and silver themselves are never to be accepted or sought.12 The rule's opening is the model transaction. The monk refuses the money and says he receives allowable robe-cloth at the right time; if the messenger asks whether anyone provides services for him, he may point out a monastery worker or a lay follower, and no more: he may not tell the messenger to give the fund to them, nor say they will keep, trade or buy with it.13​ 14 Ajahn Brahm adds that a donor who already knows the procedure may simply leave the money with the steward, and that a fund the monk has accepted in a way that breaks NP 18 is improperly deposited, and by the Commentary a requisite from it may be used by no monk at all.15

    Whose money it is. The fund never becomes the monk's, nor the steward's. As Thanissaro puts it, money placed with the steward "still belongs to the donor".16 Everything else follows from this, above all the line between telling and commanding.

    Range of application. The Commentary takes the rule to funds of every kind and for every requisite, and to funds for the Saṅgha, a group, or an impersonal purpose such as a building, and Thanissaro, by the Great Standards, agrees.17 A Saṅgha fund, Ajahn Brahm explains, stays the donor's until used, as a personal one does; but a monastery, a Buddhist society or a shrine may own money, so that what is given to one becomes its asset, and a monk, rather than refuse such a gift, tells a competent steward of it.18 A bank may serve as a steward, but a monk may not sign a cheque, an order to pay, even on an account in his own name.19 How far the protocol binds a Saṅgha or impersonal fund, and which stewards it governs, are disputed.

    Disputed — whether the protocol binds a monk dealing with a Saṅgha fund or an impersonal one.

    Ajahn Brahm holds that it binds neither fully. A monk may prompt the steward of a Saṅgha fund as often as he likes, "unlike a Personal Fund", though he may not command it; and a monk the impersonal body has authorised may order the steward of its fund, "Go buy 3 bags of cement".20 Thanissaro applies the protocol to both: with a fund left with the monks' steward, "follow the protocol under this rule", and with a building fund monks are to follow it "when telling the steward of their need for building materials", nor may a monk sign a cheque on an account set up for such a purpose.21​ 22 Neither side cites a text that settles it, and the question is open; to keep the protocol with every fund is our counsel, and it holds whichever side is right.

    Disputed — whether the protocol governs every steward, or only one the monk himself pointed out.

    Thanissaro gives the Commentary's position, which applies the rule only to a steward the monk indicated: with one the donor chose, a monk may prompt any number of times and need not tell the donor if nothing comes, and one indicated by neither is to be treated as an uninvited stranger and asked nothing.23 Thanissaro finds nothing in the Canon to contradict these points and nothing to confirm them, and, by etiquette for the donor's steward and by the Great Standards for all three, would bring every steward under the protocol.24 Ajahn Brahm follows the Commentary for a fund the monk has not been told of: a monk who suspects one may not ask the steward at all, on pain of Pc 47, unless the steward invites him or is a relative.25 The Commentary gives no reasons, Thanissaro observes, and he argues from what the Canon leaves unsaid and from the Great Standards; no text decides between them, and the question stays open. Where the two agree, with a steward the monk himself pointed out, he keeps the protocol; with any other, he should know which reading he is following.

    Effort

    Pressing the steward beyond the allowed promptings.26 When he needs the robe-cloth, the monk may go to the steward and say, "I need robe-cloth", up to three times; he may not tell the steward to give, get, trade or buy it for him, the last two of which would fall under NP 20.27​ 28 He may tell, in short, but not order. If he does order, the article, on Ajahn Brahm's account of the Commentary, is got by dubbicāritatta, wrong procedure, and may not be used by the monk who commanded, though others may use it.29 The rule lets him ask when he needs the robe, and since need is not defined, Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual leaves it to the monk to judge.30 "Prompt" renders codeti, which Brahmāli's appendix narrows at this rule to urging the offer of a robe "that has already been promised": the monk calls in what was given, he does not solicit a gift.31

    If that fails, he may stand in silence in the steward's presence up to six times. He may not sit, take food or teach there, and if he does he loses one standing; if asked why he has come, he says only, "Think about it."32 The Vibhaṅga lets one prompting be exchanged for two standings, so that four promptings leave four standings, five leave two and six leave none, and the Commentary completes the scheme down to twelve standings and no prompting.33​ 34 Promptings are counted by the times he states his need, not by his visits.35

    Result

    He gets the requisite by pressing further. The further effort is wrong conduct, and when he gets the robe-cloth it is to be relinquished, by NP 1's procedure.36

    If nothing comes. Because the money is the donor's, the monk who has used his promptings without result goes to the owners of the fund, or sends word, that he has had no benefit from it: "Please recover what's yours, or it might perish."37 The Commentary makes the failure to do so wrong conduct for neglecting a duty; Thanissaro limits that to a monk who knows whose fund it is, and where many donors share one fund, one need tell only one.38

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    What the non-offence list leaves out matters. Unlike the rules before it, it has no exemption for relatives or for those who have invited the monk to ask, and Thanissaro draws the consequence: even where the donor or the steward is related to the monk or has invited him to ask, he must follow the protocol.39

    Brahmāli tells his class that in his monastery no monk has a personal fund, everything going into a common fund for the Saṅgha and the monastery, and that he has never needed one: with a personal fund, "the defilements tend to take … quite strong roots sometimes and you think of all the stuff that you need".40 The rule allows a fund and then hedges it at every step, and the hedges point the same way.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — for the further effort, before the requisite is got.41
    • Dukkaṭa — receiving the requisite having prompted less than the limit while thinking it more, or being unsure.42
    • Dukkaṭa — by the Commentary, for not telling the donor when nothing comes.43

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he prompts three times and stands six times; if he prompts less than three times and stands less than six times; if it is given without prompting; if the owners prompt and then it is given; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.44

    Related Offences

    • NP 8, NP 9 — the robe-fund rules with the donor still holding the fund.
    • NP 18 — accepting money, which a fund must not breach in being set up.
    • NP 20 — trade, which the forbidden "Trade me" and "Buy me" would enter.
    • Pc 47 — the invitation to ask; for Saṅgha and group funds the Commentary sorts the cases by where the money is: with the monks' steward this rule, with the donor's own workers any number of promptings, with the donor himself Pc 47; Thanissaro would bring the donor's workers under this rule too.45
    • Pc 84 — valuables found in a monastery.

    Related Suttas

    • AN 4.28 — a bhikkhu "content with any kind of robe" who "does not engage in a wrong search, in what is improper, for the sake of a robe"; the promptings the rule allows are the limit of a proper search, and Upananda, who seized a man by his belt, went past it.46
