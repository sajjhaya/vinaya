    NP 13

    Story (Vatthu)

    While the Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery, the monks from the group of six, who knew that the Buddha had prohibited having a blanket made entirely of black wool, added just a little bit of white on the edge, effectively having a blanket made entirely of black wool just as before. The monks of few desires complained and criticised them, "How could the monks from the group of six do this?"

    After rebuking those monks in many ways, they told the Buddha. He had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned the monks: "Is it true, monks, that you do this?" "It's true, sir." The Buddha rebuked them: "Foolish men, how could you do this? This will affect people's confidence …" And he laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — forbidden an all-black blanket, the group of six add a little white at the edge and carry on as before; the Buddha lays down the proportions of black, white and brown wool for a new blanket.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk is having a new blanket made, he should use two parts of entirely black wool, a third part of white, and a fourth part of brown. If he has a new blanket made without using two parts of entirely black wool, a third part of white, and a fourth part of brown, he commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu makes a blanket, or has one made, it should contain no more than 50 percent black wool, and at least 25 percent white and 25 percent brown. If it contains more than 50 percent black wool, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "When a bhikkhu is having a new felt (blanket/rug) made, two parts of pure black wool are to be incorporated, a third (part) of white, and a fourth of brown. If a bhikkhu should have a new felt (blanket/rug) made without incorporating two parts of pure black wool, a third of white, and a fourth of brown, it is to be forfeited and confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Having a santhata made mostly of black wool is a nissaggiya pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "By a bhikkhu who is having a new rug made, two parts of pure black sheep's wool are to be taken, [and] a third [part] of white, a fourth [part] of ruddy brown. If a bhikkhu should have a rug made, without having taken two parts of pure black sheep's wool, [and] a third [part] of white, a fourth [part] of ruddy brown, [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture."6

    Here the renderings part in a way that matters. The full Vibhaṅga rule, Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita give a recipe, two parts black, one white, one brown, and make the offence the failure to follow it. Brahmāli's recitation recasts the recipe as limits, at most half black and at least a quarter each of white and brown, and then makes the offence only the first of them, "more than 50 percent black wool"; Ajahn Brahm's "mostly of black wool" does the same. The two forms agree on every blanket except one that keeps the black to half but has less than a quarter of white or of brown, or none; that case belongs to Object, where it is argued.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule protects a monk from himself at the point where he keeps the letter of a rule and defeats its purpose. Forbidden an all-black blanket, the group of six added a little white at the edge, and the Vibhaṅga's narrator does not pretend to be deceived: they were "effectively having a blanket made entirely of black wool just as before."7 The rule answers with a measure that leaves no edge to work, where a second prohibition could have been skirted in turn: the wool is weighed, and black may make up only its share. Its purpose, as Thanissaro puts it, is to cap the black wool a monk may put into his felt.8

    Everything but the object is NP 11's: the santhata and what counts as one today, the effort through every way of finishing, the result on getting it, the relinquishment, and the derived offences.9​ 10

    Object

    A new santhata, "newly made", whose wool does not keep the proportions.11 The Vibhaṅga sets them by weight: "having weighed it, he should use two measures" of entirely black wool, one measure of white and one of brown.12 On Thanissaro's report, the Commentary takes these quantities as relative: so long as black is no more than half the wool, there is no offence.13 The non-offence clause confirms that more white and brown is never an offence: there is no offence in using one measure each of white and brown, in using more than one measure of each, or in using "just white and brown".14 The brown, gocariya, is Ñāṇatusita's "ruddy brown" and Ajahn Brahm's "reddish-brown".15​ 16

    Black kept to half, but too little white or brown. Here the recipe and the limit part.

    Disputed — whether a blanket that keeps the black to half also needs a quarter each of white and brown.

    The rule as spoken requires a third part of white and a fourth of brown, and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual states the object the same way, "at least 25% white wool and at least 25% brown wool".17 On the other side Thanissaro gives the Commentary's reading: there is no offence "as long as black wool constitutes no more than half the total amount". Thanissaro himself names both quarters in his non-offences, "one-quarter or more white wool and one-quarter or more brown wool", as an exemption, while his summary puts the offence at a blanket "more than one-half black wool". Brahmāli's own recitation makes the offence only "more than 50 percent black wool". And for Ajahn Brahm the white and brown "may be greater" than a quarter each, and the remedy for too much black is "enough white or reddish-brown wool".18​ 19​ 20 The non-offence clause names blankets of white and brown with no black, and never one of black and white with no brown, so it does not settle the case.21 The question stays open. My own counsel, and it is no more than that, is to put in a quarter each of white and brown, which keeps the rule however it is read.

    Special Cases

    As at NP 11, the Vibhaṅga gives wrong conduct both for making one for someone else and for using one someone else has made, and Ajahn Brahm concludes that a santhata more than half black is unallowable for any monk: even the offender who has received his back after relinquishing it may not use it, and would give it away, discard it, or, more likely, shred it and make a new one with enough white or brown wool in it.22​ 23 That last remedy, he notes, may run straight into the next rule: one can argue from NP 14 that a monk who has had a santhata made, even one he cannot use, may not have another made until six years have passed.24 The case teaches a method worth having: before mending a breach of one rule, read the next. Whether the six years really run from a blanket a monk may not use is not something the texts decide, and Ajahn Brahm offers it as an argument, not a ruling; NP 14 sets out the Saṅgha's authorisation that would answer it.

    The white edge in the Story is the warning. A monk who finds himself measuring how little of a rule he needs to keep has already done what the group of six did.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — for the effort of making the santhata, or having it made, without the proportions, before it is got.25
    • Dukkaṭa — making one, or having one made, for someone else.26
    • Dukkaṭa — using one that someone else has made.27

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he makes one using one measure of white and one measure of brown; if he makes one using more than one measure of white and more than one measure of brown; if he makes one using just white and brown; if he makes a canopy, a floor cover, a cloth screen, a mattress, or a pillow; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.28

    Related Offences

    • NP 12 — the all-black santhata, the rule this one closes the gap in.
    • NP 11 — the anchor of NP 11–15, where the santhata and the shared factors are set out.
    • NP 14 — the six-year rule, which on Ajahn Brahm's argument may begin to run even from a santhata a monk may not use.

    The Story, definitions, permutations and non-offence clauses are from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation, and the shared machinery is carried from NP 11. Brahmāli's recitation and the Manual give the percentages; Thanissaro's BMC1 gives the rule's purpose, the Commentary's relative reading and the non-offence wording; Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes gives the larger shares of white and brown, the unallowability and the argument from NP 14. The Commentary is cited from Thanissaro's report of it. The Concise BMC adds nothing, and the class on this rule is read but not cited.
