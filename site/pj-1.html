    Pj 1

    Story (Vatthu)

    Sudinna was the son of a wealthy merchant of Kalanda, a village near Vesālī. He had come to the city on business when he came upon the Buddha teaching, and on hearing the teaching he thought that it is not easy for one who lives at home to lead the spiritual life "perfectly complete and pure as a polished conch shell."1 He asked the Buddha for the going forth. The Buddha asked whether he had his parents' permission; he did not, and Buddhas do not give the going forth to anyone who lacks it.

    Sudinna went home and asked. His parents refused, since he was their only child and dear to them. He asked again, twice more, and was refused again. He then lay down on the bare ground and said he would either die there or go forth, and did not eat at the next seven meals. His parents could not move him; nor could his friends, whom they sent to reason with him. In the end his friends pointed out that if he died the parents would lose him for certain, whereas if he went forth they might still see him afterward, and on that reasoning the parents relented.

    He went forth, and for a time lived on the practice: dwelling in the forest, eating almsfood, wearing rag-robes. Then a famine arose in the Vajjian country where he was staying, and he thought of his relatives in Vesālī, who were wealthy and could support the Saṅgha. He went there and stayed in the house with the peaked roof in the Great Wood; his relatives sent him sixty servings of food, which he gave to the monks. Then he went for alms to Kalanda, and came, in the course of his round, to the door of his own father's house.

    A female slave of Sudinna's relatives was about to throw away the previous evening's porridge. Sudinna told her that if it was to be thrown away she could put it in his bowl. As she did so she recognized his hands, feet, and voice, and went to tell his mother. His father found him eating the old porridge by a wall and brought him inside.

    The next morning his mother had the ground smeared with fresh cow dung and piled up two heaps, one of money and one of gold, so large that a man standing on one side could not see a man standing on the other. She set a seat between them, and had Sudinna's former wife adorned in the ornaments he had liked. When Sudinna came, his father showed him the wealth and asked him to return to the lay life and make merit; Sudinna said the wealth would be better tied in sacks and thrown into the Ganges, for it brought only fear and worry. His former wife took him by the feet and asked for whose sake — for what nymphs — he was living the spiritual life. He answered, "Sister, I don't practice the spiritual life for the sake of nymphs." At being addressed as "sister" she fainted.

    His mother then asked him for one thing: since the family had no heir, and the Licchavīs would otherwise take over their heirless property, would he at least provide offspring? Sudinna agreed. There being as yet no training rule against it, and seeing no danger in it, he took his former wife into the Great Wood and had intercourse with her three times. She conceived.

    The earth-dwelling gods raised the cry that the Saṅgha, until now free of cancer and danger, had had a cancer raised in it by Sudinna of Kalanda; and the cry was taken up from realm to realm up to the highest heavens.

    A son was born, and Sudinna's friends called him Offspring;2 in time both the son and the former wife went forth and reached awakening. But Sudinna grew thin, wretched, and pale, his veins standing out, troubled by remorse that he had gone forth in so well-proclaimed a teaching and had not been able to keep the spiritual life whole. His companions asked what was wrong, he told them, and they reported it to the Buddha.

    The Buddha rebuked him in many ways. It would be better, he said, for his penis to enter the mouth of a black snake, or a blazing charcoal pit, than to enter a woman; for that might lead to death or deadly suffering, but not to a bad rebirth, whereas this could. He then laid down the first version of the rule: a monk who has sexual intercourse is expelled and no longer in communion.

    The rule was extended twice. A monk in the Great Wood near Vesālī had befriended a female monkey by feeding her, and had intercourse with her. A number of monks walking about the dwellings came to his; the monkey went up to them, presented her backside, and made a gesture. Suspecting what this meant, they hid to one side, and when the monk returned from his almsround and fed her, they saw him repeat the act. When they questioned him he objected that the rule spoke only of women; the Buddha extended it to cover intercourse "even with a female animal."

    Later, a number of Vajjian monks from Vesālī who had been living indulgently had intercourse without first renouncing the training. In time, afflicted by loss of relatives, property, and health, they blamed only themselves, and asked through Ānanda to be given the going forth and full ordination again, promising devotion to developing the aids to awakening. The Buddha refused, saying it is impossible that he should abolish a rule entailing expulsion, but used the case to add the final clarification: a monk who has intercourse without first renouncing the training may not be ordained again, while one who renounces the training properly first and then has intercourse as a layman is not permanently barred. This produced the rule's final form, with its opening clause about the training and way of life not yet renounced.3

    Synopsis — Ven. Sudinna, asked by his mother to provide an heir, has intercourse with his former wife so that the Licchavīs will not take the heirless family property; the rule is then extended to cover animals after a monk has intercourse with a female monkey, and clarified after a group of monks who had intercourse without first renouncing the training ask to be reordained.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk, after taking on the monks' training and way of life, without first renouncing the training and revealing his weakness, has sexual intercourse, even with a female animal, he is expelled and excluded from the community."4

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu has any kind of sexual intercourse, even with an animal, he incurs a pārājika."5

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu—participating in the training and livelihood of the bhikkhus, without having renounced the training, without having declared his weakness—engage in sexual intercourse, even with a female animal, he is defeated and no longer in affiliation."6

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1, reproducing Thanissaro's rendering in a form that differs from the current BMC1, apparently an earlier printing)

    "Should any monk – participating in the training and livelihood of the monks, without having renounced the training, without having declared his weakness – engage in the sexual act, even with a female animal, he is defeated and no longer in communion."7

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu who has entered upon the training and livelihood for bhikkhus, not having rejected the training, not having disclosed [his] incapability, should engage in the act of sexual intercourse, even with just a female animal, he is disqualified, not in communion."8

    The five renderings agree on what the rule requires, and the recitation line differs from the rest chiefly by leaving things out. It drops the definition of a monk and the clause on renouncing the training, which Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual says are not intrinsic to this rule and are covered by ordination and disrobal; and it drops the word "female". The Manual gives the reason: tiracchānagatāya must be masculine or feminine, seeming to have no neuter, and the feminine is used, he takes it, because a monk is likelier to offend with a female animal, not to exclude male ones.9 The Vibhaṅga bears this out, for its permutations count a male animal as fully as a female.10 The translators give the last word three ways, expelled, defeated and disqualified, and the Vibhaṅga's own gloss of it, discussed below, says what it means.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule exists to keep the Saṅgha whole and trusted, and the Story measures the harm by how far it reached beyond the man who did it. Sudinna acted to give his family an heir, before there was any rule and seeing no danger in it; yet the earth gods' cry that he had produced a cancer in a Saṅgha until then free of one was taken up to the highest heavens,11 and the Buddha's rebuke looked past Sudinna to everyone who would hear of it: "This will affect people's confidence, and cause some to lose it." It is here that the Vinaya first gives the ten reasons for which a training rule is laid down, among them "for the well-being of the Sangha, for the comfort of the Sangha" and "to give rise to confidence in those without it, to increase the confidence of those who have it", and later origin stories carry them in abbreviation.12 The penalty answers the harm. A monk who has had intercourse is expelled: "just as a man with his head cut off is unable to continue living by reconnecting it to the body, so too is a monk who has had sexual intercourse not an ascetic, not a Sakyan monastic." He is excluded from the community, which the Vibhaṅga defines as joint legal procedures, a joint recitation and the same training, none of which he takes part in any longer.13 The loss is not a penalty served inside the Saṅgha but an exit from it, and the Vajjian monks of the Story show that it cannot be undone by asking: the Buddha would not abolish the rule for them, and ruled that a monk who has intercourse without first renouncing the training may not be ordained again.14

    Thanissaro gives the full offence four factors: effort, object, knowledge, and consent.15 The Manual reaches the same ground through three heads, and the content is the same either way.16 Thanissaro's division is followed below, with knowledge and consent taken together, since neither is sufficient alone.

    Effort

    The Vibhaṅga defines the act by entry: "whoever makes an organ enter an organ, a genital enter a genital, even to the depth of a sesame seed".17 Once that has happened nothing else changes the verdict. It does not matter whether there is a covering between the organs, whether the monk is active or passive, whether anyone reaches orgasm, or how he is dressed: the case studies record monks who had intercourse dressed as householders, naked, or in a sarong of grass or antelope hide, each thinking this would spare him, and every one of them was expelled.18​ 19 A woman or a gynandromorph has three orifices for the purposes of this rule, the anus, the vagina and the mouth; a paṇḍaka or a male has two, the anus and the mouth.20

    Penetration by anything other than the penis is not intercourse under this rule. A monk who, being lustful, put his thumb into a girl's vagina was spared expulsion and given an offence entailing suspension, which Thanissaro places under Sg 2.21 The same definition seems to put an object entering the monk's own body outside this rule as well; what such an act incurs instead depends on what happens, and belongs to Sg 1 and Sg 2.

    The Vibhaṅga opens the second part of its permutations with a four-line summary it does not explain. In Brahmāli's rendering it reads: private part into private part, mouth into private part, private part into mouth, an offence entailing expulsion; mouth into mouth, a serious offence. He takes the two unexplained words, magga and amagga, as "private part" and "mouth", so that the fourth line is a penetrative kiss.22 The Commentary reads amagga as a wound, drawing on two case studies of a corpse with a wound beside the genitals, and Thanissaro accepts it.23 Brahmāli's reading is adopted here, since it gives a canonical home to the penalty for mouth-to-mouth penetration that Thanissaro and the Concise BMC both state; the two corpse cases are full offences on either reading, since in both the penis passes through the genitals.

    Object

    The partner may be any being: a human, a spirit, or an animal, of any sex — female, male, gynandromorph or paṇḍaka — and living or dead.24 Kinship is no protection: the case studies record monks who had intercourse with a mother, a daughter, a sister and a former wife, and each was expelled.25 A corpse carries the full offence while it is undecomposed or mostly so, and even a severed head does where the monk makes contact as he enters its open mouth; a corpse that is mostly decomposed carries a lesser offence (see Derived Offences, below).26

    Disputed — whether "self-intercourse" falls under this rule. The case studies record a monk with a supple back who put his penis into his own mouth and a monk with a long penis who put it into his own anus, and each was expelled; Thanissaro takes the cases as they stand.27

    The case for setting them aside is textual, and the Manual makes it, marking the factor as one he thinks invalid and keeps only because it is generally accepted. Methuna means "pair", and the Vibhaṅga's own definition of the act is "attained to this or that pair", so the case-law judgement "seems to conflict with the rule and the padabhājanīya. When such conflict is apparent, we should go with the rule, rather than the Vibhaṅga" — a principle that rests on his view, stated at the head of the Manual, that the case studies and derived offences belong to a later period than the rule.28 He grants, with hedges, that the room this gives is probably narrow in practice: he thinks most monasteries would still call the act a pārājika, and that a community departing from them would pay for it in standing.29 The textual argument is hard to answer, since the word-analysis itself describes an act between two; but the case law says the opposite in so many words, and nothing in the texts reconciles the two, so any reconciliation would be ours; meanwhile the case law is what, by Brahmāli's own estimate in class, most monasteries would apply.

    Knowledge and Consent

    A monk must know that intercourse is taking place and consent to it.30 The Vibhaṅga works this out through a long series in which "enemy monks" force the act, bringing a woman to a monk or the monk to her: consent at any one of four moments — to the entry, to having entered, to the remaining, or to the taking out — is the full offence, and consent at none of them is no offence at all.31 The case studies apply the rule as it reads. A monk who woke to find that a woman had used him without his knowing had no offence; nor did a monk who got up quickly, nor the nun Uppalavaṇṇā when a young brahmin raped her. The monks in the Dark Wood at Sāvatthī who consented at every stage were expelled.32 Between monks the rule is the same: where a monk rapes a sleeping monk, both are expelled if the other wakes and consents, and the rapist alone if he does not.33

    Consent does not need the monk to move. A woman told a monk that if she made the effort and he did not, he would have no offence. He went along with it, and was expelled.34

    Disputed — whether mental consent alone completes the offence, or whether it must also find some outward expression.

    The first is the plainer reading, and the Manual states it in its most compressed form — "Requires mental consent", "No physical or verbal effort required" — and the do-all-the-work case is its evidence.35 Thanissaro reads the same cases as showing that consent is a mental state together with its physical or verbal expression: a monk who feels consent during part of the act but struggles so as not to express it is absolved, on the Commentary's principle that there is no offence in the mere arising of a mental state, while one who "puts up no struggle and feels mental consent, even if only fleetingly" incurs the full penalty.36 Ajahn Brahm asks for more again, requiring some deliberate physical cooperation, so that a monk who "makes absolutely no physical effort to cooperate" has no offence — which is hard to square with the do-all-the-work case, where the monk made no effort and was expelled.37 Thanissaro and the Manual agree on every case the Vibhaṅga decides, while Ajahn Brahm's wording, taken literally, would acquit the do-all-the-work monk; and the Manual parts from the other two over a monk who assents inwardly while resisting outwardly, a case the texts never put. The Vibhaṅga's verb names a state of mind and nothing more, which favours Brahmāli; but none of its cases tests the point, and the lean to Brahmāli, like any answer to the case the texts never put, is ours.

    Sensation, by contrast, is no factor at all. A monk with impaired faculties, who felt neither pleasure nor pain and thought this would spare him, was told: "Whether or not that fool felt anything, there's an offense entailing expulsion."38

    The plainest case is the one a monk should carry with him. A monk resting in the Great Wood at Vesālī found a woman sitting on him, and "kicked her off". Asked whether he had consented, he said he had not: "There's no offense for one who doesn't consent." The Commentary draws the lesson for any monk not yet free of desire: "This is how a bhikkhu still subject to sensual lust should act if he wants to protect his state of mind."39

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Thullaccaya — intercourse with a corpse that is mostly decomposed.40
    • Thullaccaya — mouth-to-mouth penetration, the fourth line of the Vibhaṅga's summary as Brahmāli reads it, whatever the other's sex or species. The Concise BMC adds that it applies even without lust, as in mouth-to-mouth resuscitation; where there is lust, Thanissaro adds the penalty under Sg 2 to it, while Brahmāli suggests that the lustful case falls under Sg 2 instead.41
    • Dukkaṭa — touching with the penis the genitals of a picture or of a wooden doll; entering a severed head's open mouth without making contact; bringing the penis to the bones of a dead woman. Thanissaro extends the same penalty to any other part of a corpse and to any insentient object, with Sg 1 applying if it leads to ejaculation.42​ 43
    • Saṅghādisesa — an attempt at intercourse that stops at bodily contact. A monk who intended intercourse and "felt remorse at the mere touch", and a monk who did as a lay follower urged and rubbed against her thighs and body instead, were each given an offence entailing suspension.44 Thanissaro places such cases under Sg 2 for lustful contact, where the penalty follows the partner — a saṅghādisesa for a woman, a thullaccaya for a paṇḍaka, a dukkaṭa for a man or an animal — or under Sg 1 if there is ejaculation.45 The Commentary's system of eleven kinds of lust, which would make the touch a mere preliminary dukkaṭa, has no basis in the Canon and contradicts these case studies, and Thanissaro rejects it.46

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence for a monk who does not know; who does not consent; who is insane; who is deranged; who is overwhelmed by pain; or who is the first offender — here both Sudinna and the monk with the monkey.47​ 48 Thanissaro renders "deranged" as "possessed by spirits". The Commentary defines insanity narrowly, as a monk who no longer knows whether he has broken major or minor rules, and the exemption covers only the period in which his perception is deranged, not his lucid intervals.49 These exemptions recur under nearly all the rules and are not repeated at length in later entries.50

    Two case studies extend the exemption to a monk who did not consent. A monk from Bhārukaccha who dreamed that he had intercourse with his former wife, and set out to disrobe, was stopped by Venerable Upāli: "There's no offense when it occurs while dreaming."51 And a monk resting in the house with the peaked roof in the Great Wood, the door open, was used by a group of women visiting the monastery, who left remarking "What a bull of a man." The Buddha explained that an erection has five causes, "sensual desire, feces, urine, or wind, or … being stung by caterpillars", that this monk's could not have come from desire, for "That monk is a perfected one", and that there was no offence. He added, in Brahmāli's rendering and Ajahn Brahm's, that monks should close the door when in seclusion during the day; Thanissaro reads a permission to close it, not an instruction, and faults the Commentary for making a dukkaṭa of leaving it open.52 Either way, the door is better closed.

    The rule's opening clause is the other side of the expulsion described above, and it is why the Vibhaṅga teaches disrobal here. A monk who renounces the training properly and then has intercourse as a layman has broken no monastic rule and may be ordained again. "Properly" is exacting. A monk who only voices his discontent — wondering aloud whether to give up the Buddha, remembering his wife, saying the life is hard — has revealed his weakness without renouncing anything. He must declare it outright, as in "I renounce the training", meaning what he says, to a human being who understands; renouncing as a joke, by a slip of the tongue, or to someone who does not understand does not count.53 Ajahn Brahm draws out the practical consequence: the declaration must be in the present tense, so that saying one will disrobe is not disrobing, and the hearer may be a monastic or a layperson provided he understands.54

    A monk who acquired female characteristics was transferred, with her ordination and seniority, to the nuns, and her offences not shared with the nuns lapsed; the same held in reverse.55

    Related Offences

    • Sg 1 — intentional emission of semen, where an act that falls short of intercourse ends in ejaculation.
    • Sg 2 — lustful bodily contact with a woman, where an attempt at intercourse stops at the touch.
    • Sg 3 — speaking lewd words to a woman, the speech counterpart to the contact offences above.
    • Sg 4 — recommending intercourse to a woman, motivated by lust: the step before the act this rule forbids.

    Related Suttas

    • AN 5.55 — a monk and a nun, son and mother, who "without having given up the training and declared their weakness … engaged in sexual intercourse"; kinship was no safeguard, for the bond grew from often seeing one another.56
    • AN 7.50 — the seven "bonds of sexuality" short of intercourse, from consenting to be massaged by women to living the spiritual life for a heavenly rebirth; a measure of how much more than intercourse the celibate life gives up.57
    • AN 7.51 — "union and disengagement": the desire for union begins in a man's delight in his own masculinity, so the celibacy the rule protects is kept first in the mind.58
    • SN 35.127 — Piṇḍola Bhāradvāja's answer to King Udena, who asked how young monks keep the celibate life: by regarding women as mothers, sisters and daughters, and by reviewing the body's parts, the practice by which this rule is kept.59

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, and the Explanation rests on his translation, its case studies and the Manual, with Thanissaro's BMC1, Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita's Analysis where each adds or differs; Brahmāli's Appendix of Technical Terms gives the reading of the Vibhaṅga's four-line summary. The class on this rule (session 001, 17 November 2011) is used once, for a practical caution his written works do not give, and the four suttas in Related Suttas are quoted from Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation.
