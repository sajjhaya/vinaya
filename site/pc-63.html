    Pc 63

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time the monks from the group of six were reopening a legal issue that they knew had been legitimately settled, saying, "The legal procedure hasn't been done;" "It's been done badly;" "It should be done again;" "It hasn't been settled;" "It's been badly settled;" "It should be settled again."1

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised them: "How can the monks from the group of six reopen a legal issue that they know has been legitimately settled?" They told the Buddha, who questioned the monks and rebuked them: "Foolish men, how can you do this? This will affect people's confidence." A monk who reopens a legal issue that he knows has been legitimately settled commits an offence entailing confession.2

    Synopsis — The group of six went about saying that a properly settled matter had not been settled. The rule that follows closes a decided question for good, and its exemption is the whole of its fairness: a monk who honestly thinks the decision was bad may say so, and is right to.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk reopens a legal issue that he knows has been legitimately settled, he commits an offense entailing confession."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu tries to reopen a vinaya issue he knows has been properly settled, he incurs a pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu knowingly agitate for the reviving of an issue that has been rightfully dealt with, it is to be confessed."5

    Brahm

    "Knowing that a Sanghakamma has been properly carried out, urging that it be performed anew is an offence of Pācittiya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should knowingly agitate for further [legal] action a legal issue which has been disposed of according to the law, [this is a case] involving expiation."7

    The renderings agree on the rule and divide on two words of its Pāli.8 Adhikaraṇa is "a legal issue" in Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation and Ñāṇatusita, "a vinaya issue" in his recitation, and "an issue" in Thanissaro; Ajahn Brahm puts saṅghakamma, the formal act, in its place, which is how most such issues are settled though it is not the same thing.9​ 10​ 11 The verb, ukkoṭeti, is "reopens" in Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga and "agitate" in Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita, and the difference is real. One names an act, the other a campaign, and Brahmāli's own recitation, "tries to reopen", leans toward the campaign. What the effort comes to is taken up below, under Effort.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The Saṅgha's peace rests on decisions that stay decided, and this rule holds a monk to good faith toward them: a matter properly settled is not to be dragged back into dispute by someone who knows it was settled rightly. The group of six went about saying that a legal issue they knew had been properly settled had not been.12

    Thanissaro gives three factors: object, an issue that has been properly dealt with; perception, that one knows it was; and effort, saying that it was not.13

    Object

    The Vibhaṅga names four kinds of legal issue: those arising from disputes, from accusations, from offences, and from business.14 Thanissaro says what each is. A dispute about Dhamma and Vinaya is settled by declaring which side is right; an accusation, by judging it true or false; an offence, by the offender's undergoing the penalty; and a duty, such as an ordination or the recitation, by performing it properly.15 An issue is settled legitimately when it is done according to the Teaching, the Monastic Law and the Teacher's instruction.16 The Vibhaṅga states what that requires only in the negative, in its non-offence clause. There is no offence in reopening a procedure one knows to have been illegitimate, done by an incomplete assembly, or done against one who did not deserve it.17 Thanissaro gives the principle the rule rests on: an issue dealt with improperly may be reopened, but one dealt with properly is closed for good.18

    What each of the three requires, from the complete assembly to the right procedure, is the Khandhaka's, which Thanissaro sets out in BMC2.19

    Ajahn Brahm adds when an objection belongs: at the meeting. A monk who thinks an act is being done improperly should say so then, and if he fears intimidation he may whisper his protest to the monk beside him, since one monk's protest is enough to stop an act that must be unanimous. Once the act is completed without protest and in proper form, it is settled. A monk who sent his consent instead of attending, and afterwards says the act should be done again in his presence, commits the offence.20 The right to protest and the rule are one arrangement seen from two ends: the objection is open until the act is done, and after that it is an offence.

    Effort

    The Vibhaṅga gives six sentences as the act: that the legal procedure has not been done, or was done badly, or should be done again; that the issue has not been settled, or was settled badly, or should be settled again.21 It does not matter who says them, and the Parivāra names what moves them: they reopen an issue "biased by desire, ill will, confusion, or fear".22 The Parivāra makes a monk ordained that very day, a newly arrived monk, a monk who gave his consent, and the monk who decided the issue in the first place all liable alike.23 Brahmāli's endnote gives the Commentary's example of the last, a small scene: the decider goes out to the yard, the monks who lost the case ask him why he decided it that way, and he answers, "Why did you not say this straightaway?" — and reopens it.24

    Thanissaro adds that the words are said "in the presence of another bhikkhu", a condition the Vibhaṅga's clause leaves unstated.25

    What follows the offence is heavier than the offence. Thanissaro reports the Commentary to Cv.IX.3: a monk who commits it may have his pātimokkha cancelled. That, he adds, gives the Community the occasion to look into his attitude, and if he keeps pressing, "one is considered a maker of strife", subject to censure, banishment or suspension.26 The Khandhaka attaches this rule's offence for reopening to each of its ways of settling an issue, and again to opening up after the Invitation a matter known before it; Thanissaro quotes one of each.27

    Perception

    The grid follows what the monk believes about the procedure. A legitimate act perceived as legitimate and reopened is the pācittiya; doubted, an offence of wrong conduct; and perceived as illegitimate, no offence at all.28 Thanissaro draws the practical rule from the doubt: a monk in doubt "may declare one's doubt, but to state baldly that the issue needs to be reopened is to incur a dukkaṭa."29 His example of the exemption: a Community has censured Bhikkhu X; a monk honestly believes X did not deserve it and says so to a fellow monk; "one commits no offense even if it turns out that X did in fact deserve censure."30 Ajahn Brahm adds the counsel that goes with it, that one "should have pointed out the defect at the time rather than afterwards."31

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The exemption is the rule's whole fairness, and it rests on the monk's honest belief, not on the facts.32​ 33 The rule does not ask a monk to agree with the Saṅgha. It asks him to be honest about whether he does.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — reopening a legitimate procedure while unsure whether it was legitimate.34
    • Dukkaṭa — reopening an illegitimate procedure while perceiving it as legitimate, or being unsure.35
    • Further action — not an offence but a consequence: cancellation of one's pātimokkha and, for a monk who persists, censure, banishment or suspension.36

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he reopens it because he knows that the legal procedure was illegitimate, done by an incomplete assembly, or done against one who did not deserve it; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.37

    Related Offences

    • Pc 79, 80, 81 — complaining after giving consent to a transaction, leaving a meeting to invalidate it, and complaining of favouritism in an allocation: with this rule, the rules protecting the Saṅgha's formal acts. Thanissaro draws the boundary: a complaint that the act was not done in accordance with the rule falls here.38
    • The seven Adhikaraṇasamatha rules — the principles for settling the four kinds of issue this rule names.39
