    Sg 5

    Story (Vatthu)

    At one time when the Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery, Venerable Udāyī was associating with and visiting a number of families in Sāvatthī. When he saw a young man without a wife and a young woman without a husband, he would praise the young woman to the young man's parents as beautiful, intelligent, skilled, and diligent, and suitable for their son; and he praised the young man to the young woman's parents in the same way. Each family replied that the other did not know them, and asked him to persuade the other side. In this way he arranged for the taking of a bride, for the giving of a bride, and for marriage.

    At that time there was a former courtesan who had a beautiful daughter. Some lay followers of the Ājīvaka religion came from another village and asked her to give the girl to their son. She refused: "I don't know who you are, sirs, and I won't give my only daughter to be taken to another village." People told the Ājīvakas to ask Udāyī, who would persuade her, and he agreed. He went to the courtesan and said, "Please give her to them. I know them." "If you know them, I'll give her away." The Ājīvakas took the girl away, and for a month they treated her like a daughter-in-law, but then like a slave.

    The girl sent a message to her mother: "Mom, I'm unhappy and miserable. For a month they treated me like a daughter-in-law, but then like a slave. Come, mom, and take me home." The courtesan went to the Ājīvakas and asked them to treat her girl properly. They replied, "We deal with the monastic, not with you. Go away! We don't want anything to do with you." When the girl sent the same message a second time, the courtesan went to Udāyī, and he went to the Ājīvakas with the same request. They replied, "We deal with the courtesan, not with you. A monastic shouldn't get involved. You should behave like a proper monastic. So go away! We don't want anything to do with you." When the message came a third time and the courtesan came to him again, Udāyī said, "When I first went, they just dismissed me. Go yourself; I'm not going."

    The courtesan then complained and criticized him: "May Venerable Udāyī be miserable and unhappy, just as my girl is miserable and unhappy because of her nasty mother-in-law, father-in-law, and husband." The girl said the same, as did other women who were unhappy with their in-laws and husbands. But the women who were happy with theirs wished him well: "May Venerable Udāyī be happy and well, just as we are happy and well because of our good mothers-in-law, fathers-in-law, and husbands." The monks heard of both, and the monks of few desires complained, "How can Venerable Udāyī act as a matchmaker?" They told the Buddha, who had the Saṅgha gathered, questioned Udāyī, rebuked him, and laid down a rule against acting as a matchmaker for marriage or for an affair.

    Soon afterwards a number of scoundrels who were enjoying themselves in a park sent a messenger to a sex worker, asking her to come. She replied, "Sirs, I don't know who you are. I'm wealthy. I don't want to go outside the city." One of the men said they should ask Udāyī to persuade her, but a Buddhist lay follower said, "No way. That's not allowable for the Sakyan monastics. He won't do it," and they made a bet on whether he would. The men went to Udāyī, and he agreed. He went to the woman and said, "Please go. I know them." "If you know them, sir, I'll go." The lay follower then complained, "How could Venerable Udāyī act as a matchmaker for a brief affair?" The monks of few desires complained in the same words and told the Buddha, who questioned and rebuked Udāyī and extended the rule to an affair, even if just a brief one.1

    Synopsis — Venerable Udāyī arranges marriages among the families he visits, one of which turns out badly, and later persuades a sex worker to go to a group of men; the Buddha lays down a rule against acting as a matchmaker, and then extends it to the briefest affair.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk acts as a matchmaker, conveying a man's intention to a woman or a woman's intention to a man, for marriage or for an affair, even if just a brief one, he commits an offense entailing suspension."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu acts as a go-between for a sexual relationship between a man and a woman, including marriage, he incurs a saṅghādisesa."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu engage in conveying a man's intentions to a woman or a woman's intentions to a man, proposing marriage or paramourage—even if only for a momentary liaison—it entails initial and subsequent meetings of the Community."4

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should engage in mediating, [and convey] a man's intention to a woman, or a woman's intention to a man, for being a wife or for being a mistress, even for being one on [just] that occasion: [this is a case] involving the community in the beginning and in the rest."5

    The four renderings agree on the act and differ over how to name what it arranges. The full translations keep the rule's three relationships, marriage, an affair, and the brief one added after the second story; the recitation folds all three into "a sexual relationship" but keeps marriage by name ("including marriage"). Ñāṇatusita reads the last of them as describing the woman rather than the arrangement, a mistress on just that occasion, which matches the "momentary wife" among the Vibhaṅga's ten kinds of wife; the reach of the rule is the same either way.6 The difference worth noticing is "matchmaker" against "go-between". The word-analysis defines the act purely as carrying a message from one party to the other, so a monk need not have chosen the match to fall under the rule.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule keeps a monk's good name from being staked on other people's marriages. In the Story a monk trusted by many families became the guarantor of the marriages he made: when one went badly, the in-laws told the bride's mother that they dealt with the monastic, and the curses of the unhappy wives and the blessings of the happy ones fell alike on his name. The second story removes any thought that the rule is about respectable marriages only. A monk who carries the message for a single brief affair has done the same thing.

    Thanissaro gives the offence two factors, effort and object, and notes that intention, perception and result play no part in it.7 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual arranges its factors by the rule's phrases, the going between and the sexual relationship it serves.8 I keep Thanissaro's two. Ajahn Brahm calls this the first rule in the Pātimokkha with no exemption for acting unknowingly,9 and the point runs through both factors.

    Effort

    The word-analysis defines acting as a matchmaker as going between: sent by a woman he goes to a man, or sent by a man he goes to a woman, and informs the one of the other's intention.10 The permutations break the role into three steps, accepting the mission, finding out the response and reporting back, and grade the offence by how many are done: all three, the full offence; any two, a serious offence; any one, wrong conduct.11 Accepting is not required for the lesser offences, so a monk who on his own initiative sounds out the possibility of a date and reports back has two steps; Thanissaro's example is exactly that case.12 Ajahn Brahm works the grid through cases a monk might meet: a monk who agrees to carry a proposal and forgets it on the way has one step, and one who learns on arrival that the other family already approves, and goes back to say so without delivering the message, has two.13 The Manual states the same ground in two steps, taking a message and reporting the reaction back.14

    The steps count however they are done. A monk who accepts, gets a pupil to find out the response and reports back himself, or finds out the response himself and gets a pupil to report back, has the full offence; if the pupil reports back on his own initiative, both have a serious offence. If a number of monks accept the mission and only one finds out the response or reports back, all of them have the full offence.15 The case studies mark where finding out begins. A monk sent to find out about a woman who asks people where she is, and hears that she is asleep, dead, or gone out, that she is not a woman, or that she lacks sexual organs, has wrong conduct and not the full offence;16 Thanissaro reads this as asking along the way, which is not yet inquiring.17 Whether the match is made does not matter. The Manual, citing the Commentary, says it is irrelevant whether a relationship actually materialises.18

    The offence lies in telling. Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm both report the Sub-commentary's case of a monk who carries a sealed letter proposing marriage without knowing what it says. It finds no offence under this rule, since the Vibhaṅga and the Commentary define conveying as telling: the monk must relate the proposal himself, by speech, by a gesture, or in a letter of his own.19​ 20 Ajahn Brahm sets the case directly after the rule's lack of any exemption for not knowing, as a qualification of it; on the ground both sources give, the definition of conveying, the sealed-letter monk has conveyed nothing, and it is that, and not his ignorance, that clears him. What a monk does not know about the couple does not excuse him; carrying is not telling.

    Disputed — whether the full offence needs the third step where the arrangement succeeds without it.

    Ajahn Brahm, following an argument he credits to some teachers, the author of the Vinayamukha among them, and calls convincing, holds that it does not. A man asks a monk to persuade a sex worker to meet him at a set time and place, and to report back only if there is a problem; the monk does so, she comes, and he has accepted and inquired only, yet he has gone between in full, and the rule, as he reads its translation, speaks of conveying an intention, not of reporting back; in such a case, he concludes, accepting and inquiring are a saṅghādisesa.21 Against this stands the Vibhaṅga's own grid, which gives a monk who accepts and finds out the response but does not report back a serious offence,22 and which Thanissaro sets out as it stands; Brahmāli's two steps still end in reporting back.23 The Manual, following the Commentary, answers the argument from the outcome: whether a relationship actually materialises is irrelevant, so the offence cannot depend on the arrangement being accomplished.24 The case Ajahn Brahm calls undecided, a monk arranging his own marriage, comes down to the same split between the rule's wording and the three steps. I lean to the Vibhaṅga, since its permutations decide the shape of the case Ajahn Brahm puts, though they never consider an arrangement that completes itself without a reply. But Ajahn Brahm's monk has done everything the rule exists to forbid, and the lean is ours, offered against a reading the rule's own words support.

    A monk planning to disrobe who arranges his own marriage is the case most likely to tempt. Thanissaro gives it wrong conduct for inquiring: a monk who asks a woman whether she would marry him after his return to lay life has done one step.25 Ajahn Brahm records it as undecided, a saṅghādisesa on the rule's wording and not on the Vibhaṅga's three steps, with learned teachers he consulted on both sides; whatever the offence, "all conscientious bhikkhus would agree that to arrange one's own marriage while still a bhikkhu is improper and blameworthy."26

    Object

    The full offence needs a man and a woman not married to each other.27 The parties need not be the couple themselves. The permutations run the proposal through the man, his parents and relatives, the woman, and whoever has authority over her, across ten kinds of protected woman, from one protected by her mother to one protected by the threat of punishment, and ten kinds of wife, from the bought wife to the momentary wife; every combination is the full offence.28 Whatever form of union a society recognises, then, falls under the rule, and dealing with parents, or with a sex worker's pimp, is the same as dealing with the couple.29

    Who counts as married decides the most practical case. In the case studies a woman quarrelled with her husband and went to her mother's house, and a monk who associated with the family reconciled them; asked whether they were divorced, he said no, and the Buddha said, "There's no offense if they're not divorced."30 A divorced couple are unmarried, so bringing them back together is the full offence, and since perception is not a factor, a monk who takes a divorced couple to be only separated is not excused; the Commentary's example is an arahant reconciling his own parents.31 In the other direction, Thanissaro takes the Commentary's definition from its discussion of the five precepts, under which a couple who live as husband and wife without any formal ceremony are married, and holds that it would seem to apply here.32 And so a monk who helps a long-cohabiting couple past a quarrel has, on that reading, done nothing under this rule, while a formally divorced couple are the ones he may not bring back together.

    The rule is between a man and a woman. Acting as a go-between for paṇḍakas is a serious offence, in one of the Vibhaṅga's own case studies,33 and the Commentary gives the same for a female yakkha or peta.34 Ajahn Brahm gives wrong conduct for arranging a relationship between two men.35

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The Commentary's examples of business for the Saṅgha, for a shrine or for one who is sick, as Thanissaro reports them, are a message about building work for the Community or a shrine, and a monk sent by a male lay follower to a female one to ask for medicine for a sick fellow monk; the Sub-commentary extends the exemption to any similar errand with no romantic side, so long as it does not become the subservience to lay people treated under Sg 13.36 Ajahn Brahm gives the same limit as advice: messages for lay people on other matters should be avoided, above all where they would be seen as corrupting families.37

    Udāyī's matches were not all bad ones. Some of the women blessed him, and the rule does not ask whether a marriage turned out well. What the Story shows is that a monk who arranges a marriage becomes answerable for it, to the families and to the bride, in a role he has no means to fill; and the first to tell him so were the Ājīvakas: "A monastic shouldn't get involved. You should behave like a proper monastic."

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Thullaccaya — any two of the three steps, including finding out and reporting back unasked; a pupil who reports back on his own initiative after his teacher accepted, and the teacher with him; acting as a go-between for paṇḍakas, and by the Commentary for a female yakkha or peta.38​ 39​ 40
    • Dukkaṭa — any one step; a monk sent to find out about a woman who learns that she is asleep, dead or gone out, or is not a woman or lacks sexual organs; and, by Ajahn Brahm, acting as a go-between for two men.41​ 42​ 43

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he goes because of business for the Saṅgha, for a shrine, or for one who is sick; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.44 The case studies add that reconciling a couple who are not divorced is no offence,45 and the Sub-commentary that carrying a sealed message without knowing its contents is none under this rule.46

    Related Offences

    • Sg 13 — corrupting families, the limit the Sub-commentary sets on the exemption for errands.
    • Divining a wedding date — by Ajahn Brahm, wrong conduct, as one of the "base arts" the Dīgha Nikāya names; DN 1 §1.26 has the ascetic Gotama refrain from "arranging the giving and taking in marriage, engagements and divorces".47​ 48

    Related Suttas

    • DN 1 — among the things the ascetic Gotama refrains from, running errands and messages and "arranging the giving and taking in marriage, engagements and divorces": the go-between's work this rule forbids, the second named as wrong livelihood.49
    • DN 31 — finding a son "a suitable wife" is among his parents' duties, a household's duty that a monk who arranges marriages takes on as his own.50
