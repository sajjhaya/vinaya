    NP 5

    Story (Vatthu)

    First sub-story. While the Buddha was staying at Rājagaha in the Bamboo Grove, the nun Uppalavaṇṇā was staying at Sāvatthī. One morning she robed up, took her bowl and robe, entered Sāvatthī for alms, ate her meal, and afterwards went to the Blind Men's Grove and sat down at the foot of a tree for the day's meditation.

    Just then some bandits who had stolen and slaughtered a cow were carrying the meat into the Blind Men's Grove. Their leader saw Uppalavaṇṇā sitting at the foot of the tree and thought, "If my sons and brothers see this nun, they'll harass her," and led them off by another path. When the meat was cooked, he took the best portion, tied it up in a palm-leaf wrap, hung it from a tree not far from her, and said, "Whatever ascetic or brahmin sees this gift, please take it." Then he left.

    Uppalavaṇṇā, coming out of meditation, heard him say it. She took the meat and returned to her dwelling. The next morning she prepared it, made it into a bundle with her upper robe, rose into the air, and came down at the Bamboo Grove. The Buddha had already gone into the village for alms, but Venerable Udāyī had been left behind to mind the dwellings. She approached him: "Sir, where's the Buddha?"

    "He's gone to the village for alms."

    "Please give this meat to the Buddha."

    "You'll please the Buddha with that meat. If you give me your sarong, you'll please me too."

    "It's hard for women to get material support, and this is one of my five robes. I don't have another. I can't give it away."

    "Sister, just as a man giving an elephant might decorate it with a girdle, so should you, when giving meat to the Buddha, decorate me with your sarong."

    Being pressured by Udāyī, Uppalavaṇṇā gave him her sarong and returned to her dwelling. The nuns who received her bowl and robe asked where her sarong was, and she told them what had happened. They complained and criticised Udāyī: "How could Venerable Udāyī receive a robe from a nun? It's hard for women to get material support." They told the monks; the monks of few desires complained and criticised him in the same words, and told the Buddha. He had the Saṅgha gathered, questioned Udāyī, established that the nun was unrelated to him, rebuked him — "a man and a woman who are unrelated don't know what's appropriate and inappropriate, what's good and bad, in dealing with each other" — and laid down the rule: a monk who receives a robe directly from an unrelated nun commits an offence entailing relinquishment and confession.1

    Second sub-story. After that, being wary of wrongdoing, the monks would not receive robes from nuns even in exchange. The nuns complained: "How can they not receive robes from us in exchange?" The monks told the Buddha, who then allowed it: "Monks, I allow you to receive things in exchange from five kinds of people: monks, nuns, trainee nuns, novice monks, and novice nuns." And he amended the rule to its final form, adding the words "except in exchange."2

    Synopsis — Udāyī pressures the nun Uppalavaṇṇā into giving him her sarong; the Buddha forbids a monk from receiving a robe directly from an unrelated nun, then amends the rule to permit receiving one in exchange.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk receives a robe directly from an unrelated nun, except in exchange, he commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu receives robe-cloth directly from an unrelated bhikkhunī, except if he gives something in return, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu accept robe-cloth from the hand of a bhikkhunī unrelated to him — except in exchange — it is to be forfeited and confessed."5

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Accepting a cloth from a bhikkhunī who is not a relation, except in exchange, is an offence of nissaggiya pācittiya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should accept a robe [-cloth] from the hand of an unrelated bhikkhunī, except in an exchange [of robes], [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture."7

    The renderings agree on the rule. The Pāli hatthato, "from the hand", is kept by Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita and rendered "directly" by Brahmāli, which is how the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī reads it;8 Ajahn Brahm's rule line leaves it out. The exchange clause differs in what it supplies: Ñāṇatusita brackets "[of robes]", while the recitation's "if he gives something in return" leaves the thing open, and the Vibhaṅga sides with the recitation, as its exchange clause under Effort shows.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule protects a monk's fairness with nuns, who could ill spare what they had. Uppalavaṇṇā brought the Buddha the best of the meat a bandit had left for any ascetic, and Udāyī asked her for her sarong as well, though she told him it was one of her five robes and she had no other; he pressed her with a simile, and she gave it.9 The nuns' complaint gives the reason, "It's hard for women to get material support", and the Buddha's rebuke echoes the one at NP 4, that unrelated men and women "don't know what's appropriate and inappropriate, what's good and bad, in dealing with each other".10​ 11 The rule's second stage shows what it does not forbid. When the monks, afraid of wrongdoing, would not even exchange robes with nuns, the nuns complained, and the Buddha allowed exchange among the five kinds of ordained.12 A monk may deal with a nun fairly; he may not take from her for nothing.

    The definitions of unrelated and nun are NP 4's.13 Thanissaro gives two factors, object and effort.14

    Object

    Robe-cloth: one of the six kinds, "not smaller than what can be assigned to another", the eight by four standard fingerbreadths of NP 1.15 Here it is cloth, made up or not, where NP 4 was a robe that had been worn. Any other requisite is no offence.16

    Effort

    Receiving it from an unrelated nun's own hand, without an exchange. Receiving means becoming its owner, so borrowing it, or taking it on trust from a close friend on the Khandhaka's conditions, is no offence.17​ 18 "From the hand" means without an intermediary: the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī allows cloth a nun gives through someone else.19 The Commentary does not ask for a hand-to-hand gift: if she sets the cloth down near him as her way of giving it and he accepts it as given, he has received it; and the factor is met even if he does not know a piece is from a nun, as when many donors pile cloth for him and one of them is.20

    Except in exchange. The Vibhaṅga lets much be exchanged for little or little for much, and the Commentary quotes the Mahā Paccarī that even a piece of yellow myrobalan, "one of the cheapest things imaginable in India", given in return is enough.21​ 22 The allowance in the Story is general: the five kinds of ordained may exchange things among themselves, which NP 20 bounds by forbidding trade with lay people and those ordained in other religions.23​ 24

    Perception does not excuse. If she is unrelated, the offence is full whether he takes her to be unrelated, is unsure, or thinks her related; a nun ordained on one side only, or a related nun he takes for unrelated or is unsure of, is wrong conduct.25 When he accepts, the effort is wrong conduct; when he gets the cloth, it is to be relinquished, by NP 1's procedure.26

    The myrobalan is the thing to remember. It costs nothing, and it changes the act from taking to exchanging, which is the whole of what the rule asks.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — for the effort of accepting, before the cloth is got.27
    • Dukkaṭa — receiving from a nun ordained on one side only; and if she is related and he takes her for unrelated, or is unsure.28

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if the nun is related; if much is exchanged with little or little with much; if he takes it on trust; if he borrows it; if he receives any requisite apart from robe-cloth; if it is a trainee nun or a novice nun; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.29

    Related Offences

    • NP 4 — having an unrelated nun wash a used robe, whose definitions this rule shares.
    • NP 20 — trade, which bounds the exchange allowed here.
    • Pc 25 — giving robe-cloth to an unrelated nun, the reverse of this rule.
    • Pc 26 — sewing a robe for an unrelated nun.

    Related Suttas

    • MN 142 — Mahāpajāpatī's gift of cloth to the Buddha, and the four purifications of an offering, among them "the offering that is purified both by the giver and by the receiver"; a gift has a receiver's side, and the rule keeps it clean: a robe is not pressed out of a nun who has no other.30
    • SN 35.127 — that monks regard women of a mother's, a sister's and a daughter's age as mothers, sisters and daughters (see NP 4); the regard behind the reason this rule shares with NP 4, that unrelated men and women do not know what is fitting between them.31
