    Pc 10

    Story (Vatthu)

    At one time the Buddha was staying at Āḷavī, at the Aggāḷava Shrine. The monks of Āḷavī were doing building work, and in the course of it they dug the earth and had it dug. The local people complained and criticised them: "How can the Sakyan monastics dig the earth and have it dug? They are hurting one-sensed life."

    The monks heard of the complaint, and the monks of few desires complained in turn to the Buddha. He asked the monks of Āḷavī whether it was true, and they admitted it was. The Buddha rebuked them: "People regard the earth as conscious. This will affect people's confidence…" He then laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — The building monks of Āḷavī dig the earth for their construction work, draw the local people's complaint that they are hurting one-sensed life, and are rebuked by the Buddha, who lays down a rule against digging the earth or having it dug.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk digs the earth or has it dug, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu digs the earth, or has it dug, he incurs a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu dig soil or have it dug, it is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Digging the earth, or having it dug, is an offence of Pācittya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should dig the earth or should have it dug, [this is a case] involving expiation."6

    The renderings agree, and nothing that divides them changes the rule. Thanissaro writes "soil" where the others write "earth", and the Pāli paṭhavī carries both senses. The word that matters is "digs", which the Vibhaṅga widens and Brahmāli declines to widen, a question taken up under Effort.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule asks a monk to respect what the people around him hold to be alive, for their confidence in the Saṅgha. The monks of Āḷavī were building. The complaint against them was that they had hurt what the people around them believed to be alive. The Buddha's rebuke names exactly that: "People regard the earth as conscious. This will affect people's confidence."7 The belief seems to have been widespread. The Jains, contemporaries of the Buddha, counted soil and vegetation as one-facultied life, possessing touch alone, and potters careful of their precepts took their clay only from termite mounds and other ground already dug, as the potter Ghaṭīkāra of the suttas does. Thanissaro adds a consideration that may carry more weight now: digging risks killing whatever small creatures live in the soil.8 The rule is the first of a pair, and Pc 11, on destroying vegetation, rests on the same belief.

    Thanissaro gives the offence four factors: object, effort, perception, and intention.9 The last two are taken together, since a single non-offence clause carries both.

    Object

    The rule protects what the Vibhaṅga calls productive earth: pure soil or clay, or ground that is mostly soil or clay with a little stone, gravel or sand in it. Ground that is mostly stone, gravel or sand is unproductive, and digging it is no offence.10 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual draws the line at more than half loam or clay.11 Burned earth is unproductive too, and Thanissaro reports the Commentary as taking this to be soil burnt in firing a bowl or a pot. Thanissaro adds that so is the fine dust that the wind leaves on dry ground.12 Earth already dug into a pile changes its status with the rain. Rained on for less than four months it is unproductive, and a monk may dig into it; rained on for more than four months it counts as productive again.13 Thanissaro counts irrigation as rain.14

    Effort

    To dig is to dig oneself; to have dug is to ask another, and a single request is a single offence however much the other person then digs.15 Asking, though, is not the same as hinting. The non-offence clause lets a monk say what is needed, or ask that something be made allowable, without telling anyone to dig,16 and such speech is called kappiya-vohāra, "allowable expression". Thanissaro's present-day example is "We're going to need a hole right here", and by his report the Commentary allows even an explicit request for a reservoir or a pit, so long as the monk does not say exactly where to dig.17 Brahmāli puts the line where the listener stands: a hint becomes a command when the person spoken to feels there is no choice but to do as the monk says.18

    The Vibhaṅga's permutations go further than the rule's two verbs, adding breaking and burning to digging, each with its causative.19 Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm take the wider list as the effort, so that driving in a stake (for Thanissaro, only so as to disturb the soil around it), making a furrow with a rake or lighting a fire on bare soil counts as digging.20​ 21 Ajahn Brahm spares the surface: sweeping, drawing in loose soil and leaving footprints are no offence, since to count them "would be too stringent".22 Thanissaro does not: for him drawing in the soil with a stick is breaking it.

    Disputed — whether the rule reaches breaking and burning at all, or only the digging its own words name.

    Brahmāli holds that it reaches digging alone. The rule names two of the six actions the Vibhaṅga lists, he can see no reason for the other four, and in his judgment "the Vibhaṅga is here unreasonably extending the rule", so that the four do not bind.23 Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm follow the Vibhaṅga, and neither argues the point; for them the word-analysis is simply what the rule means. Brahmāli is open that the narrower reading is his own, and says in class that as a guest in a monastery that keeps the rule otherwise he would follow its practice.24 The rule's wording favours him and the Vibhaṅga's analysis favours them. Which governs is a judgment about how far a word-analysis binds, which no text settles; I take Brahmāli's own rule as a guest, to follow the monastery's practice, as the guide meanwhile.

    Perception and Intention

    The permutations sort the cases by whether the monk takes the ground for earth. If it is earth and he takes it for earth, the offence is full; if he is unsure, it is wrong conduct whether or not it turns out to be earth; if he does not take it for earth, there is no offence even where it is. Taking ground that is not earth for earth, or being unsure of it, is wrong conduct.25 And the digging must be meant. The non-offence clause excuses the monk who digs unintentionally, without mindfulness, or without knowing.26

    Why a rule of this weight for an act whose harm a modern monk may not see? Brahmāli's first answer is that a pācittiya is not a heavy offence. His second is that the Pātimokkha is not a register of kamma: a monk can make very bad kamma without breaking the Vinaya, and break it in ways that have nothing to do with kamma, "such as storing food overnight". He thinks a Pātimokkha laid down today would probably not include this rule.27 A monk keeps it all the same, as training, not as a judgment that digging is cruel. The reason the Buddha gave at Āḷavī, as the text records it, was what the people watching the monks believed about the earth.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • A dukkaṭa for digging earth, or having it dug, broken or burned (on the Vibhaṅga's reading), while unsure whether it is earth.28
    • A dukkaṭa where it is not earth and he takes it for earth, or is unsure of it.29

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he says, "Consider this," "Give this," "Bring this," "There's need for this," "Make this allowable"; if it is unintentional; if he is not mindful; if he does not know; if he is insane; if he is the first offender.30 Beyond the Vibhaṅga, the Commentary, in Thanissaro's report, allows a monk to dig out a person or animal that has fallen into a pit. It can find no basis in the Canon for this and accepts it as the unanimous judgment of the ancient commentaries, perhaps, Thanissaro suggests, to err on the side of compassion. A monk who has fallen into a pit himself may not dig his way out, even to save his life.31 The Commentary on Pj 3 also allows digging a moat to hold back an approaching wildfire.32

    Related Offences

    • Pc 11 — destroying vegetation; it rests on the same belief in one-facultied life, and Thanissaro observes that the two rules together effectively keep a monk from farming.
    • Pj 3 — its Commentary is the source of the wildfire-moat allowance carried into this rule.
    • Pc 56 — lighting a fire to warm oneself; a fire lit for an allowable reason may still fall under this rule, on the wider reading of digging, for lighting it on live soil.33

    Related Suttas

    • MN 81 — the potter Ghaṭīkāra, who "does not dig the ground for clay with a pick or with his own hands; what has broken off riverbanks or is thrown up by rats, he brings home in a carrier": the care for living earth that the people of the Story looked for.34
