    Pc 53

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. The monks from the group of seventeen were playing in the water of the river Aciravatī.

    Just then, while King Pasenadi of Kosala was up in his finest stilt house with Queen Mallikā, he saw the monks from the group of seventeen playing in the river. He said to Queen Mallikā, "Mallikā, these perfected ones are playing in the water."

    "Great king, no doubt the Buddha hasn't laid down a rule. Either that, or these monks are ignorant."

    King Pasenadi thought, "How can the Buddha find out about these monks playing in the water without me telling him?" Having sent for those monks, King Pasenadi gave them a large lump of sugar, saying, "Sirs, please give this lump of sugar to the Buddha."

    The monks took the lump of sugar, went to the Buddha, and said, "Sir, this lump of sugar is a gift from King Pasenadi."

    "But, monks, where did you see the king?"

    "From the river Aciravatī, while playing in the water."

    The Buddha rebuked them and stated the rule.1

    Synopsis — An origin story in which the complaint never reaches the Buddha as a complaint. Pasenadi does not want to be the man who reports monks, so he sends a gift by the hand of the monks themselves; the Buddha asks them one question, where they saw the king, and the seventeen, who have done nothing they think needs hiding, answer it.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk plays in water, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "Playing in water is a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "The act of playing in the water is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Playing in the water is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "In the act of playing in water, [there is a case] involving expiation."6

    The renderings agree, and the word beneath them is less settled than any of them shows. The manuscripts read the rule-word three ways, hasa-, hāsa- and hassa-, and Ñāṇatusita derives it "probably" from the root that means being excited rather than the root that means laughing, adding that "the two roots have merged in Pali".7​ 8 On that derivation the Commentary's gloss, which he gives as "sporting/playing in the water", returns to the word rather than departing from it, and "playing", which every rendering uses, is the right English.9 Brahmāli's word-analysis renders the intention "aiming to have fun".10 How far that intention reaches is the rule's one dispute.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    Monks are watched, and this rule keeps a monk's composure, and the regard of those who watch him, from coming apart in the water. King Pasenadi saw the group of seventeen in the river from his stilt house and said to his queen, "Mallikā, these perfected ones are playing in the water." He did not want to be the man who reported monks to the Buddha, so he sent them with a gift of sugar, and when the Buddha asked where they had seen the king, they answered: "From the river Aciravatī, while playing in the water."11

    Thanissaro gives three factors, effort, object and intention, and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual two, the water and the playing.12​ 13 The depth of the water sets the object, and comes first.

    Object

    The water must be above the ankle; below it, the same play is a dukkaṭa.14​ 15 The depth sets the floor of the full offence; it is not a licence below it.

    Effort

    The effort is immersing oneself, coming up, or swimming.16 The Vibhaṅga does not say how the offences are counted. On the Commentary's count, which Thanissaro gives, each effort is an offence, so that a monk swimming for fun incurs a pācittiya for each stroke of hand or foot.17 Lesser play is a dukkaṭa: playing in a boat, striking the water with a hand, a foot, a stick or a stone, and playing with water, milk, dye or mud in a vessel.18 Thanissaro reads sailing a sailboat or steering a motorboat for pleasure into the boat clause.19 The line runs between purpose and play, not between wet and dry. Perception does not mitigate, the pattern Thanissaro refers to Pc 4.20​ 21

    Intention

    The sources agree that the factor is fun, and that the exemption covers having something to do in the water; Thanissaro's examples are bathing and helping a person who cannot swim.22 They divide on exercise.

    Disputed — whether swimming for exercise falls under the rule.

    Thanissaro thinks it probably does. Swimming for fitness, he notes, is discussed in none of the texts, and swimming itself has long been regarded in most Asian countries as a childish form of play. The one canonical mention of monks keeping their bodies strong, in the origin story of Sg 8, is disparaging, so "it does not seem likely that the Buddha would have recognized physical fitness as an appropriate reason for bhikkhus to go swimming." He allows the medical case: a monk swimming on a doctor's advice to heal a shoulder would probably count as having business in the water.23 The Concise BMC carries both halves.24 Ajahn Brahm allows exercise outright, listing it beside fording a river and bathing, and turns Thanissaro's premise to practical use: where only children swim, swimming "is nearly always regarded as playing".25 Both make the monk's purpose the test, and they differ over whether fitness is a purpose or a kind of fun. Thanissaro's argument from Sg 8 is his own, and the texts give no answer to it either way. I take Ajahn Brahm's caution as the guide, since it holds whichever way the question goes: where a community reads swimming as play, a monk swimming will be read as playing, and one who swims only when he has something to do in the water is safe on both.

    Games and horseplay in general fall under the Khandhaka's ruling on misbehaviour, a dukkaṭa on the Commentary's reading in Thanissaro's account; playing in water has a pācittiya rule of its own.26​ 27 The story shows why: it was done in a river, in view of a king, by monks who saw nothing in it to hide. Queen Mallikā's reply to the king is still a gloss on the rule: either the Buddha had not laid down a rule, or these monks were ignorant of it.28

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — playing in water less than ankle deep; playing in a boat; striking the water with the hand, the foot, a stick or a stone; playing with water, congee, milk, buttermilk, dye, urine or mud in a vessel.29
    • Dukkaṭa — where he is not playing in water but perceives that he is, or is unsure of it. Where he is, all three perceptions carry the full pācittiya.30

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he is not aiming to have fun; if, when there is something to be done, he enters the water and then immerses himself or emerges on the surface or swims; if, while crossing a body of water, he immerses himself or emerges on the surface or swims; if there is an emergency; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.31

    Thanissaro glosses the emergency as escaping a fire or a wild beast.32 Bathing is the ordinary reason a monk is in a river at all, and Pc 57 regulates how often.

    Related Offences

    • Pc 52 — tickling: the neighbouring rule, sharing this one's intention word and the first two of its non-offence clauses.
    • Pc 57 — bathing more than once a fortnight: the rule for the usual lawful reason for being in the water.
    • Sg 8 — its origin story supplies Thanissaro's evidence that physical fitness was not an esteemed pursuit for a monk.
    • Kd 15:36 — the ruling on games, athletics and horseplay generally, a dukkaṭa on the Commentary's reading.

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; the dialogue is the whole of the story and is kept verbatim and in order. Brahmāli renders the king's pāsāda "stilt house" and argues the rendering in his Appendix I. Ajahn Brahm's telling drops Queen Mallikā and states the king's motive, and the Story follows the Vibhaṅga. The Manual, the recitation, the class transcript (session 028, 12 June 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1 and BMC2, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were checked. The class is not cited: Brahmāli's allowance of exercise is Ajahn Brahm's in writing, and the shower exchange cannot be split between speakers. The Commentary is known here only through Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita.
