    Sg 1

    Story (Vatthu)

    At one time the Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove. Venerable Seyyasaka was dissatisfied with the spiritual life, and he became thin, haggard and pale, with veins protruding all over his body. Venerable Udāyī saw him and asked whether he was dissatisfied with the spiritual life. He said he was. "Well then," Udāyī said, "eat, sleep, and bathe as much as you like. And whenever you become discontent and lust overwhelms you, just masturbate with your hand." Seyyasaka asked whether that was allowable. "Yes, I do it too."

    Seyyasaka did as he was told, and after some time he had a good color, a bright face, clear skin and sharp senses. The monks who were his friends asked whether he had been taking medicine. He told them what he had been doing, and they asked, "Do you eat the food given in faith with the same hand you use to masturbate?" He said that he did. The monks of few desires complained and criticized him, rebuked him, and told the Buddha.

    The Buddha had the Saṅgha gathered, questioned Seyyasaka, and rebuked him: "Haven't I given many teachings for the sake of dispassion, not for the sake of lust; for the sake of freedom from bondage, not for the sake of bondage; for the sake of non-grasping, not for the sake of grasping? When I've taught like this, how can you choose lust, bondage, and grasping?" He went on through the teachings he had given for the fading away of lust and for the stilling of the fevers of worldly pleasures, and said that this conduct would affect people's confidence and cause some to lose it. Then he laid down the rule: intentional emission of semen is an offence entailing suspension.

    Soon afterwards some monks ate fine foods, fell asleep absentminded and heedless, and emitted semen while dreaming. They became anxious, since a dream is not without intention, and told the Buddha. "It's true, monks, that a dream is not without intention, but it's negligible." He amended the rule to except what occurs while dreaming.1

    Synopsis — Venerable Seyyasaka, dissatisfied with the spiritual life, follows Venerable Udāyī's advice to masturbate, and the Buddha lays down the rule; when monks fear they have broken it in their sleep, he excepts the negligible intention of a dream.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "Intentional emission of semen, except while dreaming, is an offense entailing suspension."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu intentionally ejaculates, except while dreaming, he incurs a saṅghādisesa."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Intentional emission of semen, except while dreaming, entails initial and subsequent meetings of the Community."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "The intentional emission of semen, except in a dream, is a saṅghādisesa."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "The intentional emission of semen, except in a dream: [this is a case] involving the community in the beginning and in the rest."6

    The five renderings agree on what the rule forbids, the rule's own wording being about as bare as a training rule gets. Where they differ is the name of the penalty, which this rule is the first to carry. Brahmāli's "suspension" names what happens to the offender; Thanissaro's "initial and subsequent meetings of the Community" and Ñāṇatusita's "in the beginning and in the rest" keep the derivation of saṅghādisesa from saṅgha and ādisesa, because only the Saṅgha can carry out the procedures that clear the offence, a point Brahmāli's endnote says "can only be understood from the Pali".7 The procedure itself belongs to the class overview at the head of this section. One word bears on practice: supinantā, "while dreaming", which Ñāṇatusita takes as "in a dream", the -anta meaning "in" or adding nothing; an older reading of it as "at the end of a dream" is taken up under Exemptions.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule guards a monk against intentional emission, since the danger in it lies on the spiritual path itself. The first four saṅghādisesas all concern sexual conduct, and Brahmāli's introduction finds it striking that sexual expression should count as so serious a fault. Part of the reason is the harm such conduct does to others; more broadly, "it reflects the danger that sexuality poses for progress on the spiritual path and the ease with which one may get trapped in sensuality."8 The origin story shows the same thing from the inside. Seyyasaka's trouble was discontent, and Udāyī's remedy cured the symptom; the Buddha's rebuke is that it chose lust, bondage and grasping, the very things his teaching was given to undo. The amendment then sets the rule's reach: a dream "is not without intention, but it's negligible", and what the rule catches is the waking will.

    Thanissaro gives the offence three factors: result, intention and effort, that is, "emission of semen caused by an intentional effort."9 All three together make the saṅghādisesa; intention and effort without the result make a thullaccaya; and any other combination, such as an effort without the intention or an intention without any effort, is no offence, as the Vibhaṅga's closing permutations set out.10 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual divides the same ground under the rule's own words, placing the effort, including getting someone else to make it, under "intentionally".11 I follow Thanissaro's three and his order, beginning with the result, since what the intention and the effort aim at is defined by it.

    Result

    The Vibhaṅga lists ten colours of semen, from blue to the colour of ghee, a classification Thanissaro traces to an ancient Indian diagnostic practice, and runs its permutations through every combination of them, down to a monk who intends one colour and emits another, to arrive at a plain conclusion: colour and quality do not matter.12 It follows, as Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm both note, that a monk who had a vasectomy before ordaining can still commit the offence, since he still discharges seminal fluid at orgasm.13

    Emission is defined as "making it move from its base".14 The Commentary places that moment where the semen enters the urinary tract, which Thanissaro glosses as "in other words, at the point of orgasm"; from there the process cannot be reversed, so the result is fulfilled even if a monk pinches the end of his penis to keep the semen from leaving the body.15 Some sub-sub-commentaries, Thanissaro reports, set the line earlier, at the small amount of clear fluid that appears before ejaculation. He finds nothing in the Vibhaṅga to prove the Commentary wrong, Ajahn Brahm holds that this fluid is not what the Pāli means by semen, and the Manual puts the result "at orgasm".16 The pre-ejaculatory fluid, then, does not complete the offence, though the effort that produced it, if it was aimed at emission, is already the thullaccaya. Ajahn Brahm draws the consequence of irreversibility the other way: a monk who stops masturbating at the edge of climax, afraid of the offence, and emits all the same has the full offence, for the emission came of an effort his intention had driven.17

    Intention

    Intentional is defined as "knowing, perceiving, having intended, having decided, he transgresses", the same definition the Vibhaṅga gives at Pj 3, Pc 61 and Pc 77.18 As the Commentary explains it, the monk knows that he is making an effort, is aware that the effort is bringing about an emission, and has made up his mind; as Thanissaro puts it, "one is not simply toying with the idea."19

    The Vibhaṅga then lists ten purposes for which a monk might bring about an emission — for health, for pleasure, for medicine, as a gift, for merit, for a sacrifice, for heaven, for seed, for investigating, and for fun — and its permutations give the full offence for every one of them, alone or in any combination.20 Thanissaro adds two modern instances: semen produced as a gift in a tantric ritual, and semen donated for artificial insemination. The Commentary, Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm report, narrows the intention to the wish to enjoy the emission, two of the ten. Thanissaro finds that the narrowing "has no basis in the Canon", and Ajahn Brahm calls it "an error of the commentator", since "the Vibhaṅga must always be preferred when there is such an inconsistency."21 The motive does not matter; the aim does.

    Without that aim there is no offence, and most of the case studies come in pairs to show it. A monk who emitted while bathing in warm water, applying medicine to a sore, scratching his scrotum, walking along a path or having his thigh massaged was told, "There's no offense if one isn't aiming at emission"; the same act done aiming at emission was the full offence if semen was emitted, and a serious offence if it was not.22 Thanissaro sorts the blameless cases into four: an emission that comes by accident, as when a monk toys with his penis for the pleasure of the contact alone; one where he does not know he is making an effort, as when dreaming or half awake; one where he is not conscious that his effort is bringing it about, as when absorbed in treating a sore; and one where his effort has another purpose, as when he grabs his penis on waking to keep the semen off his robes and bedding.23

    The first of these is not a licence. Ajahn Brahm, setting out the same cases, gives the instruction that goes with them: where the penis is excited by any such means, stop the cause at once, since "whenever there is erection the desire to emit semen is likely to arise, hard to suppress but easy to accomplish", and a monk who carries on thinking that he is in control is "simply being stupid".24 The Commentary makes the same point with a monk whose thighs are heavy enough that walking brings on an erection: if sensual fever arises, he must stop and contemplate the foulness of the body, or incur a thullaccaya simply for moving his legs. Thanissaro reads the "fever" as the wish to emit, since the Commentary elsewhere gives no penalty for enjoying the contact alone.25 The line, then, is the moment the wish arises; from there a further movement made with that wish is an effort aimed at emission.

    A monk who aims at intercourse and emits semen in the attempt is a harder case. The Commentary gives him nothing under this rule, since he did not aim at emission, and a dukkaṭa for the attempt.26 Ajahn Brahm doubts the premise: in such a state the wish to emit is among the likeliest, so that a premature emission "still counts as intentional and results in a saṅghādisesa."27 The Commentary's dukkaṭa belongs to its scheme of eleven kinds of lust, each kept to its own rule, which Thanissaro rejects at Pj 1: on that scheme a premature emission before intercourse carries no offence at all, where he places such a case under Sg 1 for an intentional emission or Sg 2 for the lustful contact, or both, since the Vibhaṅga puts an attempt at intercourse with any part of the body other than the three orifices under the saṅghādisesa rules.28 The contact itself belongs to Sg 2.

    Effort

    The Vibhaṅga defines effort in four ways: by means of one's own body, by means of something external, organic or not, by means of both, and by shaking the pelvis in the air.29 It adds five causes of the erection — sensual desire, feces, urine, intestinal gas, and being stung by caterpillars — and gives the full offence for each where the monk intends and makes an effort.30 As the ten purposes make the motive irrelevant, the five causes make the source of the arousal irrelevant. The case studies show how wide the definition is: a monk who, aiming at emission, pressed his penis with his fist, rubbed it with his thumb, with a piece of wood or against his bed, put it into a keyhole, sand or mud, poured water on it, bathed against the current, ran in water or through a lotus grove, made his body rigid, had his belly heated in the sauna or massaged his preceptor's back there, committed the full offence if semen was emitted.31

    The effort need not be the monk's own. A monk who, aiming at emission, told a novice, "Take hold of my penis", and emitted, was given the full offence, and the Manual makes getting someone else to apply the effort part of the factor.32 Two case studies under Pj 1 go a step further: a woman offered to bring a monk to emission with her hand, he let her, and the Buddha gave a saṅghādisesa. Thanissaro concludes that a monk who acquiesces while someone else brings about his emission is not excused because he lay perfectly still.33

    The Commentary adds a requirement the Vibhaṅga does not state: that the effort be directed at the penis itself, so that a monk who brought on an emission by stimulating some other part of his body would have no offence under this rule.34 Thanissaro shows that this runs into the case studies — the belly heated in the sauna, the preceptor's back, the massaged thigh — which the Commentary meets by rewriting them, and in the belly case by denying that such an effort could be an offence at all, which is no longer commenting but rewriting the rule; the added factor cannot stand.35 Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes taught the Commentary's view and carry a dated note of 2007 withdrawing it in favour of Thanissaro's; his body text was not revised to match, so a reader of those pages still meets the older view.36 Any bodily effort aimed at emission, anywhere, fulfils the factor.

    Disputed — whose semen was emitted in the case of the sleeping novice, and so what the case shows.

    A monk took hold of the penis of a sleeping novice and semen was emitted, and the Buddha gave no saṅghādisesa but "an offense of wrong conduct".37 Thanissaro notes that the Pāli does not say whose semen it was, and reads it as the novice's: the case then shows that this rule concerns making oneself emit, not another, and the dukkaṭa is for lustful contact with another man, which belongs to Sg 2; read the other way, the Buddha's not asking whether the monk aimed at emission would depart from the standard pattern of the case studies, though Thanissaro adds that nothing else in the rule or the Vibhaṅga makes the point, which would explain the monk's own uncertainty whether he had offended.38 Brahmāli, whose translation reads "the monk emitted semen", answers in an endnote that these rules concern monks, not novices, so that a ruling of no saṅghādisesa "would make no sense if it referred to the novice monk", and that the case studies are too "widely diverging" for every one of them to be a potential saṅghādisesa; Ajahn Brahm reads the case the same way, but on the ground that the effort did not touch the monk's own penis, the factor his 2007 note withdraws.39​ 40 Thanissaro has an answer to the objection from the ruling's words, so the dispute is close; I lean, slightly, to Brahmāli's reading, which the ruling's own words fit without a further step. Either way the dukkaṭa stands and the Commentary's added factor falls, since the belly, back and thigh cases defeat it on any reading of this one; what the dispute decides is only what this case teaches, and on that a slight lean is all I offer.

    Effort is bodily, and two case studies mark where it stops. A monk who was thinking a worldly thought and emitted was told, "There's no offense for one thinking a worldly thought."41 And a lustful monk who stared at a woman's genitals and emitted was given no saṅghādisesa, but the Buddha laid down a separate offence of wrong conduct for staring at a woman's genitals motivated by lust.42 Thanissaro reads this as showing that effort with the eyes is not bodily effort under this rule, and the dukkaṭa as holding whatever the intention or result; the Commentary extends it to the genitals of a female animal and to the place on a clothed woman's body where they are, Thanissaro to a pornographic photograph, and the Concise BMC to "any visual representation".43

    No offence is not the same as no harm. A monk who emits while thinking sensual thoughts, making no physical effort, has no offence whether or not he wished for it and whether or not he enjoyed it; but the Commentary warns that one should not let oneself be overcome by such thoughts, and Thanissaro gives the reason in a simile the Buddha told of his own striving. Wet, sappy timber will not light however long a man works the fire-stick, and so it is with a contemplative who keeps away from sensuality in body while its "desire, infatuation, urge, thirst, and fever" are not stilled within him: he "is incapable of knowledge, vision, and unexcelled self-awakening."44 The rule can reach only the body. The simile is about everything the rule cannot reach, and it is the reason the rule exists.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Thullaccaya — intention and effort without emission, the "serious offense" of the closing permutations and the case studies. Ajahn Brahm holds that a single deliberate touch of the penis, made to emit, is enough.45
    • Thullaccaya — an effort aimed at emission made before sleep, even where a change of heart before sleeping spares the emission that follows (see Exemptions).46
    • Dukkaṭa — staring lustfully at a woman's genitals, whatever the intention or result, with the extensions given under Effort.47
    • Dukkaṭa — taking hold of a sleeping novice's penis, the case disputed under Effort.48

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if it is while dreaming; if he is not aiming at emission; if he is insane; if he is deranged; if he is overwhelmed by pain; or if he is the first offender.49 The second has been covered under Intention.

    The dream exemption covers the will inside the dream, not an effort made before it or a movement made after waking. The Commentary rejects an old reading of "while dreaming" as "at the end of a dream", which would have allowed an effort on waking from a wet dream: what happens in sleep is the Abhidhamma's concern, it says, and what happens after waking is the Vinaya's.50 Thanissaro reports the Commentary's advice to a monk who wakes in the course of a wet dream: he should lie still and make no move that would be an effort; if the emission then comes of itself he has no offence, whether or not he enjoys it, and he has none if he takes hold of his penis only to keep the semen off his robes or bedding.51 Ajahn Brahm marks the edge on both sides: a monk who wakes just before the climax and, wishing for the emission, merely touches his penis has the intention and the effort in the waking state and the full offence, while a monk only half awake, not yet aware that he is making an effort, has none.52

    An effort made before sleep counts as an effort made awake, a ruling of the Commentary's that Thanissaro reports. A monk overcome with lust who grips his penis with his fist or thighs and falls asleep in that position hoping for an emission has the full offence if it comes; if instead he calms the lust by reflecting on the foulness of the body and falls asleep with a pure mind, he has none for the emission.53 Thanissaro adds that the Sub-commentary requires the effort, as well as the lust, to be given up, and that the earlier effort is still a thullaccaya.54 Ajahn Brahm draws the line at the effort: a monk who goes to sleep hoping for a wet dream has no offence if the movement that brings it on happens in the dream, but the full offence if he arranges his body for it before he sleeps.55

    Related Offences

    • Sg 2 — lustful bodily contact with a woman: the home of the contact in an attempt at intercourse, and of lustful contact with a man, which the sleeping-novice case touches.
    • Pj 1 — the two case studies of a woman bringing a monk to emission with her hand, and the Commentary's eleven kinds of lust, rejected there.
    • Pj 3, Pc 61, Pc 77 — the same four-term definition of "intentional".

    Related Suttas

    • MN 36 — the wet, sappy timber that will not catch fire however long the fire-stick is worked: one who keeps away from sensuality in body, while its "fever" is not stilled within, is "incapable of knowledge and vision". The simile reaches what the rule cannot.56
    • SN 35.240 — the tortoise that draws its limbs into its shell until the jackal leaves, "finding no opening": the guarded senses, which keep the will this rule catches from forming.57
    • SN 47.7 — the monkey caught in the hunter's pitch by hand, foot and muzzle, for straying outside its own resort; for a monk, the domain of others is "the five cords of sensual pleasure", where Udāyī sent Seyyasaka for a cure.58
