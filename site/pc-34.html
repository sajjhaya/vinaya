    Pc 34

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. There was a female lay follower, Kāṇamātā, who had faith and confidence. She had a daughter, Kāṇā, who had been given in marriage to a man in a certain village.1

    On one occasion Kāṇā went to her mother's house on some business. Kāṇā's husband sent her a message: "Please come, Kāṇā, I want you back." Kāṇamātā thought, "It's shameful to go empty-handed," and she baked some cookies. Just when the cookies were finished, an alms-collecting monk entered Kāṇamātā's house, and she gave him some. After leaving, he told another monk, and he too was given cookies. And the same happened a third time. At that, all the cookies were gone. A second time Kāṇā's husband sent her the same message, and everything unfolded as before. A third time he sent the same message, adding, "If Kāṇā doesn't come, I'll find another wife." But once again all the cookies were given to monks. Kāṇā's husband found another wife, and when Kāṇā heard what had happened, she cried.2

    Soon afterwards the Buddha went to Kāṇamātā's house and sat down on the prepared seat. Kāṇamātā approached, bowed, and sat down. The Buddha asked her why Kāṇā was crying, and she told him what had happened. After instructing, inspiring, and gladdening her with a teaching, he got up from his seat and left.3

    Soon afterwards a certain caravan was ready to go south from Rājagaha. An alms-collecting monk went up to that caravan to get almsfood, and a lay follower gave him flour products. After leaving, that monk told another monk, and he too was given flour products. And the same happened a third time. At that, all his provisions were gone. That lay follower said to the other people in the caravan, "Sirs, please wait one day. I've given my provisions to the monks. I need to prepare more." — "We can't wait. The caravan is already on its way." And they left. After preparing provisions, that lay follower followed after the caravan, but he was robbed by thieves. People complained and criticized them, "How can the Sakyan monastics receive without moderation? This man gave to them, and then because he was following after the caravan he was robbed by thieves."4

    The monks heard the complaints of those people and they told the Buddha. Soon afterwards he gave a teaching and addressed the monks: "Well then, monks, I will lay down a training rule for the following ten reasons: for the well-being of the Sangha, for the comfort of the Sangha, for the restraint of bad people, for the ease of good monks, for the restraint of the corruptions relating to the present life, for the restraint of the corruptions relating to future lives, to give rise to confidence in those without it, to increase the confidence of those who have it, for the longevity of the true Teaching, and for supporting the training." He then stated the rule.5

    Synopsis — A mother bakes travelling-gifts for a daughter who must not go back to her husband empty-handed, and a chain of monks — each told of the baking by the last — empties the house three times over until her husband takes another wife; a second chain does the same to a caravan traveller's provisions and leaves him to follow the caravan alone and be robbed; and the rule that follows caps what a monk may accept and turns the chain of telling that caused the harm into the duty that prevents it.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk goes to a family and is invited to take cookies or crackers, he may accept two or three bowlfuls if he wishes. If he accepts more than that, he commits an offense entailing confession. If he accepts two or three bowlfuls, he should take it away and share it with the monks. This is the proper procedure."6

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu is invited by a household to take as many cookies as he likes, he should accept three bowlfuls at most. If he accepts more than that, he incurs a pācittiya."7

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "In case a bhikkhu arriving at a family residence is presented with cakes or cooked grain-meal, he may accept two or three bowlfuls if he so desires. If he should accept more than that, it is to be confessed. Having accepted the two-or-three bowlfuls and having taken them from there, he is to share them among the bhikkhus. This is the proper course here."8

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Accepting more than three bowlfuls of food which was meant for presents or provisions for a journey is an offence of Pācittya."9

    Ñāṇatusita

    "Now, should a family invite a bhikkhu who has approached to take as many cakes and parched cakes [as he likes], by a bhikkhu who is wishing [so] two or three bowls full [of cakes] can be accepted; if he should accept more than that, [this is a case] involving expiation. Having accepted two or three bowls full, having taken [them] away from there, [it] is to be shared together with [other] bhikkhus. This is the proper procedure here."10

    The renderings agree on what the rule requires and differ over what to call its food. Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation has "cookies" for pūva and "crackers" for mantha, and his Appendix I rejects "cakes" for pūva, since the Vibhaṅga's pūvas were cooked and dry enough to send as gifts; Thanissaro has "cakes or cooked grain-meal", Ñāṇatusita "cakes and parched cakes", and Ajahn Brahm names no food at all and gives only its purpose.11 I use Brahmāli's cookies and crackers, and the choice decides nothing, because the Vibhaṅga defines both words by what the food was prepared for, and that definition, discussed below under Object, is where the rule's reach is decided. The recitation line keeps only "cookies" and puts the ceiling as "three bowlfuls at most" where the rule says "two or three"; the ceiling is the same.12

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    Moderation in receiving is a monk's share in every gift, and this rule holds him to it where a household's generosity is most exposed, so that what a family gives freely is never turned against it, for the good faith of those who give. No monk in the origin stories takes much. Three in succession empty Kāṇamātā's house, each acting on what the last one told him, until her daughter's husband takes another wife; a second chain empties a traveller's provisions, and he follows his caravan alone and is robbed. The people's complaint names the fault exactly, "How can the Sakyan monastics receive without moderation?", and the Buddha laid the rule down for the ten reasons, without a rebuke.13​ 14

    Thanissaro gives the rule's purpose as preventing bhikkhus "from abusing a donor's generosity and good faith", and two factors, effort and object; I take the object first, and put the rule's procedure under Effort, where it belongs.15

    Object

    The object is cookies and crackers, and the Vibhaṅga defines them by purpose, not by substance: cookies are "whatever has been prepared for the purpose of sending away", crackers "whatever has been prepared as provisions for a journey".16 Brahmāli's Appendix I quotes the Commentary reading the first the same way, counting any fine cake prepared as a gift as a pūva.17 Thanissaro accordingly speaks of presents and provisions, and adds a limit that keeps the second honest: the journey is one the donors themselves are planning, so food they have prepared for a journey the monk is about to take is outside the rule.18

    Disputed — whether the purpose-definitions widen the rule beyond these two foods.

    Thanissaro reports the Vinaya-mukha reasoning by the Great Standards that any food prepared in large quantities, for sale or for a party, banquet or reception, should fall under the rule as well.19 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual takes the opposite view and gives its reason: the definitions explain why the householders made so much, they do not bring every food into the rule, and "if al[l] types of food were intended, the rule would not have used the words pūva and mantha."20 On one reading the definitions state the rule's criterion; on the other they explain the story. As an account of what the rule says, I find Brahmāli's the stronger, since a criterion of bulk preparation would have needed no food-names at all. But the Vinaya-mukha is making a claim about what the rule should be extended to rather than about what it says, and Brahmāli's objection does not quite reach it. A monk offered the run of a wedding table is inside the rule on one reading and outside it on the other, and the texts do not choose; a monk who keeps the measure and the telling with cookies and crackers and any other food prepared in bulk is safe on both.

    Effort

    The effort is accepting more than the measure, and the Vibhaṅga makes the open invitation part of the case: is invited to take means "Take as much as you like."21 Thanissaro notes that receiving is defined in the context of such an invitation.22 What size a bowl is belongs to NP 21. Perception does not mitigate: whether the monk knows he has taken more, doubts it, or believes he has taken less, the full offence stands, on the pattern of Pc 4.23 And the offence falls at the acceptance. The rules on either side of this one locate the offence in the eating; this one has no eating clause, and its harm is done to the household before anyone has eaten.24

    The rule attaches a procedure to an acceptance within the measure, and the origin story explains why: what emptied Kāṇamātā's house was information, each monk coming because the last had told him. Having accepted two or three bowlfuls, the monk, on leaving, tells any monk he sees that he has accepted that much from such-and-such a place, and that the other should accept nothing there. Not telling is an offence of wrong conduct, and so is accepting after being told, which Brahmāli's endnote places on the monk who was told.25​ 26 Thanissaro gives the Commentary's arithmetic, by which the ceiling holds for all the monks together and not for each: a monk who took two tells the next to take one at most and everyone else to take nothing, so that "all-in-all, the bhikkhus accept a total of no more than three". Ajahn Brahm gives the same arithmetic.27​ 28

    What was taken is then carried back from almsround and shared.29 On the Commentary's reckoning, which Thanissaro reports, the monk may keep one bowlful and must share the rest "among an entire Community, i.e., not just among his friends"; a monk who took a single bowlful may do with it as he likes.30 Ajahn Brahm, noting that food not meant as presents or provisions may be accepted beyond three bowlfuls without offence, adds a practical question of his own: "though how one carries them away is not quite clear!"31

    What lasts is the shape of the procedure. The rule does not refuse the first monk his bowlfuls; it asks him to say so, so that the next monk knows what has already been taken from a house that offered freely. The rule keeps the telling and changes what is told.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — on leaving, seeing a monk and not telling him what he has accepted and where.32
    • Dukkaṭa — for the monk who, having been told, accepts anything from that place.33​ 34
    • Dukkaṭa — where it is less than two or three bowlfuls but he perceives it as more, or is unsure. Where it is more, all three perceptions carry the full pācittiya.35

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he accepts two or three bowlfuls; if he accepts less than two or three bowlfuls; if they give anything that has not been prepared for sending away or as provisions for a journey; if they give the leftovers from what was prepared for sending away or as provisions for a journey; if they give after the plans to travel have been canceled; if it is from relatives; if it is from those who have given an invitation; if it is for the benefit of someone else; if it is by means of his own property; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.36

    Two of these have been read further. From relatives and from those who have given an invitation, Thanissaro reports the Commentary allowing more than three bowlfuls given outright; but if they say take as much as you like from presents or provisions, "the proper course is to take only two or three bowlfuls".37 Ajahn Brahm reaches the same limit by comparison with NP 7, whose invitation phrase is the same.38 On taking for the benefit of someone else, Thanissaro offers the reading the context allows: the monk takes extra "not on his own initiative, but because the donor asks him to".39 There is no exemption for illness. Ñāṇatusita notes that the Dharmaguptaka and Mahīśāsaka recensions give one, where the Pāli exempts taking for the sake of another instead.40

    Related Offences

    • NP 7 — where the phrase "invited to take as much as he likes" also governs the rule; Ajahn Brahm reads the two together.
    • NP 21 — where bowl sizes are defined; a "bowlful" here is a bowl of one of those sizes.
    • Pc 4 — the rule whose perception pattern this one follows, where perception does not mitigate.
    • Pc 35 — the next rule, on eating after a monk has finished his meal and refused more, where the offence falls at the eating.
    • Pc 41, Pc 51 — the other rules in which pūva appears, and the evidence Brahmāli's Appendix I uses to fix what a pūva is.

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, both sub-stories, in the de-dramatised register; the three repetitions are given by the Vibhaṅga's own compression, the third message's escalation is kept as the source's device, and the rule-laying, which has no questioning and no rebuke, is carried in full. The Manual, Brahmāli's Appendix I, the recitation, the class transcript (session 026, 31 May 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were checked; the class is not cited, since the written sources carry each point it makes. Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm each retell the origin story with details the Vibhaṅga lacks, and these are not carried into the Story. The Commentary is known here through Thanissaro and Brahmāli's Appendix I, and the Vinaya-mukha through Thanissaro; neither was consulted directly.
