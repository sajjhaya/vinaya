    NP 29

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time monks who had completed the rainy-season residence were staying in wilderness dwellings. Thieves who were active during the month of Kattika attacked those monks, thinking, "They have been given things."

    The monks told the Buddha. Soon afterwards the Buddha gave a teaching and addressed the monks: "Monks, I allow monks who are staying in wilderness dwellings to store one of their three robes in an inhabited area."

    When they heard about this, monks stored one of their three robes in inhabited areas, staying apart from them for more than six days. The robes were lost, destroyed, burned, and eaten by rats. As a consequence, those monks became poorly dressed. Other monks asked them why, and they told them what had happened. The monks of few desires complained and criticised them: "How could those monks store one of their three robes in an inhabited area and then stay apart from it for more than six days?"

    After rebuking those monks in many ways, they told the Buddha. Soon afterwards he had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned the monks: "Is it true, monks, that there are monks who do this?"

    "It's true, sir."

    The Buddha rebuked them — "How could those foolish men do this? This will affect people's confidence" — and laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — Thieves attack monks in wilderness dwellings after the rains, and the Buddha allows them to store one of their three robes in an inhabited area; when monks stay apart from the stored robes for more than six days and the robes are lost, he makes staying apart from such a robe for more than six days an offence entailing relinquishment.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "There are wilderness dwellings that are considered risky and dangerous. After observing the Kattika full moon that ends the rainy season, a monk who is staying in such a dwelling may, if he so desires, store one of his three robes in an inhabited area so long as he has a reason for staying apart from that robe. He should stay apart from that robe for six days at the most. If he stays apart from it longer than that, except if the monks have agreed, he commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu has completed the rains residence and is staying in a dangerous wilderness dwelling, he may keep one of his three robes in an inhabited area. If he then stays apart from that robe for more than six days, except with the Saṅgha's agreement, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "There are wilderness lodgings that are considered dubious and risky. A bhikkhu living in such lodgings after having observed the Kattika full moon may keep any one of his three robes in a village if he so desires. Should he have any reason to live apart from the robe, he may do so for six nights at most. If he should live apart from it beyond that—unless authorized by the bhikkhus—it is to be forfeited and confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "A monk who has completed the Rains-Retreat and is staying in a forest monastery, endangered by thieves, during the last month of the rainy-season may leave one of his three robes in a nearby village for safe-keeping, but should he then be separated from this robe for more than six days he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "Now, the Kattika-full-moon has been observed. [There are] those wilderness lodgings which are considered risky, which are dangerous. A bhikkhu dwelling in such kind of lodgings, who is wishing [to do so], may put aside one of the three robes inside an inhabited area. And if there may be any reason for that bhikkhu for dwelling apart from that robe, the bhikkhu can dwell apart from that robe for six days at the most; if he should dwell apart for more than that, except with the authorisation of bhikkhus, [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture."6

    The renderings agree on what the rule requires. The opening word, upavassaṃ, is read by the Commentary as "after observing", which Brahmāli, Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm follow; Ñāṇatusita reads it as a participle, "has been observed", and notes that an absolutive does not normally open a clause with no main verb; the sense, that the rains are over, is the same.7 Ajahn Brahm folds the Commentary's conditions into his rendering, the thieves and the last month of the rains.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule guards the wilderness life and the robes it depends on. Monks went out to the wilderness after the rains, thieves came for them because "They have been given things", and the Buddha allowed a robe to be stored in a village; when monks left their robes there unvisited, the robes were lost, burned and eaten by rats, and the monks went poorly dressed.8 Thanissaro reads the season the same way: thieves were active then, perhaps because the monks had just received new requisites, and the allowance gives a monk in a dangerous wilderness lodging a safe place for one robe.9 The allowance answers the thieves; the six days answer the neglect.

    This rule is a satellite of NP 2: the three robes, the Saṅgha's agreement and what it is to be apart from a robe are NP 2's.10 Thanissaro's two factors are object, any one of the three robes, and effort, staying apart from it beyond six days, and he reports the Commentary's four conditions for the circumstance in which the allowance is available.11​ 12

    Circumstance

    The monk has spent the first rains without a break; one who did not keep the rains, or kept the later rains, may not use the allowance, Ajahn Brahm adds.13​ 14 It is the month after the rains, and no later.15​ 16 His lodging is a wilderness one, at least five hundred bow-lengths from the village, which Brahmāli gives as 800 metres, measured by the shortest walkable path, yet near enough that he can walk for alms in the morning and return to eat before noon.17​ 18 And the lodging is risky and dangerous, which the Vibhaṅga defines by what has been seen: thieves camping, eating, standing, sitting or lying down in or near the monastery, or thieves injuring, robbing or beating people there.19 How far "near" reaches is not defined for this rule; Thanissaro suggests it was perhaps felt unwise to fix a danger zone too exactly.20 Thanissaro adds that a monk whose kaṭhina privileges are in effect needs no allowance, since NP 2 does not bind him.21

    Disputed — whether this rule grants a monk something NP 2 would deny him, or limits a freedom NP 2 already gives.

    The answer depends on NP 2's own dispute, whether that rule is lifted in the month after the rains for a monk without the robe-making ceremony, which is argued at NP 2. Thanissaro holds that it is not lifted, so this rule provides the endangered wilderness monk a safe place for one robe, with six nights apart; Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual and Ajahn Brahm hold that it is, so this rule limits an open allowance, the Manual stating that it "places a constraint on the exemption given in NP2."22​ 23​ 24 The modern teachers remain divided; the question is NP 2's, and it is left there.

    Object

    Any one of the three robes, stored anywhere in the monk's alms village.25 The reason the rule asks for is glossed simply as "if there is something to be done".26

    Effort

    Staying apart from the robe beyond six days. On the Manual's count the offence falls on the sixth dawn counting from when the monk was last in the robe's presence, which the Vibhaṅga states from the other end, "at dawn on the seventh day"; whether a monk in his lodging is apart from the robe at all is discussed below.27​ 28 Ajahn Brahm, reading the rule the same way, gives the practice: leave the robe in the village five nights and spend the sixth with it, and the robe is not neglected.29 Perception does not excuse: a monk who thinks the dawn has not yet come, when it has, commits the offence all the same.30

    Disputed — whether a monk staying in his own wilderness lodging is apart from the robe he has left in the village.

    A non-offence clause can be read two ways, and in Thanissaro's report the commentaries take both. On the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī's reading, a monk in his wilderness lodging counts as apart from the robe, so the six-day count governs his whole month, and he enters the village territory for the seventh dawn to begin another six. The Commentary takes the clause to cover a monk away from both lodging and village, who as the seventh dawn nears enters the village, checks on his robe and returns; from this, Thanissaro reports, the Sub-commentary concludes that a monk staying in his lodging is not apart from the robe at all.31 Thanissaro finds the Commentary's reading of the clause forced, but the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī's at odds with the Vibhaṅga's gloss of "any reason" as business, which suggests time away from the lodging; he prefers the Commentary's, and leaves the choice to each Community, since both serve the rule's purpose.32 The Manual takes the reason clause to refer only back to the dangerous dwelling, which, if right, removes the support for Thanissaro's objection.33 On the other side of the dispute stand the Manual's factor and Ajahn Brahm, who both take the presence test, the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī's side: the count runs from when the monk was last in the robe's presence, wherever he stays, and the remedy is to go to the robe.34​ 35 The texts do not decide it, but the practice does not wait on it: the five-night cycle, the practice of that side, satisfies both readings.

    The allowance protects the robe from thieves; the limit protects it from the monk himself, and the remedy is no more than going to look at it.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — using a robe that should be relinquished, without first relinquishing it.36
    • Dukkaṭa — fewer than six days having passed, perceiving more, or unsure.37

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he stays apart from the robe for six days or less; if, after staying apart from it for six days, he stays overnight within the village zone and then leaves; if within the six days he gives up the determination, or the robe has been given away, lost, destroyed, burned, stolen, or taken on trust; if he has the permission of the monks; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.38 The village zone, Thanissaro suggests, is the village's territory, the land from which it collects taxes as well as its houses.39 The monks' permission is NP 2's, given to a sick monk by a transaction of motion and one announcement.40

    Related Offences

    • NP 2 — the anchor: the three robes, being apart, the Saṅgha's permission, and the dispute over the month after the rains on which this rule depends.
    • NP 1 — the relinquishment, and the extra cloth a rescinded robe becomes.
    • NP 28 — the other rule of the month after the rains.
    • Pd 4 — the other rule built on the risky and dangerous wilderness dwelling.
