    Pc 4

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī, in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time the monks from the group of six were instructing lay followers to memorise the Teaching. Those lay followers became disrespectful, undeferential, and rude towards the monks. The monks of few desires complained and criticised them: "How can the monks from the group of six instruct lay followers to memorise the Teaching?" After rebuking them in many ways, they told the Buddha. Soon afterwards he had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned the monks: "Is it true, monks, that you do this?"

    "It's true, sir."

    The Buddha rebuked them: "Foolish men, how can you do this? This will affect people's confidence." Then he laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — The group of six drill lay followers in reciting the Teaching by rote until the laypeople grow disrespectful of the monks, and the rule against teaching the Dhamma by rote to the unordained follows.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk instructs a person who is not fully ordained to memorize the Teaching, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu teaches an unordained person to learn the word of the Buddha by heart, he incurs a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu have an unordained person recite Dhamma line by line (with him), it is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Teaching Dhamma by rote to novices or laypeople is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should have one who has not been fully admitted [into the community] recite the Dhamma [line] by line, [this is a case] involving expiation."6

    The renderings name one act, rote drilling, and differ only in how literally they take padaso, "by the line". Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita keep it, "line by line"; Brahmāli's "memorize" and Ajahn Brahm's "by rote" give its purpose instead, and Brahmāli's endnote says so: the phrase means "causing (someone) to say the Teaching by the line", which implies helping another to memorise it.7

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    Monks and the lay people they teach are joined by respect, and the rule guards it by keeping a monk's teaching from becoming a drill, for the confidence of the laity and the good name of the Saṅgha. The Story gives only the effect: drilled in reciting the Teaching, the lay followers "became disrespectful, undeferential, and rude toward the monks", and the Buddha's rebuke speaks of their confidence.8 Why should the drill have had that effect? The texts do not say. Thanissaro suggests that memorising and reciting the texts was felt to be the province of the ordained, so that a monk who trained lay reciters might be seen as shirking his duty, as less protective of his tradition than the brahmins were of the Vedas, or in time as the layman's hired tutor; Ajahn Brahm likewise reads the practice as a cause of a "disharmonious monk-laity relationship".9​ 10 It is a rule about a method of teaching, not about the Dhamma reaching the laity. A monk may teach the Dhamma to lay people by discourse or by question and answer; what he may not do is drill an unordained person in reciting it by rote.11

    On Thanissaro's count the offence has two factors, effort and object; Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual lifts out what counts as the Dhamma as a third.12​ 13 Thanissaro's two serve here, and the Dhamma falls under effort, where he places it.

    Effort

    The Vibhaṅga describes four ways of drilling a text: reciting together, starting and finishing together; one starting a line and both finishing it; prompting with the next syllable; and the pupil supplying the next phrase alone.14 The aim, as Thanissaro puts it, is "to train him/her by rote to be a skilled reciter of a text", and the offences are counted by the unit of the drill, one for every line, or for every syllable.15​ 16 Ajahn Brahm adds that any method amounting to rote teaching is included, and that teaching the pronunciation of a Pāli word by having someone repeat it is not.17

    What is drilled must be the Teaching, which the Vibhaṅga defines as what was spoken by the Buddha, by disciples, by sages and by gods, connected with the beneficial and with the Teaching.18 The Manual estimates it as passages from the four main Nikāyas and the four main books of the Vinaya, "a rough estimate of what the Dhamma would have consisted of at the time of the Buddha".19 Whether translations count is disputed, and the argument belongs to Pc 7, where the same definition carries most weight; Thanissaro notes that it has caused no controversy at this rule.20 In practice the question seldom arises. The Canon is in print, and what monks still learn by heart are such texts as the Pātimokkha and the chants; to train a novice or a lay person to recite these by rote is the full offence.21​ 22

    Object

    The pupil is anyone but a fully ordained monk or nun: a novice or a lay person.23 Intention does not soften the offence. A monk training a mixed group of monks and novices offends for the novices even if he means to train only the monks.24 Nor does perception: if the pupil is unordained, it is the full offence whether the monk perceives him so, is unsure, or takes him for ordained; if the pupil is ordained and taken for unordained or doubted, it is a dukkaṭa. Thanissaro sets out this pattern of six cases here, since many later rules repeat it.25​ 26

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The rule reaches the drill and nothing adjacent to it. Thanissaro reads the exemption for practising together as covering monks who chant with lay people reading from a book or reciting from memory, as at the evening chanting, and who are not teaching them.27 A monk who takes his place in that chanting, or prompts a lay reciter, has broken nothing.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • A dukkaṭa where the pupil is in fact fully ordained but the monk perceives him as unordained, or is unsure.28

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if the recitation is done together; if they practise together; if he prompts one who is speaking a mostly familiar text; if he prompts one who is reciting; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.29 In Thanissaro's report the Commentary extends the allowance to a monk learning a text from an unordained person, probably on the model of the Itivuttaka, which the monks first learned from a servant woman.30

    Related Offences

    • Pc 7 — teaching more than six sentences of Dhamma to a woman with no knowledgeable man present; the rule where the definition of "Dhamma" is argued in full.
    • Pc 69, 70 — associating with (including sharing the Dhamma with) a monk suspended for not giving up a wrong view, and with an expelled novice; with Pc 7, the limits Ajahn Brahm names on to whom a monk may teach.31
    • Sk 57–72 — the training rules on teaching the Dhamma only to those who listen respectfully, the manner of teaching as against its method.32
