    Pc 41

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying in the house with the peaked roof in the Great Wood near Vesālī. The Saṅgha had an abundance of fresh food. Venerable Ānanda told the Buddha, who said, "Well then, Ānanda, give the cookies to those who take leftovers."

    "Yes, sir." Ānanda had them sit in a row and gave them one cookie each, until he accidentally gave two to a female wanderer. The female wanderers sitting next to her said to her, "This monastic is your lover."

    "He's not. He gave me two, thinking they were one."

    And a second time. And a third time Ānanda gave them one cookie each, until he accidentally gave two to that same female wanderer. Once again the female wanderers sitting next to her said to her, "This monastic is your lover."

    "He's not. He gave me two, thinking they were one."

    And they started to argue about whether or not they were lovers.

    A certain Ājīvaka ascetic, too, went to that distribution of food. A monk mixed rice with a large amount of ghee and gave him a large lump. He took it and left. Another Ājīvaka asked him, "Where did you get that lump?"

    "From the food distribution of the ascetic Gotama, that shaven-headed householder."

    Some lay followers overheard that conversation between those Ājīvaka ascetics. They then went to the Buddha, bowed, sat down, and said, "Sir, these monastics of other religions want to disparage the Buddha, the Teaching, and the Sangha. It would be good if the monks didn't personally give anything to the monastics of other religions."

    After the Buddha had instructed, inspired, and gladdened those lay followers with a teaching, they got up from their seats, bowed down, circumambulated him with their right sides toward him, and left. Soon afterwards the Buddha gave a teaching and addressed the monks, laying down the training rule for the ten reasons, and stated the rule.1​ 2

    Synopsis — Ānanda, distributing surplus food to the wanderers who lived on the monastery's leftovers, hands the same woman two cookies three times over and sets her neighbours gossiping; then a monk hands an Ājīvaka a lump of rice and ghee, and the Ājīvaka goes off calling the Buddha a shaven-headed householder. It is the second incident that produces the rule, and the request comes from the lay followers who overheard it.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk personally gives fresh or cooked food to a naked ascetic, to a male wanderer, or to a female wanderer, he commits an offense entailing confession."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu gives food directly to a non-Buddhist mendicant, he incurs a pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu give staple or non-staple food with his own hand to a naked ascetic, a male wanderer, or a female wanderer, it is to be confessed."5

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Giving food with one's own hand to one ordained outside of Buddhism is an offence of Pācittya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should give with his own hand uncooked food or cooked food to a naked ascetic or to a male wanderer or to a female wanderer, [this is a case] involving expiation."7

    The renderings agree, and their differences are compressions. Sahatthā, "with his own hand", is kept by all but Brahmāli's recitation, which says "directly".8 The food-words are Pc 29's question. The one compression that matters is the list. The rule names three kinds of person, and Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga, Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita keep all three, where Brahmāli's recitation has "a non-Buddhist mendicant" and Ajahn Brahm "one ordained outside of Buddhism".9​ 10​ 11​ 12 The shorter lines answer a question the three-term form leaves open, whether monastics of other Buddhist traditions are included, and it belongs to Object.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    Handing food to a monastic of another religion puts a monk in the place of that religion's lay supporter, and this rule guards his standing as one who has gone forth, together with the confidence of the lay followers who asked for it. The first incident, Ānanda's double cookie, ends only in gossip; the second shows the injury. An Ājīvaka given a lump of rice and ghee goes off calling the Buddha "that shaven-headed householder", and the insult is about status: other religions observed the same formalities of receiving food from their lay followers, so a monk who hands food over in that way, as Thanissaro puts it, "would be placing himself in the position of a lay follower of that religion".13 The Ājīvaka said it to another Ājīvaka, and it was lay followers who overheard it and asked for the rule: "It would be good if the monks didn't personally give anything to the monastics of other religions."14

    Thanissaro treats the rule under two factors, object and effort.15​ 16

    Object

    The Vibhaṅga defines the three kinds of person by exclusion. A naked ascetic is "any wanderer who is naked"; a male wanderer is any apart from Buddhist monks and novices; a female wanderer any apart from Buddhist nuns, trainee nuns and novice nuns.17 Between them they cover everyone who has gone forth except the five Buddhist categories, and Thanissaro carries the rule into the present by the Great Standards: anyone ordained in another religion now falls under it, a Catholic priest or a rabbi as much as a wandering ascetic.18 Ajahn Brahm reaches the same place and adds the limit from the other side: the rule does not touch lay people of other religions.19

    Whether it touches monastics of other Buddhist traditions the sources answer differently in kind. Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual makes the object anyone ordained "who is not a lay person and not a Buddhist", and Ajahn Brahm writes that the rule does not prohibit giving food to Buddhist monks of other traditions.20​ 21 Thanissaro takes no side and reports that Communities differ over "people ordained in other Buddhist religions — such as Zen priests or Tibetan lamas", and the Concise BMC repeats him.22​ 23 So the two sources who state a view exclude them, though the texts do not settle it, and a monk whose Community takes the wider view has the indirect giving described below, which costs nothing.

    The thing given is fresh or cooked food, which between them take in every edible, tonics and medicines included. Water and tooth cleaners are the rule's exceptions, as at Pc 40, but here they are not free: giving them is an offence of wrong conduct.24​ 25​ 26 Perception does not mitigate. Whether the monk knows the person is a monastic of another religion, is unsure, or does not realise it, the full offence stands; mistaking someone else for one is wrong conduct.27

    Effort

    The effort is giving, and gives is defined in the three modes of Pc 40: by body, by what is connected to the body, or by releasing.28 Thanissaro calls this rule the corollary of that one. Pc 40 governs what a monk may put in his own mouth, and this rule what he may put into another's hand.29 And so the rule regulates a gesture, not a gift. Nothing stops the food reaching the recipient; the rule closes the one way of handing it over that would make the monk look like a donor.

    The non-offence clauses make the situation easy to manage, because a monk may have someone else give the food, or put it down near the person.30 The one who gives on his behalf must not be fully ordained: Thanissaro reports the Commentary adding the limit, and the Concise BMC traces it to the nuns' version of the rule.31​ 32 Ajahn Brahm gives the counsel for a Catholic priest staying as a guest: place the food before him or have a novice give it, a loophole, he says, to be used tactfully.33 A monk who sets the plate down in front of his guest has kept the rule and lost nothing of the courtesy.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — giving water or a tooth cleaner to a monastic of another religion.34
    • Dukkaṭa — where the person is not a monastic of another religion but he perceives them as one, or is unsure. Where the person is one, all three perceptions carry the full pācittiya.35

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he does not give, but has it given; if he gives by placing it near the person; if he gives ointments for external use; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.36

    Thanissaro reports the new sub-commentary on the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī taking the ointments to have been probably meant as oils, which, being edible, would otherwise count as fresh food; with the exemption a monk may hand a jar of oil for external use to anyone.37

    Related Offences

    • Pc 40 — putting food not given into one's own mouth; this rule is its corollary and borrows its three modes of giving.
    • Pc 29 — where the naming of the two food-categories is worked through.
    • Pc 34 — where pūva, the cookies of this rule's story, is discussed.
    • Pc 4 — the rule whose perception pattern this one follows.
    • Bhikkhunī Pc 46 — the nuns' rule on giving food, with a householder in place of the naked ascetic; the Concise BMC reads the limit on who may give on a monk's behalf from it.

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, both incidents, in the de-dramatised register; the wanderers' and the Ājīvakas' exchanges and the lay followers' request are verbatim, and the rule is laid down with the ten-reasons formula and no rebuke. Brahmāli's recitation, the Manual and the class transcript (session 027, 7 June 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1, Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were checked; the class is not cited, since the written sources carry each point it makes. Ajahn Brahm's retelling adds details the Vibhaṅga lacks, among them that the Ājīvaka belittled the monks in front of the laity, and these are not carried into the Story. The Kosambī passage Ajahn Brahm cites for excluding other Buddhist traditions concerns a divided Saṅgha and is not used as evidence here. The Commentary and the new sub-commentary on the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī are known here only through Thanissaro and the Concise BMC.
