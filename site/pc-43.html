    Pc 43

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. Venerable Upananda the Sakyan went to the house of a friend and sat down with his wife in their bedroom. The husband approached Upananda, bowed, and sat down. He then said to his wife, "Please give him alms." And she did so.

    Soon afterwards he said, "Please leave, sir, the alms have been given."

    But the woman, knowing that her husband was lustful, said, "Please sit, sir, don't go."

    A second time and a third time he repeated his request, and both times his wife repeated hers.

    He then left the house and complained to the monks, "Venerables, Venerable Upananda is seated with my wife in our bedroom. When I ask him to leave because we're busy, he doesn't want to go."

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised Upananda, "How could Venerable Upananda sit down intruding on a lustful couple?" They told the Buddha, who questioned Upananda, established that it was true, rebuked him, and stated the rule.1​ 2

    Synopsis — Upananda visits a friend, is given alms by the man's wife in their bedroom, and then will not take the hint. The husband asks him three times to go; the wife, who can see what her husband wants, asks him three times to stay; and Upananda sits. The rule that follows is about the monk's position between them, and not about either of theirs.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk sits down intruding on a lustful couple, he commits an offense entailing confession."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu sits down intruding on a lustful couple, he incurs a pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu sit intruding on a family 'with its meal,' it is to be confessed."5

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Sitting down intruding upon a man and a woman when one or both are sexually aroused is an offence of Pācittiya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu, having intruded upon an family having a meal, should take seat, [this is a case] involving expiation."7

    The renderings divide over one word, sabhojane, which can be read as sa + bhojana, "with its meal", or as "with both people", and they divide sharply: Brahmāli has "a lustful couple", Ajahn Brahm "a man and a woman when one or both are sexually aroused", Thanissaro keeps "with its meal" in quotation marks, and Ñāṇatusita has "a family having a meal".8​ 9​ 10​ 11 The difference does not change the rule, because the Vibhaṅga defines the word by the couple's state and not by any meal, and every source applies the rule to a couple in their bedroom. Thanissaro holds that the pun needs no defence, since "eating has been commonly used as a metaphor for sex"; Ajahn Brahm argues it from other compounds of bhoga.12​ 13 Ñāṇatusita alone wonders whether the word first meant a family at its meal, observing that a rule about a bedroom would hardly need to specify sitting down, but he offers it as a possibility about the rule's history, not its application.14 What the rule asks of a monk depends on where he sits, and that is Effort's question.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The bedroom is the one room of a lay household where a monk has no place once he is not wanted, and this rule keeps his restraint, and the household's trust in him, at its door. Upananda was given alms by his friend's wife in the couple's bedroom and then would not go. The husband asked him three times to leave, the wife, seeing what her husband wanted, asked him three times to stay, and Upananda sat on until the husband went to the monks: "When I ask him to leave because we're busy, he doesn't want to go." The Buddha's rebuke was that it "will affect people's confidence".15

    The rule that follows is about the monk's place between them. Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual gives four factors: the monk sits down in the couple's presence in their bedroom, at least one of them is there, at least one of them is lustful, and no one else is present; Thanissaro covers the same ground under object and effort.16​ 17​ 18 His two labels are used here, and the Manual's last factor, that no one else is present, sits under Effort.

    Object

    The object is the couple, and the Vibhaṅga defines them by two negatives: "The woman and the man have not both left, and both are not without lust."19 So the offence survives one of them stepping out, and survives one of them being uninterested. The Manual adds that the Pāli's eva means just one woman and one man, so the rule contemplates exactly two lay people and one monk; Thanissaro's gloss is the same picture, a man and woman in their private quarters "with at least one of them desiring sexual intercourse with the other".20​ 21

    Effort

    The effort is intruding and sitting. The Vibhaṅga glosses intruding as "enters after", the verb of Pc 16, where a monk squeezes into another's sleeping place.22 The sitting is defined by the room. In a large house the offence falls if he sits more than an arm's reach inside the door frame; in a small house, if he sits beyond the ridge beam.23 Thanissaro gives the second as "the back half of the house", and Brahmāli's endnote gives the Commentary reading the ridge beam the same way: going beyond it is "going beyond the middle". Thanissaro thinks a one-room apartment or hotel room would probably count as a separate bedroom.24​ 25

    Perception attaches to the room, not to the couple. If it is a bedroom, the full offence follows whether the monk perceives it as one, is unsure, or does not; if it is not, perceiving it as one or being unsure is wrong conduct.26 The Vibhaṅga says nothing about perception of the couple's state, and Thanissaro carries the same pattern across to it, with a hedge: "apparently" it holds there too. Whether intention is a factor he leaves open, since the Parivāra and commentaries say it is and the Vibhaṅga does not mention it, and so he advises a monk not to sit with a couple unless both make him entirely certain that he is welcome, a policy he thinks wise for anyone, monk or not.27

    Another person lifts the offence. The non-offence clause reads bhikkhu dutiyo hoti. Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation, Thanissaro and the Concise BMC read it as a second monk; the Manual reads it as the monk having someone with him, anyone at all, and Ajahn Brahm reads it the same way.28​ 29​ 30​ 31​ 32 The grammar is against the Manual: in the next rule the same construction gives "a male companion who understands", where the companion is the man himself, and the Manual reads it that way there.33 I follow the Manual all the same, so that a novice, an anagārika or a lay person in the room lifts the offence. The choice is made against the weight of the grammar, though the Manual's other ground, the eva of the definition, by which the rule contemplates exactly two lay people and the monk, still stands for it.34

    Upananda had two people telling him opposite things, and took the answer he preferred.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — where it is not a bedroom but he perceives it as one, or is unsure. Where it is a bedroom, all three perceptions carry the full pācittiya.35

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if, in a large house, he sits down, but not more than one arm's reach inside the door frame; if, in a small house, he sits down, but not beyond the ridge beam; if he has a companion; if both have left; if both are without lust; if it is not a bedroom; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.36

    "If he has a companion" follows the Manual's reading, as the Explanation sets out. It also joins this rule to the one before it. At Pc 42 Upananda's offence was sending his companion away; here the presence of a companion is what would have saved him.

    Related Offences

    • Pc 42 — the rule before, with the same offender, where dismissing the companion is the offence.
    • Pc 44, Pc 45 — sitting in private with a woman, with and without a concealed seat; the same offender and the same family of rules.
    • Pc 16 — the other rule built on "intruding", into another monk's sleeping place.
    • Pc 83 — entering a king's sleeping quarters, the royal counterpart of this rule.
    • Aniyata 1, Aniyata 2 — the anchors of the privacy rules.
    • Pc 4 — the rule whose perception pattern this one follows, here on the room.
