    Pc 65

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Rājagaha in the Bamboo Grove, the squirrel sanctuary. At that time in Rājagaha there was a group of seventeen boys who were friends, with Upāli as their leader.1

    Upāli's parents considered, "How can we make sure that Upāli is able to live happily without exhausting himself after we've passed away? He could become a clerk, but then his fingers will hurt. Or he could become an accountant, but then his chest will hurt. Or he could become a banker, but then his eyes will hurt. But these Sakyan monastics have pleasant habits and a happy life. They eat nice food and sleep in beds sheltered from the wind. If Upāli goes forth with them, he'll be able to live happily without exhausting himself after we've passed away."2

    Upāli overheard this conversation between his parents. He then went to the other boys and said, "Come, let's go forth with the Sakyan monastics."

    "If you go forth, so will we."

    The boys went each to his own parents and said, "Please allow me to go forth into homelessness." Since the parents knew that the boys all had the same desire and good intention, they gave their approval. The boys then went to the monks and asked for the going forth. And the monks gave them the going forth and the full ordination.3

    Soon afterwards they got up early in the morning and cried, "Give us congee, give us a meal, give us fresh food." The monks said, "Wait until it gets light. If any of that becomes available then, you can have it. If not, you'll eat after walking for alms." But they carried on as before. And they defecated and urinated on the furniture.4

    After rising early in the morning, the Buddha heard the sound of those boys. He asked Venerable Ānanda, who told him what was happening. Soon afterwards the Buddha had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned the monks: "Is it true, monks, that the monks give the full ordination to people they know are less than twenty years old?" "It's true, sir." The Buddha rebuked them: "How can those foolish men do this? A person who is less than twenty years old is unable to endure cold and heat; hunger and thirst; horseflies, mosquitoes, wind, and the burning sun; creeping animals and insects; and rude and unwelcome speech. And they are unable to bear up with bodily feelings that are painful, severe, sharp, and destructive of life. But a person who's twenty is able to endure these things. This will affect people's confidence." A monk who gives the full ordination to a person he knows is less than twenty years old commits an offence entailing confession; moreover, that person has not received the full ordination and those monks are blameworthy.5

    Synopsis — Seventeen boys were ordained on a parental calculation about a comfortable career, and cried for their breakfast before dawn. The rule that follows does not only penalise the ordaining; it declares the ordination not to have taken effect.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk gives the full ordination to a person he knows is less than twenty years old, he commits an offense entailing confession. Moreover, that person has not received the full ordination and those monks are blameworthy."6

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu acts as preceptor for anyone he knows is less than twenty years old, he incurs a pācittiya."7

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu knowingly give full Acceptance (ordination) to an individual less than twenty years old, the individual is not accepted and the bhikkhus are blameworthy; and as for him (the preceptor), it is to be confessed."8

    Brahm

    "Ordaining a person knowing that he is under twenty years of age is an offence of Pācittiya."9

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should knowingly have a person who is less than twenty years [old] fully admitted [into the bhikkhu-community], then that person is one who has not been fully admitted and those bhikkhus are blameworthy. Because of that, this [is a case] involving expiation."10

    The renderings part over the rule's second half. After the penalty the Pāli adds so ca puggalo anupasampanno, te ca bhikkhū gārayhā, idaṁ tasmiṁ pācittiyaṁ: that person is not fully ordained, those monks are blameworthy, and for this there is a pācittiya.11 Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga, Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita keep it; the recitation and Ajahn Brahm drop it.12​ 13 The dropped clause is the one with the heaviest consequence in the rule, and it is taken up below, under Perception.

    The renderings also name the actor differently. The Pāli verb is a causative, "should have fully ordained", and names no one; Brahmāli's recitation writes the word-analysis into the rule line with "acts as preceptor", and Thanissaro hands the offence to the preceptor in a closing parenthesis.14​ 15​ 16 All of them are reading the same word-analysis, which gives the full offence to the preceptor alone; that is taken up under Effort.17

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    Before a man is given the full ordination, this rule asks his preceptor to make sure he is old enough for the life he is entering, for the man's sake and for the Saṅgha's steadiness. The origin story shows an ordination made for the wrong reason and failing in the obvious way. Upāli's parents wanted him to live without exhausting himself, and judged the monks' life an easy one; seventeen boys went forth together, were given the full ordination, and before dawn were crying for their breakfast. The Buddha's rebuke gives the rule its reason. A person under twenty cannot yet endure cold and heat, hunger and thirst, insects and the sun, rude speech and painful feelings, and a person of twenty can.18

    Thanissaro gives the offence three factors: object, perception and effort.19 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual hangs its three on the rule's own words: the monk is the preceptor, the twenty years run from the rebirth-linking consciousness, and the offence falls when the ordination is complete.20 I follow Thanissaro's, since the Vibhaṅga's grid runs along his perception factor.

    Object

    The candidate must be less than twenty, and the Vibhaṅga's definition says only that he "has not reached twenty years".21 When the count begins is settled in the Khandhaka. Venerable Kumārakassapa, ordained twenty years after he appeared in the womb, wondered whether he had been ordained at all, and the Buddha answered that a person's birth is when the mind first appears in the mother's womb, and allowed the full ordination to one who is twenty years from that moment.22 Since a conception date cannot be known, Thanissaro reports that the usual practice is to add six months to the years since birth, to allow for a premature birth, and he supports the figure from the Commentary: "a baby born after seven months in the womb may survive, but one born after only six months in the womb won't."23

    Disputed — whether a Saṅgha today should count the twenty years from conception, as the Khandhaka does, or from birth.

    Brahmāli, in class, prefers birth. He treats the conception reckoning as a commentarial standard, bound to the way people thought when the commentaries were written, and argues that a modern Saṅgha should use its own, twenty years from birth.24 Against him, the reckoning is not the Commentary's but the Buddha's, given at Kd 1:75 in answer to exactly this question, and Thanissaro, Ajahn Brahm and the Concise BMC state it without qualification.25​ 26​ 27​ 28 So, it seems, does Brahmāli's own Manual, which counts the twenty years "from the time of the appearance of the rebirth-linking consciousness", citing the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī; whether that is his considered position or a report of the standard he set aside in class, the two do not quite agree.29 For him, his standard is the stricter, and he says so himself: counting from birth, "we won't be going wrong as far as the commentary is concerned anyway."30 I would follow him for that reason, since a man twenty years from birth is over twenty on any reckoning. This settles only which number is safer, not which is right; a man ordained at nineteen and a half from birth is validly ordained on the texts' own terms, which nothing in Brahmāli's argument denies.

    Perception

    The grid has one striking permutation. A monk who ordains a man under twenty while perceiving him as over twenty commits no offence at all, and the non-offence clauses repeat it; doubt is an offence of wrong conduct whatever the man's age, and so is ordaining a man of twenty while perceiving him as under.31​ 32 But the ordination fails regardless, and here the rule divides into its two questions. "That person has not received the full ordination": he is, Thanissaro says, "only a novice", whether or not he or anyone else knows.33​ 34

    Thanissaro gives the Commentary's view of where the defect leads. If such a man later acts as preceptor or teacher for someone else, that ordination stands provided the quorum is complete without him; and as long as he does not know, there is no obstacle for him to heaven or to the transcendent states, but once he knows, he should arrange at once to be ordained properly.35 The practical edge of this is Brahmāli's counsel in class: a man who thinks he may be under age should wait a couple of years.36

    His Appendix I goes further, arguing that in the earliest stage a formal act improperly performed "stands as valid if no-one is aware of the problem or no-one brings attention to it", and that the common habit of doubting the validity of one's own or another's ordination "is misguided."37 That argument answers the long chains of anxiety about lineage that grow up around rules like this one. I do not think it reaches this rule's own defect, though he names ordination as one of the most obvious examples, since here the invalidity is stated in the rule line itself and not inferred by a commentator; but the limit is mine, and his Introduction to the Khandhakas lists the twenty-year requirement among the conditions of a valid act without carving it out.38

    Effort

    The word-analysis grades the preparations. Searching for a group, a teacher, a bowl or a robe with the intention of ordaining, or establishing a monastery zone, is an offence of wrong conduct; so is the motion, and so is each of the first two announcements. "When the last announcement is finished, the preceptor commits an offense entailing confession, while the group and the teacher commit an offense of wrong conduct."39 The full offence is the preceptor's alone. Thanissaro limits the dukkaṭa of the others to those who know the candidate's age.40

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence where the monk perceives the candidate as twenty or more, whatever his real age.41 The Khandhaka's procedure makes that perception an informed one. Among the obstacles a candidate is asked about before the formal act is "Are you twenty years old?", and the answer is announced to the assembly, so that, as Ajahn Brahm observes, the monks should know whether the man they are ordaining is over twenty or not.42​ 43 The question is asked for the candidate's sake as much as the Saṅgha's. A man under twenty who is ordained without anyone's knowing it has lost nothing he cannot recover: when he learns the truth he is ordained again, and until then, as Thanissaro reports the Commentary, nothing stands between him and the goal.44

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — for each preparatory step taken with the intention of ordaining a man one knows to be under twenty: searching for a group, a teacher, a bowl or a robe, or establishing a monastery zone.45
    • Dukkaṭa — after the motion, and after each of the first two announcements.46
    • Dukkaṭa — for the group and the teacher when the last announcement is finished; Thanissaro limits it to those who know the candidate's age.47​ 48
    • Dukkaṭa — ordaining a man under twenty while unsure of his age, and a man of twenty or more while perceiving him as under, or unsure.49

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence "if he gives the full ordination to someone less than twenty years old, but he perceives them as more than twenty; if he gives the full ordination to someone more than twenty years old, and he perceives them as more than twenty"; nor if he is insane, or the first offender.50 The ordination of a man under twenty is invalid in every case.

    Related Offences

    • Kd 1:49 — the same origin story, ending in a Khandhaka prohibition, not a training rule.51
    • Kd 1:75 — Kumārakassapa's question and the ruling that the count runs from conception.52
    • Kd 1:76 — the obstacles put to a candidate, of which the last is his age.53
    • The conclusion of the saṅghādisesa section — the Pātimokkha's other te ca bhikkhū gārayhā: a rehabilitation by fewer than twenty monks does not take effect, and the monks are blameworthy.54​ 55
    • Pc 15, 17, 37, 52, 53, 55, 60, 74, 75, 77 — rules whose origin stories concern the group of seventeen monks, who are very likely these boys grown up, as Ajahn Brahm and Thanissaro both take them to be.56​ 57
    • Pc 63 — reopening a settled legal issue, where the validity of formal acts is treated.

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register, with the parents' deliberation, the boys' exchange, the crying and the rebuke given nearly whole and the interrogation compressed as the source compresses it; the Khandhaka tells the same story in the same words at Kd 1:49. Ajahn Brahm's retelling compresses but does not diverge. The Manual, the recitation, the class transcript (session 029, 19 June 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1 and BMC2, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were all checked; the Concise BMC condenses BMC1 here. The commentaries are known here only as Thanissaro and the Manual report them, and the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī also as Ñāṇatusita does. Ajahn Brahm's Thai practice of nineteen years and six months rests on the Vinaya-mukha, which is not used in this book, and it is not followed. The class is cited for Brahmāli's side of the Disputed passage and for one practice ruling.
