    Pc 68

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time the monk Ariṭṭha, the ex-vulture-hunter, had the following bad and erroneous view: "As I understand the Teaching of the Buddha, the things he calls obstacles are unable to obstruct one who indulges in them."1​ 2

    A number of monks heard that Ariṭṭha had that view. They went to him and asked, "Is it true, Ariṭṭha, that you have such a view?"

    "Yes, indeed. As I understand the Buddha's Teaching, the things he calls obstacles are unable to obstruct one who indulges in them."

    "No, Ariṭṭha, don't misrepresent the Buddha, for it's not good to misrepresent him. The Buddha would never say such a thing. The Buddha has given many discourses about the obstacles being obstructive and how they obstruct one who indulges in them. The Buddha has said that the enjoyment provided by worldly pleasures is small, whereas the suffering and trouble with them are huge, and so their drawbacks are greater. The Buddha has said that worldly pleasures are similar to a skeleton … similar to a piece of meat … similar to a grass torch … similar to a pit of coals … similar to a dream … similar to borrowed goods … similar to fruits on a tree … similar to a knife and chopping block … similar to swords and stakes … similar to a snake's head; the suffering and trouble with them are huge, and so their drawbacks are greater."3

    But even though the monks corrected Ariṭṭha like this, he stubbornly held on to that bad and erroneous view, and continued to insist on it. Since they were unable to make him give up that view, they went to the Buddha and told him what had happened.4

    Soon afterwards the Buddha had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned Ariṭṭha: "Is it true, Ariṭṭha, that you have such a view?"

    "Yes indeed, sir."

    "Foolish man, who do you think I have taught like this? Haven't I given many discourses about the obstacles being obstructive and how they obstruct one who indulges in them? I have said that the enjoyment provided by worldly pleasures is small, whereas the suffering and trouble with them are huge, and so their drawbacks are greater. I have said that worldly pleasures are similar to a skeleton … similar to a piece of meat … similar to a grass torch … similar to a pit of coals … similar to a dream … similar to borrowed goods … similar to fruits on a tree … similar to a knife and chopping block … similar to swords and stakes … similar to a snake's head; the suffering and trouble with them are huge, and so their drawbacks are greater. And yet, foolish man, by misunderstanding you have misrepresented me, hurt yourself, and made much demerit. This will be for your long-lasting harm and suffering. This will affect people's confidence."5

    A monk who says such a thing should be corrected by the monks, and if he continues as before the monks should press him up to three times to make him give up that view. If he then gives it up, all is well. If he does not, he commits an offence entailing confession.6

    Synopsis — A monk maintained that what the Buddha calls an obstruction is no obstruction to one who indulges in it, would not be talked out of it, and affirmed it to the Buddha's face in the assembly. The rule that follows does not penalise the view; it penalises refusing to let go of it after the Saṅgha has formally asked three times.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk says, 'As I understand the Buddha's Teaching, the things he calls obstacles are unable to obstruct one who indulges in them,' then the monks should correct him like this: 'No, venerable, don't misrepresent the Buddha, for it's not good to misrepresent the Buddha. The Buddha would never say such a thing. In many discourses the Buddha has declared the obstacles to be obstructive and how they obstruct one who indulges in them.' If that monk continues as before, the monks should press him up to three times to make him give up that view. If he then gives it up, all is well. If he does not, he commits an offense entailing confession."7

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu says that things declared as obstructive by the Buddha are not actually obstructive, he should be admonished up to three times to make him give up that view. If he does not, he incurs a pācittiya."8

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu say the following: 'As I understand the Dhamma taught by the Blessed One, those acts the Blessed One says are obstructive, when engaged in are not genuine obstructions,' the bhikkhus are to admonish him thus: 'Do not say that, venerable sir. Do not slander the Blessed One, for it is not good to slander the Blessed One. The Blessed One would not say anything like that. In many ways, friend, the Blessed One has described obstructive acts, and when engaged in they are genuine obstructions. [The Sri Lankan and Burmese recensions read: In many ways, friend, the Blessed One has described obstructive acts as obstructive, and when engaged in they are genuine obstructions.]' And should that bhikkhu, thus admonished by the bhikkhus, persist as before, the bhikkhus are to rebuke him up to three times for the sake of relinquishing that. If while being rebuked up to three times he relinquishes that, that is good. If he does not relinquish (that), it is to be confessed."9

    Brahm

    "Persistently refusing to give up a seriously wrong view is an offence of Pācittiya."10

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should say so, 'As I understand the Teaching taught by the Fortunate One, these obstructive acts which are spoken of by the Fortunate One: they are not enough to be an obstruction for the one who is being engaged in [them],' [then] that bhikkhu is to be spoken to thus by the bhikkhus … and [if] that bhikkhu being spoken to thus by the bhikkhus should persist in the same way [as before], [then] that bhikkhu is to be argued with up to three times by the bhikkhus for the relinquishing of that [view], [and if that bhikkhu,] being argued with up to three times, should relinquish that [view], then this is good, [but] if he should not relinquish [it]: [this is a case] involving expiation."11

    Two of the five renderings are summaries: Brahmāli's recitation, and Ajahn Brahm's line, which keeps only "a seriously wrong view".12​ 13 The summaries are useful, but Ajahn Brahm's makes the offence turn on a view, where the Pāli makes it turn on saying — yo pana bhikkhu evaṁ vadeyya, "if a monk should say this" — and the word-analysis gives the duty to correct him to the monks "who see it or hear about it".14​ 15 Thanissaro draws the consequence: "Simply holding such a view is not enough to bring a bhikkhu under the purview of this rule."16

    The other difference that matters is the Saṅgha's verb. Samanubhāsati is "press" in Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga and Glossary, "admonish" in his recitation, "rebuke" in Thanissaro's and "argue with" in Ñāṇatusita's.17​ 18​ 19​ 20 The rule distinguishes two acts that "admonish" runs together: the informal vadati, which Brahmāli renders "correct", and the formal samanubhāsana, an act of the Saṅgha with a motion and three announcements.21 The offence falls only at the second, and the ladder is taken up below, under Effort.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule protects a monk from a view that would undo his own practice, and the Teaching from being misrepresented in the Buddha's name. Ariṭṭha held that the things the Buddha calls obstructions do not obstruct one who indulges in them, his fellow monks answered him with the Buddha's many similes for sensual pleasure, and he affirmed the view to the Buddha's face in the assembly.22 The Buddha's rebuke names who is harmed: "by misunderstanding you have misrepresented me, hurt yourself, and made much demerit." Brahmāli's introduction takes this rule and the two after it together, as concerning the view that sexual intercourse is no obstacle on the path, and observes that "such views are heavily censured in the Vinaya Piṭaka."23 Yet the rule does not penalise the view. It penalises refusing to let it go after the Saṅgha has formally asked three times, and every step before that is an invitation to give it up.

    Thanissaro's back-matter Addendum gives the offence as a single factor of effort in two limbs — insisting that an obstruction is not one, even when rebuked three times in a properly performed act of the Community — and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual refers the whole procedure to Sg 10.24​ 25 I take the view as the object, the ladder as the effort, and the legitimacy of the act as what the perception grid is about.

    Object

    The object is a view: that what the Buddha calls obstructions do not obstruct. The Vibhaṅga does not define "obstruction", though the origin story shows that it covers the sexual act at the least.26 Thanissaro gives the Commentary's broader definition: whatever obstructs heaven or emancipation, in five categories, the last of which is, for a bhikkhu, the intentional transgression of the Buddha's ordinances — an obstruction "only as long as one has not undergone the penalty called for in the relevant rule".27

    Disputed — whether the view this rule reaches is only the view about sex, or any denial that a deliberate transgression obstructs.

    Thanissaro reads it widely, following the Commentary's fifth category: his summary gives the view as "that there is nothing wrong in intentionally transgressing the Buddha's ordinances".28 Brahmāli reads the rule as about sex: the Manual's one factor under "obstructions" is sexual intercourse, and his introduction reads Pc 68–70 the same way.29​ 30 He does not confine "obstruction" to sex: in class he allowed that lying might be included; what he rejected was the extension to every rule, as "overstating the case a lot", since some Vinaya rules have nothing to do with "what might obstruct you in the mental sense."31 His objection has force, for a monk who eats after noon has not closed the path; and, on my reading, Thanissaro's report already carries one answer to it: a transgression obstructs only until its penalty is undergone, so what the view denies is the need to make amends, rather than that a small fault is a lasting bar. The practical distance between them is small, since a monk who denies that the Vinaya's rules matter is caught by Pc 72 in any case; what the wider reading adds is that a Saṅgha may use this rule's procedure against a view about something other than sex. The Vibhaṅga does not define the word, and each reading is that of a teacher who knows the texts well; a Saṅgha whose monks correct any such assertion when they hear it, and that keeps the formal pressing for views about sex, acts on both.

    Effort

    The Vibhaṅga sets out a ladder. First, any monk who sees or hears of the assertion should correct the offender, three times; if he gives up the view, all is well, and if not, it is an offence of wrong conduct. The monks who hear and say nothing commit an offence of wrong conduct too, since otherwise, as Thanissaro says, the offender may go on asserting it without incurring any penalty.32​ 33 How far that duty reaches Thanissaro answers from the Commentary at Sg 10, where the same sentence stands.34 Second, he is corrected three times in the midst of the Saṅgha, "even if he has to be pulled into the Sangha", and refusal is again an offence of wrong conduct.35 Third, the formal pressing: a motion and three announcements, with an offence of wrong conduct after the motion and after each of the first two announcements, and the pācittiya when the last is finished.36 The Manual fixes both ends to the act: he must give the view up "before the end of the saṅghakamma", and the offence falls at its end.37

    At every rung the invitation stands: "If he gives up that view, all is well." Unlike the parallel saṅghādisesa rules, the Vibhaṅga here does not say that the lesser offences are cancelled when the full one falls, and Thanissaro advises that a monk who goes the whole way should make amends for all of them.38​ 39

    The pācittiya is not the end of the process. A monk who still asserts the view is subject to an act of suspension, which cuts him off from communion with other monks until he gives it up, and Thanissaro advises a Community preparing to press a stubborn monk to be ready to suspend him at once if the pressing fails.40

    Perception

    The grid follows the formal act, not the monk. Where the act is legitimate, the offence is a pācittiya whether the monk takes it to be legitimate, doubts it, or takes it to be illegitimate; where it is not legitimate, it is an offence of wrong conduct however he takes it.41 Thanissaro names the pattern as Sg 10's, not Pc 4's, and gives the reason for the lesser offence: it is "probably for one's unwillingness to relinquish one's view after being reprimanded."42 On the Mahāsaṅgīti and Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana reading, an invalid act does not make the refusal harmless; it only makes the full penalty unavailable.43

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The first non-offence clause restates the rule's shape: a monk never formally pressed has committed no pācittiya, however long he holds the view.

    Ajahn Brahm adds a caution Thanissaro shares. A seriously wrong view is one "essentially opposed to the Dhamma", and the procedures of this rule, above all suspension, are not to be used "for minor differences of opinion on unimportant matters."44 Thanissaro gives the reason from the other side: suspension touches the very grounds of dispute, may prolong the strife that led to it and even lead to schism, and so "should not be performed lightly."45 In the story's own ending the Saṅgha ejected Ariṭṭha, he disrobed, and, as the received text has it, the Buddha had the ejection lifted.46 The procedure was never meant to win an argument: at every rung it asks the same thing, that a monk let go of a view, and it stops the moment he does.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — for the monk who does not give up the view after being corrected informally three times, and again after being corrected three times in the midst of the Saṅgha.47
    • Dukkaṭa — for every monk who hears of the assertion and says nothing.48
    • Dukkaṭa — after the motion, and after each of the first two announcements; the Vibhaṅga does not say these are annulled by the full offence.49​ 50
    • Dukkaṭa — on the Mahāsaṅgīti text, where the formal act is not legitimate, however the monk perceives it.51​ 52

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence "if he has not been pressed; if he gives it up; if he is insane; if he is the first offender" — the last supplied in Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga and printed in the PTS edition, but absent from the Mahāsaṅgīti and Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana texts.53​ 54​ 55

    Related Offences

    • Sg 10, 11, 12, 13 — the four saṅghādisesa rules with the same pressing procedure; the Manual refers this rule's procedure to Sg 10. There the preliminary offences are dukkaṭas, and thullaccayas at the first two announcements, and are annulled by the full offence.56​ 57
    • Pc 69, 70 — living with a monk ejected for this view, and supporting a novice expelled for it: the two rules that take this one's view as their object.
    • Pj 1 — sexual intercourse: the act the origin story shows Ariṭṭha's view to cover.58
    • Pc 72 — disparaging the training rules, which catches a monk who belittles the Vinaya without claiming that transgression is harmless.
    • Kd 11 — the ejection of Ariṭṭha, and, as the received text has it, its lifting after he disrobed.59

    Related Suttas

    • MN 22 — the Alagaddūpama, where the same Ariṭṭha holds the same view and the Buddha gives the simile of the snake grasped the wrong way.60
    • MN 38 — Sāti, "pressed and questioned and cross-questioned" by his fellow monks, "still obstinately adhered to that pernicious view", and the Buddha rebukes him in the words of this rule's origin story, "misrepresented us by your wrong grasp and injured yourself and stored up much demerit": the pressing by his fellows is the step before the Saṅgha's formal pressing, which this rule penalises a monk for resisting.61

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register. The ten similes are given twice because the Vibhaṅga gives them twice, once in the monks' correction and once in the Buddha's rebuke, each with the source's own ellipses; and since the final ruling is given whole in the Translation, the Story closes on its substance. Ajahn Brahm's retelling adds the simile of the snake, which belongs to the Majjhima's version of the story and is not carried there; MN 22 is given under Related Suttas instead, and it and MN 38 are quoted from Bodhi's translation. The Manual, the recitation, the class transcript (session 030, 16 July 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1 and BMC2 with his Addendum, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were all checked; the Concise BMC condenses BMC1 here. The commentaries are known here only as Thanissaro reports them. Ajahn Brahm's ordering of the two formal acts rests on the Vinaya-mukha, which is not used in this book, and is not followed. The class is cited for Brahmāli's side of the Disputed passage.
