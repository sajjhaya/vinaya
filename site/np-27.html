    NP 27

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time a man who was going away said to his wife, "Please weigh some thread, take it to the weavers, get them to weave robe-cloth, and put the robe-cloth aside. When I return, I'll give it to Venerable Upananda."

    An alms-collecting monk heard that man speaking those words. He then went to Upananda the Sakyan and said, "Upananda, you have much merit. In such-and-such a place I heard a man, as he was going away, tell his wife to get robe-cloth woven so that he could give it to you when he returned."

    "He's my supporter." And the weaver was Upananda's supporter too.

    Upananda then went to that weaver and said, "This robe-cloth that you're weaving for me, make it long and wide. And make it closely woven, well-woven, well-stretched, well-scraped, and well-combed."

    "Venerable, they've already weighed the thread and given it to me, telling me to weave the robe-cloth with that. I won't be able to make it long, wide, or closely woven. But I'm able to make it well-woven, well-stretched, well-scraped, and well-combed."

    "Just make it long, wide, and closely woven. There'll be enough thread."

    Then, when all the thread had been used up, that weaver went to that woman and said, "Ma'am, I need more thread."

    "But didn't I tell you to weave the robe-cloth with that thread?"

    "You did. But Venerable Upananda told me to make it long, wide, and closely woven. And he said there would be enough thread." That woman then gave him as much thread again as she had done the first time.

    When Upananda heard that her husband had returned from his travels, he went to his house and sat down on the prepared seat. That man approached him, bowed, and sat down. He then said to his wife, "Has the robe-cloth been woven?"

    "Yes, it has."

    "Please bring it. I'll give it to Venerable Upananda."

    She then got the robe-cloth, gave it to her husband, and told him what had happened. After giving the robe-cloth to Upananda, he complained and criticised him: "These Sakyan monastics have great desires; they're not content. It's no easy matter to give them robe-cloth. How could Venerable Upananda go to the weavers and say what kind of robe-cloth he wanted without first being invited by me?"

    The monks heard the complaints of that man, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised Upananda: "How could Venerable Upananda go to a householder's weavers and say what kind of robe-cloth he wants without first being invited?"

    After rebuking him in many ways, they told the Buddha. Soon afterwards he had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned Upananda: "Is it true, Upananda, that you did this?"

    "It's true, sir."

    "Is he a relative of yours?"

    "No, sir."

    "Foolish man, people who are unrelated don't know what's appropriate and inappropriate, what's good and bad, in dealing with each other. And still you did this. This will affect people's confidence." And he laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — Upananda hears that a man is having robe-cloth woven for him and, uninvited, tells the weaver to make it long, wide and closely woven, so that the man's wife has to give the thread a second time; the man complains, and the Buddha makes it an offence entailing relinquishment for a monk, uninvited, to go to an unrelated householder's weavers and specify the robe-cloth he wants.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a male or female householder is having robe-cloth woven by weavers for an unrelated monk and, without first being invited, that monk goes to those weavers and specifies the kind of robe-cloth he wants, saying, 'This robe-cloth that you are weaving for me, make it long and wide; make it closely woven, well-woven, well-stretched, well-scraped, and well-combed, and perhaps I will even give you a small gift,' then, in saying that and afterwards giving them a small gift, even a bit of almsfood, he commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "It may be that a householder is having robe-cloth woven for an unrelated bhikkhu. If that bhikkhu, without being invited, asks the weavers for better or larger robe-cloth, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "In case a man or woman householder unrelated (to the bhikkhu) has robe-cloth woven by weavers for the sake of a bhikkhu, and if the bhikkhu, not previously invited (by the householder), having approached the weavers, should make stipulations with regard to the cloth, saying, 'This cloth, friends, is being woven for my sake. Make it long, make it broad, make it tightly woven, well woven, well spread, well scraped, well smoothed, and perhaps I may reward you with a little something'; and should the bhikkhu, having said that, reward them with a little something, even as much as almsfood, it (the cloth) is to be forfeited and confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "When a monk hears that a layperson, who is not a relation, is having thread woven into robe-material to offer to him and, without being invited to do so, he goes and tells the weaver the sort of cloth he is to weave, then on receiving that robe-material he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "Now, if an unrelated male householder or female householder should have a robe-cloth woven for a bhikkhu by cloth-weavers, and then if that bhikkhu, uninvited beforehand, having approached the cloth-weavers, should make a suggestion about the robe-cloth [saying]: 'Friends, this robe-cloth which is being woven for me: make [it] long, wide, thick, well woven, well diffused, well scraped, and well plucked! Certainly we will also [then] present a little something to the sirs,' and if that bhikkhu, having said so, should present a little something, even just a little alms-food, [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture."6

    The full renderings agree. The recitation line is a compression: it drops the approach to the weavers and the promised gift, and condenses the seven ways of improving the cloth to "better or larger".7 Ñāṇatusita observes that in speaking to the weavers the monk refers to them as āyasmantānaṃ, "sirs", an over-polite form for laypeople, "Perhaps it was used in order to flatter".8 Effort takes up whether the gift matters, and whether all seven improvements count alike.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    What a donor has chosen to give is enough: the rule guards a monk's contentment with it, and the trust between monks and the lay people who supply them. The thread had been weighed out at the man's word; Upananda's word to the weaver doubled it at the household's cost and without its knowledge, and the man's complaint names the fault: the monastics "have great desires; they're not content."9 The Buddha's rebuke gives the reason a stranger's gift needs this care, that "people who are unrelated don't know what's appropriate and inappropriate, what's good and bad, in dealing with each other", and names the cost: "This will affect people's confidence."10

    This rule is the sibling of NP 8, where the stipulation goes to a donor about cloth to be bought; here it goes to the weavers, about cloth still on the loom, and Thanissaro reads the new rule as owed to that change in object and effort.11 Unrelated, householder, without first being invited, perception regarding the donor, the exemption for another's benefit and the exemption for asking for something cheaper are as at NP 8.12​ 13​ 14 Thanissaro gives the offence three factors, object, effort and result.15

    Object

    Robe-cloth of any of the six kinds, not smaller than what can be assigned to another, being woven for this monk at the arrangement of an unrelated householder who has not invited him.16​ 17 Under NP 8 the object is a finished robe; here it is the cloth itself, sewn or not.18 Invitation is defined for this rule as the donor's having asked him beforehand what kind of robe-cloth he needs and should have woven; a monk asked in that way may go to the weavers and say what he wants.19

    Effort

    The monk goes to the weavers and, by what he says, gets them to improve the cloth.20 The Vibhaṅga sets the lesser offence at their compliance: when the weaver makes the cloth longer, wider or closer "because of the monk's statement", the effort is wrong conduct.21 A request the weavers ignore leaves only the cloth the donor meant, and, as at NP 8, Ajahn Brahm, who puts a dukkaṭa on the telling itself, says there is then no further offence.22

    The promised gift is not required. The rule's own words have the monk promise a little something and give it, but the Vibhaṅga completes the effort without it and grants no exemption for withholding it, and in Thanissaro's and Ajahn Brahm's report the Commentary reads the quoted speech as an example of how a monk might move the weavers to add more thread; in the origin story, as Ajahn Brahm notes, Upananda promised nothing.23​ 24 What a gift would be is defined widely, down to a tooth cleaner, a piece of string, or a teaching, and Thanissaro draws from it that there is no ceiling either: a monk who offers to pay the weavers in full for the extra work does not escape, and full payment would insult the donors besides.25​ 26

    Any of the seven improvements fulfils the effort, not only those that need more thread. The Vibhaṅga's clause on the effort names three of the seven, length, width and closeness, and the Story shows why they stand apart: the weaver could make the cloth well woven, stretched, scraped and combed with the thread he had, and it was the length, width and closeness that took the second weighing.27​ 28 In Thanissaro's report the Sub-commentary notes that only those three involve added thread, and on his account its implied conclusion is that any of the seven fulfils the factor whether or not thread is added.29 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual reaches the same breadth from the clause that excuses asking for something cheaper: any request that makes the cloth more expensive falls under the rule, and it need not even be better than the weavers would have made, since he may not know what they were making.30​ 31 What the rule protects is the donor's arrangement, not only his thread.

    Result

    The monk obtains the cloth, and the full offence falls on receipt; the cloth is relinquished and given back by NP 1's procedure.32​ 33

    The one direction a monk may always steer such a gift is down: there is no offence "if someone wants to have expensive robe-cloth woven, but he has them weave inexpensive robe-cloth instead."34 Brahmāli made this the rule's use today. The spirit, he said in class, is contentment, and where lay people want to give something very expensive it is fitting to tell them it is too much and steer them to something cheaper, for "Otherwise you tend to spoil the sangha."35

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — when the weavers improve the cloth in line with the monk's statement, before he receives it.36
    • Dukkaṭa — specifying to the weavers of a related householder while perceiving the householder as unrelated, or unsure.37
    • Dukkaṭa (Ajahn Brahm) — the uninvited telling itself.38

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if it is from relatives; if it is from those who have given an invitation; if it is for the benefit of someone else; if it is by means of his own property; if someone wants to have expensive robe-cloth woven, but he has them weave inexpensive robe-cloth instead; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.39 By means of his own property covers only the case where the monk himself hired the weavers.40

    Related Offences

    • NP 8 — the sibling rule: stipulations to the donor about cloth to be bought; this rule carries the same interference to the weavers and the cloth.
    • NP 26 — the lesser weaver rule: there the monk begs the thread and commissions the weaving; this rule states the timing of the dukkaṭa that NP 26 borrows.
    • NP 6 — the anchor for asking non-relatives and for asking on another's behalf.
    • Pc 47 — the terms and duration of invitations, on which this rule's exemption for inviters depends.

    Related Suttas

    • AN 8.30 — "This Dhamma is for one with few desires, not for one with strong desires", a bhikkhu being "content with any kind of robes, almsfood, lodgings, and medicines and provisions for the sick" (see NP 1); the thread had been weighed out at the man's word, and a monk with few desires leaves the measure of a robe to the household that gives it.41
