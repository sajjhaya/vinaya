    NP 30

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time an association had prepared a meal together with robe-cloth for the Saṅgha, intending to offer the robe-cloth after giving the meal.

    But the monks from the group of six went to that association and said, "Please give this robe-cloth to us."

    "Venerables, we can't do that. We've prepared our annual alms-offering together with robe-cloth for the Sangha."

    "The Sangha has many donors and supporters. But since we're staying here, we look to you for support. If you don't give to us, who will? So give us the robe-cloth."

    Being pressured by the monks from the group of six, that association gave the prepared robe-cloth to them and served the food to the Saṅgha. The monks who knew that a meal together with robe-cloth had been prepared for the Saṅgha, but who did not know that the robe-cloth had been given to the monks from the group of six, said, "Please dedicate the robe-cloth."

    "There isn't any. The monks from the group of six have diverted to themselves the robe-cloth we had prepared."

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised those monks: "How could the monks from the group of six divert to themselves things they knew were intended for the Sangha?"

    After rebuking those monks in many ways, they told the Buddha. Soon afterwards he had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned the monks: "Is it true, monks, that you did this?"

    "It's true, sir."

    The Buddha rebuked them — "Foolish men, how could you do this? This will affect people's confidence" — and laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — An association prepares its annual meal and robe-cloth for the Saṅgha, and the group of six press it into giving them the cloth instead; the Buddha makes it an offence entailing relinquishment for a monk to divert to himself material support he knows was intended for the Saṅgha.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk diverts to himself material support that he knows was intended for the Sangha, he commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu diverts to himself an offering he knows was intended for the Saṅgha, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu knowingly divert to himself gains that had been allocated for a Community, they are to be forfeited and confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "When a donor intends to offer a gift to the Saṅgha, and a monk knows this, then should that monk cause the gift to be offered to him instead, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should knowingly allocate for himself a gain which is belonging to the community [and] which has been allocated to it, [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture."6

    The renderings agree. The rule is built on one verb used twice: the gain has been pariṇata, turned toward the Saṅgha, and the monk pariṇāmeyya, turns it toward himself; Ñāṇatusita keeps the repetition ("allocate for himself a gain … which has been allocated"), where Brahmāli and Thanissaro render the second turning as "diverts".7 Ajahn Brahm's rendering states the effort as the factor in Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual does: causing the gift to be offered to him instead.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule keeps a monk from standing between a donor's gift and the Saṅgha it was meant for; it does so for his own sake and for the confidence of donors whose intention would be set aside. The group of six did not take the cloth; they talked the association out of its own plan, with "The Sangha has many donors and supporters … If you don't give to us, who will?", and the Saṅgha got its meal and never saw its cloth.8 Thanissaro sets the rule beside the Buddha's teaching to Vaccha that one who prevents another from giving alms "creates an obstruction and stumbling block for three people": to the donor's merit, to the recipients' gain, and to himself, whom "already he has maimed and injured".9​ 10 This rule and Pc 82 are the two guards against that obstruction, here when the monk turns the gift to himself, there when he turns it to another.11

    Thanissaro gives the offence four factors: object, perception, effort and result; the Manual defines three, the diverting, the offering and the receipt, and leaves the knowing to the rule's own word. I take Thanissaro's, since in perception this rule differs from every other in its class.12​ 13

    Object

    Material support intended for the Saṅgha: the four requisites, down to a tooth cleaner or a piece of string.14 It is intended when the donors have said they will give or prepare it.15 The donor may be anyone, a fellow monk or one's own mother, and in Thanissaro's report the Commentary holds that the Saṅgha's claim stands even against a sick monk's need.16 Ajahn Brahm's example: a monk who hears that a senior monk means to give his old bowl to the Saṅgha, and persuades him to give it to himself instead, commits the full offence.17

    Perception

    The monk knows the gift is intended for the Saṅgha, by himself, from others, or from the donor.18 This is the only rule in the class where perception is a factor of the full offence.19 The editions differ on the monk who does not perceive the gift as intended: the PTS edition gives the full offence, the Burmese and Sri Lankan a dukkaṭa, the Thai no offence, and Thanissaro takes the Thai reading as the most consistent with the rule's "knowingly" and the parallel at Pc 82 in every edition. Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga reads the same: "If it was intended for the Sangha, but he does not perceive it as such, and he diverts it to himself, there is no offense".20​ 21 A monk who is unsure, and diverts it anyway, commits wrong conduct.22

    Effort

    The monk does whatever causes the donor to give the gift to him instead of to the Saṅgha, the Manual says; Ajahn Brahm includes every method of persuasion, down to "Please give it to me", and Thanissaro notes that the texts make no allowance here for kappiya-vohāra, the proper expressions by which a monk elsewhere makes a need known without asking.23​ 24​ 25 The effort itself is wrong conduct, before anything is received.26

    Disputed — whether a hint, dropped by a monk who knows the gift is meant for the Saṅgha, fulfils the effort.

    Ajahn Brahm holds that it does not: a monk who only hints, or tells the donor his needs, incurs no offence even if the donor then gives to him, and had the group of six only said that they needed robe-material, there would have been none.27 Brahmāli held in class that "even any kind of hinting is included", his example being the monk who says the Saṅgha does not really need the gift but he needs a robe: "You're causing the donor to change their intention."28 The Manual's "whatever he does", and Thanissaro's note that the texts make no allowance here for proper expressions, which are hints by design, sit more naturally with Brahmāli's reading, though neither answers Ajahn Brahm.29​ 30 The texts do not settle it; a monk who knows a gift is meant for the Saṅgha and says nothing to turn the gift his way is clear on both readings.

    Result

    The monk obtains the gift, and the offence falls when he receives it.31 It is relinquished by NP 1's procedure and given back to him; Ajahn Brahm suggests, though the Vinaya does not say so, that he should then give it to the Saṅgha for which it was meant.32​ 33

    Disputed — what follows for the monk whose attempt fails.

    In Thanissaro's report the Commentary holds that a monk who tried to divert a gift that then reached the Saṅgha anyway should take no share in it, should return a share he received, and, if he passes it to lay people instead, is to be judged under Pj 2.34 Thanissaro finds this unnecessarily harsh: the monk who succeeds forfeits the gift, receives it back and may use it, so the Commentary's line weighs more heavily on the one who failed, and he prefers the Vibhaṅga's own measure, the dukkaṭa for the effort and nothing more.35 The Vibhaṅga is silent on a share. Thanissaro's reading keeps the penalties in proportion, and I find it the better, but proportion is an argument, not a text; a monk who takes no share of a gift he tried to divert keeps the Commentary's line as well.

    Special Cases

    Every other turning of a gift is wrong conduct, without the preliminary offence for the effort: from one Saṅgha to another or to a shrine, from a shrine to another shrine, a Saṅgha or a person, from one person to another, a Saṅgha or a shrine; turning the Saṅgha's gift to another person is Pc 82's.36​ 37 The Commentary reaches even to telling a donor to feed one dog rather than another, and Thanissaro accepts the point: a monk has no business interfering with gifts freely given to any being.38 The rule covers gifts intended; a gift already given is another matter. Once a gift has been received on the Saṅgha's behalf, not necessarily by a monk and not necessarily into the hand, it is the Saṅgha's, and turning it to oneself is a question for Pj 2.39

    The non-offence clause teaches how to answer a donor. Asked where to give, a monk may name the four things the clause allows: where the gift will be useful, where it goes toward repairs, where it will last, or where the donor feels inspired.40

    Disputed — whether a monk may answer a donor with more than the clause's four answers.

    Thanissaro would not, even when asked. He grounds the clause in the Buddha's own reply to King Pasenadi, "Wherever the mind feels inspired": giving is the donor's freedom, so that a monk may say where a gift bears great fruit, as the Buddha did, but may be no more specific than that reply or the clause, and "When not asked, he has no business at all telling people where they should give their gifts".41 Ajahn Brahm allows other answers of the same kind, such as that a monastery is building a shrine, but not one that amounts to a command.42 To donors unsure where to give, Brahmāli commended the Saṅgha of the four quarters, the whole monastic community present and to come, as the larger and more wholesome intention.43 The texts do not settle how far beyond the four answers a monk may go, but the four themselves are safe on every reading.

    Diversion is not always scheming. Brahmāli warned that it can happen quite unintentionally, a gift meant for the Saṅgha received a shade too personally, with nothing malicious in it, "unless you have mindfulness with you".44 The group of six pressed the donors; a careless monk need only reach out his hand.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — the effort to divert to oneself a gift one knows is intended for the Saṅgha, before it is received.45
    • Dukkaṭa — diverting to oneself a gift intended for the Saṅgha while unsure of it; and a gift not intended for the Saṅgha, perceived as intended or doubted.46
    • Dukkaṭa — on success, with no preliminary offence: from one Saṅgha to another Saṅgha or a shrine; from a shrine to another shrine, a Saṅgha or an individual; from an individual to another individual, a Saṅgha or a shrine.47​ 48
    • Pācittiya — diverting a gift intended for the Saṅgha to another individual (Pc 82).49

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if, being asked, "Where may we give?", he says, "Give where your gift will be useful", "Give where it goes toward repairs", "Give where it will last for a long time", or "Give where you feel inspired"; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.50 A monk who perceives a gift as intended for no one commits no offence under this rule, but, as Thanissaro notes, that does not give "carte blanche": any request he makes must still clear the rules on asking.51

    Related Offences

    • Pc 82 — the sibling rule: diverting the Saṅgha's gift to another individual, a pācittiya; its origin story is the same association, the group of six asking for one another.
    • Pj 2 — once a gift has been received on the Saṅgha's behalf, taking it is a question of theft, not diversion.
    • NP 6, NP 7, NP 22, NP 26, Pc 39 — the rules on asking, which a monk who perceives a gift as unallocated must still clear.

    Related Suttas

    • MN 142 — "I say that in no way is a gift to a person individually ever more fruitful than an offering made to the Sangha"; a monk who diverts the Saṅgha's gift to himself turns it into the lesser gift.52
    • AN 3.57 — one who prevents another from giving "creates an obstruction and stumbling block for three people"; the obstruction Thanissaro sets this rule against, which falls on the monk who causes it as well as on the donor and the Saṅgha.53
    • SN 3.24 — King Pasenadi's "Venerable sir, where should a gift be given?" and the answer, "Wherever one's mind has confidence, great king"; the reply Thanissaro sets beside the non-offence clause, leaving where a gift goes to the donor.54
    • SN 17.5 — the monk whose "mind is overcome and obsessed by gain, honour, and praise" (see Pj 4); the pull of gain that moved the group of six to talk an association out of its gift.55
