    Pc 19

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Kosambī in Ghosita's Monastery. At that time a government official who was Venerable Channa's supporter was making him a dwelling. When the dwelling was finished, Channa had it roofed and plastered over and over. Being overloaded, the dwelling collapsed. Then, while collecting grass and sticks, Channa spoiled the barley field belonging to a certain brahmin. That brahmin complained and criticised him: "How can the venerables spoil my barley field?"

    The monks heard the complaints of that brahmin, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised Channa: "How could Venerable Channa have a finished dwelling roofed and plastered over and over until it collapsed from overloading?" After rebuking him in many ways, they told the Buddha. He had the Saṅgha gathered, questioned Channa, confirmed that it was so, rebuked him, and laid down the training rule.1

    Synopsis — Ven. Channa has his sponsor's newly finished dwelling roofed and plastered again and again until the weight brings it down, and then spoils a brahmin's barley field while gathering grass and sticks; the Buddha sets a limit of three layers on the covering of a sponsored dwelling.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "When a monk is building a large dwelling, then standing where there are no cultivated plants, he may apply two or three layers of roofing material, taking it as far as the doorcase and using it for fixing the door and for treating the window openings. If he applies more than that, even if he stands where there are no cultivated plants, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu who is building a large dwelling applies more than three layers of building material, he incurs a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "When a bhikkhu is having a large dwelling built, he may supervise two or three layers of facing to plaster the area around the window frame and reinforce the area around the door frame the width of the door opening, while standing where there are no crops to speak of. Should he supervise more than that, even if standing where there are no crops to speak of, it is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "When a monk is building or repairing his dwelling, or having someone do this for him, he may replaster the walls only around the door and windows, and he may not cover the dwelling with more than three layers of roofing material, exceeding this is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "By a bhikkhu who is having a large dwelling built, a layer of two or three coverings can be ordered [to be applied onto the dwelling], by [a bhikkhu] standing on [a place which has] few crops, up to the frame of the door for [the purpose of] fixing the bolt, [and] for plastering the window. If he should order more than that, even [when] standing on [a place which has] few crops, [this is a case] involving expiation."6

    The renderings differ because the rule itself is unclear. Ñāṇatusita calls its meaning obscure and the wording possibly corrupted, and quotes Pachow, for whom the versions are so various "that there seems hardly to be anything in common in all the texts".7 Two differences are worth naming. The verb is "apply" in Brahmāli, "supervise" in Thanissaro and "can be ordered" in Ñāṇatusita, who rests on the Vibhaṅga's own paraphrase, "having ordered", since the monk is having the dwelling built.8 The difference is idle, for the Vibhaṅga counts the work whether he does it or has it done. And the clause about standing where there are no crops is kept by Thanissaro and the full translation and dropped from the recitation. The larger difference, what the three layers are laid on, is not a matter of words and is taken up under Effort.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule keeps a monk from building beyond his need at his supporters' cost, for their confidence and his own contentment. Channa's supporter built him a dwelling, and Channa had it roofed and plastered again and again until it fell down under the weight, then spoiled a brahmin's barley field gathering grass and sticks.9 The Story suggests a rule about safety, but Thanissaro reads its aim from the non-offence clauses, which exempt a dwelling built for someone else and one built with the monk's own resources: the rule is aimed at "preventing bhikkhus from abusing the generosity of the person sponsoring the building work."10

    Thanissaro gives the offence two factors: object, a large dwelling with a sponsor, intended for oneself; and effort, having more than three layers applied, directing the work or doing it oneself.11

    Object

    "Is building" is defined as at Sg 6, building it oneself or having it built, and "a large dwelling" as at Sg 7, "one with an owner", that is, a sponsor; Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual makes the referral in one line, and Thanissaro calls this rule an extension of Sg 7.12​ 13​ 14 A dwelling is something plastered, inside or out, so a shelter, a cave, a grass hut, or anything that is not a dwelling falls outside the rule.15 Thanissaro reports the Sub-commentary exempting small dwellings built to Sg 6's measure; but the Vibhaṅga defines a large dwelling by its owner, not its size, and the Manual, referring the term to Sg 7, gives it no lower limit.16​ 17

    Two exemptions carry away most of the rule's modern reach. There is no offence if the dwelling is for someone else's benefit, and none if it is built with the monk's own property.18 Ajahn Brahm puts the second with some relish: a monk who overbuilds with his own resources until the dwelling collapses commits no offence, "only masonry does!" The first, he notes, covers the usual case, a dwelling built for the Saṅgha in general.19 It is a monk building for himself, on a sponsor's gift, whom the rule binds.

    Effort

    The effort is to apply more than three layers, or to have them applied, to a new dwelling or an old one under repair.20 The Vibhaṅga counts by the piece: for bricks, an offence for every brick; for plaster, every lump; for grass, every handful; for leaves, every leaf.21 Perception does not mitigate, as in the pattern Thanissaro sets out at Pc 4: over the limit, the offence is full whatever the monk believes.22​ 23 The Vibhaṅga adds an instruction in the same spirit: a monk who has had two layers laid "may ask for a third layer, and he should then leave", which, Brahmāli's endnote reports, the Commentary takes as showing that he may not keep on asking.24 Doing or directing the work while standing where crops grow, grain and vegetables, is wrong conduct.25

    Disputed — what the three-layer limit restricts: the areas around the door and windows, the roof, or the whole building.

    The Vibhaṅga, Thanissaro observes, is non-committal exactly where the readings part.26 On the rule's syntax, which his translation follows, the layers reinforce the wall around the door frame and the windows, where a door or shutter blowing open would damage it. The Commentary, he reports, puts the limit on the roof alone, a reading that "does violence to the syntax of the rule" but has its logic, since an overloaded roof burdens building and sponsor more than a door frame does.27​ 28 Ajahn Brahm, following the Commentary by his own account, puts the three-layer limit on the roof, and adds that the walls may be replastered only around the door and windows.29 Brahmāli takes the widest reading: no exception is stated for any other part of the building, and if the rule is meant not to burden lay supporters, three layers is the limit anywhere.30 Ñāṇatusita, on the word for covering, thinks it probably refers to the wall as well as the roof.31 The texts do not settle which the rule intends; what all of them share is the limit itself: no more than three layers keeps it on every reading, and replastering the walls only around the door and windows meets Ajahn Brahm's as well.

    Modern materials do not divide into layers as tiles and grass do, and Brahmāli says in class that the rule "doesn't really fit very well with modern ways of building"; what the texts do give is the purpose. He draws it out from Ajahn Brahm's rule of thumb that a monk's dwelling should be no better than his supporters' homes: "it's nice to live in a place that is simpler, even much simpler, than the poorest of your supporters."32 The three layers are a number; what they measure is the sponsor's generosity.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • A dukkaṭa for doing the work, or directing it, while standing where crops are growing.33​ 34
    • A dukkaṭa where the covering is within the limit but the monk takes it to exceed it, or is unsure.35

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he applies two or three layers; if he applies less than two or three layers; if it is a shelter; if it is a cave; if it is a grass hut; if it is for the benefit of someone else; if it is by means of his own property; if it is anything apart from a dwelling; if he is insane; if he is the first offender.36

    Related Offences

    • Sg 7 — the anchor for "a large dwelling" and the meaning of its sponsor, with no limit of size; Thanissaro treats this rule as its extension.
    • Sg 6 — the anchor for "builds", and the measurement Thanissaro reports the Sub-commentary using to exempt small dwellings.
    • Pc 11 — destroying vegetation; the origin story's barley field brings it within reach, though this rule's dukkaṭa concerns where the monk stands, not what he destroys.
    • Pc 18 — the chapter's other building rule: there a single loose leg falls through a floor, here a whole dwelling comes down.

    A note on sourcing

    The Story, the rule, the definitions, the counting by the piece, the permutations and the non-offence clauses are from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation, with his endnote on leaving after the third layer, and the recitation line from his Pātimokkha recitation translation. The Manual gives the referral to Sg 6 and Sg 7 and his reading of the limit as applying anywhere. Thanissaro's BMC1, with its Addendum, supplies the two factors, the purpose read from the non-offence clauses, the two readings of the limit, and the Commentary's and Sub-commentary's views as he reports them. Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes gives his roof limit and replastering restriction and the own-resources exemption; his retelling has Channa gather the grass and sticks for repairs, which the Vibhaṅga does not say, and is not carried. The Concise BMC was checked and condenses BMC1 without adding to it. Ñāṇatusita gives the rule translation and the sense of adhiṭṭhātabbaṃ. The class transcript (session 023, 10 May 2012) gives Brahmāli's remark on modern materials and his rule of thumb from Ajahn Brahm.
