    Nissaggiya Pācittiya

    With the thirty nissaggiya pācittiya rules we cross for the first time into the lahukāpatti, the "light offences", everything below the pārājikas and saṅghādisesas. "Light" does not mean trivial. It means recoverable by confession, without the communal process of penance and probation that a saṅghādisesa sets in motion. What sets this class apart from the lighter classes still to come is the extra step its name carries: before the offence can be confessed and cleared, the wrongly handled article must first be given up.1

    The name gives the two parts in order. Nissaggiya, in connection with a training rule, means "entailing forfeiture"; used of an article, it means "to be forfeited". Pācittiya is a word of uncertain etymology. The Parivāra offers a didactic derivation, that it names the letting of skilful qualities fall away (patati) through a deluded mind (citta), though the term is more likely connected to the verb pacinati, "to discern or know".2 Every rule in the class follows one pattern. A bhikkhu acquires or uses some requisite wrongly (a robe kept too long, wool carried too far, money accepted, a bowl exchanged before its time), and he must forfeit it before he may make the offence known and confess it to a fellow bhikkhu, a group, or the whole Community.3

    In most cases the forfeiture is symbolic. Once the offender has confessed, the article is returned to him so that a donor's gift does not go to waste, and what the rule corrects is the wrongful holding, not the possession of the thing.4 Only three rules in the class require the article to be given up for good, and they concern the things a bhikkhu may never rightly own at all: money, and what is gained through dealing in it.5 Even then, the forfeiture protocols let the Community benefit from the item, again as a way of preserving the faith of the one who gave it.6

    The thirty rules divide evenly into three chapters (vagga) of ten: the robe-cloth chapter, the silk-and-wool chapter, and the bowl-and-requisites chapter.7 The first five rules of the middle chapter (NP 11–15) concern the santhata, a felted blanket, which today is "all but unknown". The bhikkhunīs did not inherit these rules, and Brahmāli suggests that this may mean the santhata was not an important requisite or, perhaps, that it went out of use after the earliest period.8

    Footnotes (Nissaggiya Pācittiya — Class Overview)

    1. The garukāpatti/lahukāpatti (heavy/light) division is set out in the Saṅghādisesa class overview; the nissaggiya pācittiya is the first light class. Thanissaro, BMC1, Nissaggiya Pācittiya, class introduction — each rule "involves an item that a bhikkhu has acquired or used wrongly, and that he must forfeit before he may 'make the offense known'—confess it." The forfeiture step is what distinguishes this class from the plain pācittiya class, cleared by confession alone.↩︎

    2. Thanissaro, BMC1, Nissaggiya Pācittiya, class introduction — "nissaggiya … means 'entailing forfeiture'. Used in connection with articles, it means 'to be forfeited.'" On pācittiya: "a word of uncertain etymology," the Parivāra's patati/citta derivation given as didactic, the likelier connection being to pacinati (pp. pacita), "to discern, distinguish, or know."↩︎

    3. Thanissaro, BMC1, Nissaggiya Pācittiya, class introduction — "Each of the rules in this category involves an item that a bhikkhu has acquired or used wrongly, and that he must forfeit before he may … confess it … to a fellow bhikkhu, a group of bhikkhus, or to the Community as a whole. This confession is what clears him of the offense."↩︎

    4. Thanissaro, BMC1, Nissaggiya Pācittiya, class introduction — "In most cases, the forfeiture is symbolic. After his confession, the offender receives the item in return so that, as a donor's gift, it does not go to waste."↩︎

    5. Thanissaro, BMC1, Nissaggiya Pācittiya, class introduction — the three rules requiring permanent forfeiture rather than return (the money and monetary-dealing rules, NP 18–20); cross-refers to the Introduction's note that money, gold, and gems are the clearest cases of items a bhikkhu may never rightly own.↩︎

    6. Thanissaro, BMC1, Nissaggiya Pācittiya, class introduction — "Even under the three rules requiring that the offender give up the item for good, the forfeiture protocols allow for the Community to benefit from the item, again as a way of preserving the donor's faith."↩︎

    7. Thanissaro, BMC1, Nissaggiya Pācittiya — "There are thirty rules in this category, divided into three chapters (vagga) of ten rules each": the Robe-cloth Chapter (NP 1–10), the (silk and wool) Chapter (NP 11–20), and the bowl-and-requisites Chapter (NP 21–30).↩︎

    8. Brahmāli, The Bhikkhunī-vibhaṅga, "The nissaggiya pācittiyas (NP)" — of the twelve nissaggiyas unique to the monks, "Five of the twelve concern blankets, the so-called santhatas. The fact that these rules were not inherited by the nuns may mean either that the santhata was not considered an important requisite or, perhaps, that its use was discontinued after the earliest period. In the present day, the santhata is all but unknown."↩︎
