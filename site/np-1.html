    NP 1

    Story (Vatthu)

    After staying at Rājagaha for as long as he liked, the Buddha set out wandering toward Vesālī. On the road between Rājagaha and Vesālī he saw a number of monks walking along, loaded up with robes on their heads, shoulders and hips. He thought, "These foolish men have turned to an abundance in robes too readily. Let me set a limit on robes for the monks." He arrived at Vesālī and stayed at the Gotamaka Shrine. It was midwinter, when the days are cold and snowy, and the Buddha sat outside at night wearing only one robe without being cold. Becoming cold at the end of the first part of the night, he put on a second robe. Becoming cold again at the end of the middle part of the night, he put on a third. At the end of the last part of the night, when the sky was flaring up at dawn, he became cold once more, and putting on a fourth robe, he was fine. He thought, "Even those on this spiritual path who come from respectable families, who are sensitive to the cold and fear the cold, are able to get by with three robes." He told the monks what had happened and what he had thought, and allowed them three robes: a double-layered outer robe, a single-layered upper robe and a single-layered sarong.1

    When they heard about this, the monks from the group of six went to the village in one set of three robes, stayed in the monastery in another set, and went to bathe in yet another. The monks of few desires complained and criticized them, "How can the monks from the group of six keep extra robes?" They told the Buddha, who had the Saṅgha gathered, questioned the six and rebuked them: "Foolish men, how can you do this? This will affect people's confidence." He laid down a first rule, that a monk who keeps an extra robe commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession.

    Soon afterwards Venerable Ānanda was offered an extra robe. He wanted to give it to Venerable Sāriputta, who was staying at Sāketa, and knowing that the Buddha had laid down a rule against having extra robes, he thought, "What should I do now?" He told the Buddha, who asked, "How long is it, Ānanda, before Sāriputta returns?" "Nine or ten days." The Buddha then allowed an extra robe to be kept for ten days at the most, and laid down the rule in its final form.2 Soon the monks were given extra robes and did not know what to do with them, and the Buddha allowed them to assign an extra robe to another.3

    Synopsis — the Buddha, having found over one cold night that three robes suffice, allows the monks three; the group of six take the allowance as licence to keep three sets; and the limit is eased, to ten days by Ānanda's robe for Sāriputta, and then by the allowance to assign an extra robe to another, into the machinery this rule now carries.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "When his robe is finished and the robe season has ended, a monk should keep an extra robe for ten days at the most. If he keeps it longer than that, he commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession."4

    Condensed recitation translation — "When a bhikkhu does not have robe-season privileges, if he keeps robe-cloth for more than ten days that is neither determined nor stored through vikappana, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."5

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "When a bhikkhu has finished his robe and the frame is dismantled (his kaṭhina privileges are ended), he is to keep extra robe-cloth ten days at most. Beyond that, it is to be forfeited and confessed."6

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Keeping an 'extra-cloth' beyond ten days, except when the end of the rainy-season privileges or the Kaṭhina privileges still apply, is a nissaggiya pācittiya."7

    Ñāṇatusita

    "When the robe [-cloth] is finished by a bhikkhu, when the kaṭhina [-frame-privileges] have been withdrawn, [then] extra robe [-cloth] is to be kept for ten days at the most. For one who lets it pass beyond that, [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture."8

    The five renderings agree on what the rule requires and differ over its opening clause. The Pāli, ubbhatasmiṃ kaṭhine, says that the kaṭhina, the frame on which robes were sewn, has been lifted. Thanissaro keeps the frame and glosses it; Ñāṇatusita, who argues that the verb means "withdraws" and not "destroys", keeps the privileges the word had come to stand for; and Brahmāli translates by the purpose, "the robe season has ended", because the metaphor "would normally be lost on a modern audience".9​ 10 The recitation and Ajahn Brahm turn the clause into the rule's exception, a monk who "does not have robe-season privileges", and the recitation alone builds the definition of an extra robe into the rule as well. The choice changes nothing, since the Vibhaṅga defines the clause by the end of those privileges, and Effort sets them out.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    What this rule protects is a monk's contentment with the cloth he needs. The Buddha set the limit after meeting monks on the road loaded with robes, of whom he thought, "These foolish men have turned to an abundance in robes too readily", and after a midwinter night on which one robe served him until the first part of the night was over and a fourth was needed only at dawn; his conclusion was that even those "sensitive to the cold" are "able to get by with three robes".11 The limit gave way at once to a real need, Ānanda's robe for Sāriputta, who was nine or ten days away, and not to the group of six, who kept three sets of three. And so the ten days are time to settle what a piece of cloth is for, not leave to keep it.

    This is the first rule of the Nissaggiya Pācittiya class, and it carries the machinery the class runs on: what makes cloth extra, the two ways it stops being extra, the season in which the rule does not apply, and the relinquishment and confession by which the offence is cleared. Later rules point back here for each of them. Thanissaro gives the offence two factors, object and effort; Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual lists five, which fall under the same two, the robe-season privileges under effort and ownership, material and size under object. I take Thanissaro's two, the simpler division.12​ 13

    Object

    A piece of extra robe-cloth: cloth of one of the six kinds, "not smaller than what can be assigned to another", that "has not been determined nor assigned to another".14 The six are linen, cotton, silk, wool, sunn hemp and hemp; by the Great Standards synthetic fibres count as well, while cloth of hair, grass, bark or hide, and by extension leather, is outside the rule.15 Colour does not take a piece out of it: cloth dyed a colour unsuitable for robes still counts, and so does white cloth, which can be dyed.16​ 17 The smallest piece that counts is eight standard fingerbreadths by four, about 16 by 8 cm on Thanissaro's figure, which the Manual follows; Brahmāli's appendix of technical terms gives the standard fingerbreadth a larger value, on which the piece would be about 40 by 20 cm.18​ 19​ 20 The length of a standard fingerbreadth is argued at Pc 87. A piece must reach both measures: on the view of the Burmese Vinaya masters that Ajahn Brahm reports, a long bandage only three fingerbreadths wide is not extra cloth, though he notes that neither the Canon nor the Commentary speaks to the point.21

    The cloth must be his. Finished requisites count as well as uncut cloth, but robe-cloth belonging to the Community does not, so the sheets and pillowcases of a monastery dwelling are outside the rule.22​ 23 Nor is cloth his until it reaches his hand. A robe offered for an absent monk and kept for him by others is not yet his extra cloth, however long it waits, and his ten days begin when it is handed to him.24​ 25

    Cloth stops being extra when it is determined for use or assigned to another. The Khandhaka sorts the requisites: the three robes, the sitting mat, a sheet, a washcloth and a cloth for requisites are determined and not assigned, while the rainy-season robe and the itch-covering cloth are determined while in use and assigned otherwise.26 Only one cloth may be determined as each of the three robes. A monk determines a cloth by touching it while resolving in his mind what it is to be, or by saying so aloud, with or without the customary Pāli formula.27 A spare robe, since only one set can be determined as robes, is determined as a cloth for requisites, a category with no limit on its number. The Commentary records a majority of elders, with one dissenting voice, holding that even the three robes may be determined that way, to escape the rule that follows; Ajahn Brahm observes that the Buddha's limit was thereby forgotten, and Brahmāli that the common practice of keeping extra robes "is only made possible by exploiting loopholes in the rules".28​ 29​ 30

    Assignment, vikappanā, is for cloth a monk is not using. Brahmāli shows from the Vibhaṅga's own words that it is a change of ownership in name, the cloth staying in the first monk's hands, and renders it "assignment" against the common "shared ownership", for which he finds no evidence in any source.31 Assigned cloth may be kept as long as one likes but not used until the assignment is undone. How it is made and undone, and the offence of using the cloth before, belong to Pc 59.

    Cloth that was determined or assigned can become extra again, and the ten days then start at once. By the Commentary's list, determination ceases when the cloth is given away, which includes its being relinquished under any rule of this class; when it is stolen, lost or destroyed, or taken on trust; when the owner commits a pārājika, disrobes, dies or changes sex; when he formally relinquishes the determination; and, for the three robes alone, when the cloth develops a hole.32 Most of these take the cloth out of his hands as well. The ones that leave it with him, and so bring it under this rule, are his own relinquishing of the determination and the hole, and, for assigned cloth, his taking it back on trust or the other monk's rescinding the assignment.33 The hole is the case that catches monks. By the Commentary it is a full break, through both layers of a double-layer robe, at least the size of the nail of the little finger, and set at least a span from the long side of the robe and eight fingerbreadths from the short, or four on a lower robe. A worn spot farther in than that is best patched before it is cut out, for the determination lapses if it is cut first.34

    Disputed — what kind of hole makes a robe's determination lapse.

    Against the Commentary's measurements, Ajahn Brahm reports the view of other respected elders that a determination lapses only when a tear or hole is of such a size and in such a place as to make the robe impractical to wear.35 Neither side's reasons are given in the sources, and the question is open. A monk who mends a hole and re-determines the robe within ten days has nothing to fear from either view.

    Effort

    Keeping the cloth past the tenth dawn after it was received. The Vibhaṅga puts the offence "at dawn on the eleventh day", and the Commentary explains that the dawn of the day the cloth was given counts as the first, so the offence falls on the tenth dawn after receipt, as the Manual states it.36​ 37 The span is therefore always a little less than ten full days. Ajahn Brahm's case shows the count running from a lapse rather than a gift: an upper robe that tears in the middle on the evening of Tuesday 3 June, and is left as it is, brings the offence at dawn on Friday 13 June.38 The texts do not define dawn itself; the question is argued at Pc 37. Perception does not excuse. The Vibhaṅga gives the full offence whether the monk thinks more than ten days have passed, is unsure, or thinks fewer, and equally if he takes the cloth to be determined, assigned, given away, lost, destroyed, burned or stolen when it is not.39

    The ten days do not run in the robe season, which is what the rule's opening clause excepts. For a monk who has not taken part in the robe-making ceremony it is the last month of the rainy season; for one who has, it runs on through the cold season, five months in all.40​ 41 The ceremony is allowed to monks who have completed the rainy-season residence, and of the five things it makes allowable, "as much robe-cloth as you need" is what lifts this rule, as Brahmāli and Ñāṇatusita both note.42​ 43 It was the season when lay people who had come to know the monks over the rains gave cloth, and when monks gathered enough pieces to sew a robe.44 The extended season ends with the cold season, or earlier "according to one of the eight key phrases" or when "the Sangha has ended it".45 In practice a monk loses it early only if the Saṅgha ends it by a formal act, or if he has come to the end both of his tie to the monastery (he has left without intending to return, or has learned while away that the Saṅgha has ended the privileges) and of his robe-making (he has finished the robe, decided not to make one, lost the cloth, or given up the expectation of more); either alone is not enough. The Manual states the second case in its commonest form, leaving for good and giving up the wish to make a robe.46​ 47 Once the season ends, cloth kept through it must be determined, assigned or given away within the ten days that follow. Which other rules the season lifts is counted differently, five by Thanissaro and six by Ajahn Brahm, who adds NP 2; that difference concerns the month without the ceremony and belongs to NP 2.48​ 49

    Disputed — whether the month's privileges belong only to a monk who has completed the rains residence.

    Ajahn Brahm says they do, and denies them to a monk who did not complete the residence or who entered the second one.50 Thanissaro records the restriction as an old tradition the Vinaya-mukha follows and says he has tried to trace its source in the Canon and commentaries "but without success". The Vibhaṅgas make the last month of the rainy season the robe season, which is also the last month of the second residence, and "neither the Canon nor the commentaries to these rules make these privileges contingent on having completed the Rains".51 The robe-making ceremony is another matter, since the Khandhaka allows it only to monks who have completed the residence; the dispute is over the month alone. The Vibhaṅga's definition of the month, "for one who has not participated in the robe-making ceremony", says nothing of the residence, which is the textual ground of Thanissaro's reading. The definition leans his way, on our reading, but a lean is not a ruling, and the restriction remains the practice Ajahn Brahm teaches; a monk who did not complete the residence should know which reading his monastery keeps.

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    Giving the cloth away, one of the exemptions, needs words that give. By the Commentary, "I give this to you", "I give this to so-and-so" or "Take this, it's yours" transfer the cloth, while "Make this yours", "May this be yours" or handing it over in silence do not, and since perception does not excuse, cloth given away like that still counts as his.52

    Procedure

    Because this is the class's first rule, the Vibhaṅga gives the procedure in full, and the later rules refer back to it. The monk relinquishes the cloth first and confesses after. He may relinquish it to a Saṅgha, to several monks or to one, and there is a form of words for each; to one monk it is "This robe-cloth, which I have kept over ten days, is to be relinquished. I relinquish it to you." He then confesses the offence, the other monk receives the confession, and the cloth is given back: "I give this robe-cloth back to you."53 By the Commentary the whole may be done in any language.54 The Concise BMC adds, without naming its source, that if the cloth is lost or destroyed before it can be relinquished, the monk may simply confess.55 It must be given back: when the group of six did not give relinquished robe-cloth back, the Buddha made the failure an offence of wrong conduct.56 The pattern holds for every rule of the class except the few in which the article is relinquished to a full Saṅgha and not returned, which are noted where they come.57 Brahmāli suggests that the point of so largely ceremonial a relinquishment "might be to make the rule stick better in memory".58

    The returned cloth is extra cloth again with ten new days, so the wise course, Thanissaro advises, is to determine or assign it as soon as it comes back.59 The rule asks more than a count of days. Ajahn Brahm writes of the "monks of few desires" who determine their one outer robe, one upper robe and one sarong as such, and keep whatever else cold or sun requires "to an absolute minimum".60

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — using robe-cloth that should be relinquished before relinquishing it. The Vibhaṅga states this penalty at only six rules of the class, this one, NP 2, 3, 21, 28 and 29, and the Commentary extends it to all of them.61​ 62
    • Dukkaṭa — keeping cloth that is not past ten days while thinking it is, or while unsure; the Commentary takes the offence to lie in using it.63​ 64
    • Dukkaṭa — for the monk who receives relinquished cloth and does not give it back. The Commentary confines this to one who assumes the cloth is now his and would judge one who knows it is not under Pj 2, by its value; Thanissaro answers that the relinquishment gives up ownership, so the cloth has no owner and cannot be stolen, and keeps the Vibhaṅga's wrong conduct in every case.65​ 66

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if within ten days the cloth has been determined, assigned to another, given away, lost, destroyed, burned, stolen or taken on trust; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.67 The rule does not apply in the robe season.

    Related Offences

    • NP 2 — being apart from one of the three robes, under the same opening clause; whether the month's privileges lift it is argued there.
    • NP 3 — robe-cloth received out of season, kept up to a month while more is awaited.
    • NP 21 — the same ten-day limit for an extra bowl.
    • Pc 59 — using assigned cloth before the assignment is undone, and where assignment is set out.
    • Pc 87 — where the length of a standard fingerbreadth is argued.
    • Pc 37 — where the meaning of dawn is argued.

    Related Suttas

    • AN 8.30 — "This Dhamma is for one with few desires, not for one with strong desires", and "for one who is content", a bhikkhu being "content with any kind of robes, almsfood, lodgings, and medicines and provisions for the sick"; the ten days are for settling cloth beyond that contentment, not for keeping it.68
    • MN 3 — "Bhikkhus, be my heirs in Dhamma, not my heirs in material things"; the three sets the group of six kept were an inheritance of the second kind.69
    • SN 16.8 — the elder monks of former times, as the Buddha describes them to Mahākassapa, who "were triple-robe users and spoke in praise of using the triple robe": the measure the origin story's cold night arrived at, kept as a practice and praised.70

    The Story is from Brahmāli's translations alone, his Khandhaka for the road and the cold night, which the Vibhaṅga presupposes, and his Vibhaṅga for the rule's two stages; Ajahn Brahm's account of a standing exchange of requisites between Ānanda and Sāriputta is not in either and is not followed. The Explanation rests on those translations, the Manual, Brahmāli's appendix of technical terms and his introductions, Thanissaro's BMC1 and BMC2, and Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes; the Concise BMC restates BMC1 and is cited once, and Ñāṇatusita is used for the rule's opening clause. The Commentary is cited as Brahmāli, Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm report it and was not read for the rule. The class on this rule was read and is not cited, and in Related Suttas AN 8.30, MN 3 and SN 16.8 are quoted from Bhikkhu Bodhi's translations.
