    NP 20

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī, in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time Venerable Upananda the Sakyan had become skilled at making robes. He made an upper robe of old cloth, well-dyed and beautifully made, and he wore it.

    Just then a certain wanderer who was wearing an expensive robe went up to Upananda and said, "Your upper robe is beautiful. Please give it to me in exchange for my robe."

    "Are you sure?"

    "I am."

    Saying, "Alright, then," he gave it.

    The wanderer put on the upper robe and went to the wanderers' monastery. The wanderers said to him, "This upper robe of yours is beautiful. Where did you get it?"

    "I traded it for my robe."

    "But how long will it last? Your other robe was better."

    The wanderer realised they were right, and so he returned to Upananda and said, "Here's your upper robe. Please give me back mine."

    "But didn't I ask you if you were sure? I won't give it back."

    Then that wanderer complained and criticised him: "Even householders give back to each other when they regret a trade. How, then, can a monastic not do the same?"

    The monks heard the wanderer's complaint, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised Upananda: "How could Venerable Upananda barter with a wanderer?" After rebuking him in many ways, they told the Buddha. Soon afterwards he had the Saṅgha gathered, questioned Upananda, and rebuked him: "Foolish man, how could you do this? This will affect people's confidence." Then he laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — Upananda trades robes with a wanderer, refuses to undo the deal when the wanderer regrets it, and the rule against a monk bartering follows.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk engages in various kinds of barter, he commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu barters, except with fellow monastics, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu engage in various types of trade, it (the article obtained) is to be forfeited and confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Exchanging anything of one's own for something other than gold or money is a nissaggiya pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should engage in the various kinds of bartering, [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture."6

    The rule's kayavikkaya is literally "buying and selling", and the renderings divide over whether money is in it. Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga and recitation say "barter" and Ñāṇatusita "bartering", reading it as goods for goods, Ñāṇatusita noting that "no money appears to be involved here", though Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual counts one's own money among the goods;7 Thanissaro's "trade" leaves money in, and Ajahn Brahm's "anything of one's own for something other than gold or money" includes buying with money outright.8 Which trades the rule covers was argued at NP 19, and I follow the Vibhaṅga there in placing buying with money here. The recitation writes in the exception for fellow monastics, which comes from the origin story of NP 5.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule protects a monk's freedom from the trader's bargain, which the Story shows going wrong in a monk's hands. Upananda made a deal and held the wanderer to it, and the wanderer's complaint was that "even householders give back to each other when they regret a trade"; the Buddha's rebuke was that "this will affect people's confidence."9 Thanissaro gives the purpose from the monk's side: the rule relieves him of the responsibility a trade brings, of getting a fair price for his goods and giving a fair deal to the other.10 The Story shows a monk who took that responsibility and discharged it badly.

    The offence has two factors, object and effort.11

    Object

    Anything at all, the Vibhaṅga says: the four requisites, and "even a bit of bath powder, a tooth cleaner, or a piece of string."12 The Commentary limits the rule to allowable goods, and Thanissaro reads the list the other way, as reaching even the least valuable allowable things and more valuable ones all the more.13 The Manual adds that a monk's own money is among the things that can be traded, so that buying with it falls here.14 Ajahn Brahm's examples are as ordinary as the rule: two dollars for an orange juice in a café, a spare bowl bartered for a robe, excess sugar traded for tea.15

    Services. The Vibhaṅga speaks of goods, and Brahmāli in class read its word, bhaṇḍa, as physical goods, so that paying for a service would fall outside the rule, though he offered it tentatively.16 Thanissaro reports that the Commentary treats wages paid for a service as a trade, to be confessed as a pācittiya since the payment cannot be got back, and Thanissaro himself speaks of a cheque or card receipt handed over "in exchange for goods or services".17​ 18 Which reading is right the texts do not say, and a monk who pays for a service by stating his need, as the non-offence clauses allow, has no question to answer.

    Disputed — whether trading with goods or funds that are not the monk's own is the offence.

    The Vibhaṅga defines a trade as "his own goods are in the hands of the other and the other's goods are in his own hands."19 In Thanissaro's report the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī reads "his own" as his personal possession, and so does Ajahn Brahm: a monk who buys with a cheque or card on his own account commits the offence, but one who exchanges at the airline office a cheque his mother drew on her account incurs none, since her goods go to the airline and not his; commanding a fund held for him is, for him, wrong conduct under NP 10.20​ 21 Thanissaro rejects the deduction, since the Vibhaṅga elsewhere forbids a monk to buy with funds that are not his and the non-offence clauses exempt no trade with another's goods; so "his own" means whatever starts on his side of the trade, a steward's funds held for him among them, and a monk who hands a signed card receipt or a cheque to a seller commits the full offence when goods and paper change hands.22​ 23 The texts do not decide it; my own judgment is only that a monk who wishes to be safe treats any card or cheque he uses to buy for himself as within the rule.

    Effort

    Proposing the trade and completing it. He "misbehaves, saying, 'Give that for this,' 'Bring that for this,' 'Trade that with this,' 'Exchange that for this'", and that is wrong conduct; when his goods are in the other's hands and the other's in his, the full offence.24 Thanissaro adds that a proposal by gesture, a note or sign language counts the same, since the non-offence clauses make no exception for it.25 Perception does not excuse: a trade is the full offence whether he perceives it as a trade, is unsure, or does not.26

    Procedure

    The goods are relinquished to a Saṅgha, a group or an individual, by the procedure of NP 1, and given back.27 Where the thing was bought with money, Ajahn Brahm suggests the other monks may encourage him to give it away for good, though he grants that this goes beyond the Vibhaṅga; where it has been used up, he confesses.28

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    Each of the exemptions, asking the price, telling an attendant, saying what he has and what he needs, leaves the deal to the other side.29 Thanissaro deduces from the Commentary that a monk who has asked the price should not then bargain, even where bargaining is the custom, a taxi fare for instance; and he holds that what a monk may tell an attendant is what he needs, never an order to buy or exchange.30 Trading with the five kinds of fellow monastic is no offence, from the origin story of NP 5; and the Commentary, Thanissaro reports, allows the plain "Give this. Take that", which is no trade, only with a monk's parents, since to anyone else it wastes a gift of faith or is begging.31

    Upananda's wanderer asked for the trade and then asked to undo it, and the monk held him to the bargain, where even a householder would have given it back. A monk who only says what he has and what he needs leaves the bargain, and its fairness, to the other side.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — proposing the trade, before it is completed.32
    • Dukkaṭa — what is not a trade, perceived as a trade or doubted.33

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he asks about the value; if he tells an attendant; if he says, "I have this and I have need of such-and-such;" if he is insane; if he is the first offender.34

    Related Offences

    • NP 19 — trading in money, which takes every trade that ends with money in the monk's hands.
    • NP 18 — receiving money; a monk who buys with money he holds has usually broken it already.
    • NP 10 — the steward and the fund, and the line between a hint and a command; Ajahn Brahm makes commanding the fund wrong conduct under that rule, Thanissaro this rule's full offence.
    • NP 5 — the exchange of robes with fellow monastics, from whose origin story the exception here comes.

    Related Suttas

    • DN 1 §1.10 — among the virtues for which the worldling praises the Tathāgata, "he refrains from running errands, from buying and selling"; buying and selling, kayavikkaya, is the very word of this rule.35

    A note on sourcing

    The Story, definitions, permutations, relinquishment and non-offence clauses are from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation, and the Manual gives one's own money and one's own goods. Thanissaro's BMC1 gives the purpose, the factors, the object's reach, the Commentary on services and on parents, the case against reading "his own" narrowly, cards and cheques, gestures, bargaining and the attendant; Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes gives the examples, the mother's cheque, funds and forfeiture. The Commentary is cited from Thanissaro's report of it; Ñāṇatusita for his rendering and reading. The class on this rule (session 015, 28 February 2012) was read for its questions, and one view of Brahmāli's on services, given only there, is kept. DN 1 is quoted in Walshe's translation. The Concise BMC adds nothing.
