    Sg 10

    Story (Vatthu)

    At one time when the Buddha was staying at Rājagaha in the Bamboo Grove, Devadatta went to Kokālika, Kaṭamodakatissaka, Khaṇḍadeviyāputta and Samuddadatta and said to them, "Let's cause a schism in the Sangha of the ascetic Gotama. Let's break its authority." Kokālika said, "The ascetic Gotama is powerful and mighty. How can we achieve this?"

    Devadatta's answer was to go to the Buddha and request five things, saying, "In many ways, sir, you praise fewness of wishes, contentment, self-effacement, ascetic practices, being inspiring, the reduction in things, and being energetic. And there are five things that lead to just that": that the monks should stay in the wilderness for life, eat only almsfood for life, wear rag robes for life, live at the foot of a tree for life, and not eat fish or meat for life, and that whoever stayed near an inhabited area, accepted an invitational meal, accepted robe-cloth from a householder, took shelter, or ate fish or meat should commit an offence. "The ascetic Gotama won't allow this. We'll then be able to win people over with these five points." Kokālika said, "It might be possible to cause a schism in the Sangha with these five points, for people have confidence in austerity."

    Devadatta and his followers went to the Buddha, bowed, sat down, and Devadatta made his request. The Buddha replied, "No, Devadatta. Those who wish may stay in the wilderness, and those who wish may live near inhabited areas. Those who wish may eat only almsfood, and those who wish may accept invitational meals. Those who wish may wear rag robes, and those who wish may accept robe-cloth from householders. I have allowed the foot of a tree as a resting place for eight months of the year, as well as fish and meat that are pure in three respects: one hasn't seen, heard, or suspected that the animal was specifically killed to feed a monastic."

    Thinking, "The Buddha doesn't allow the five points," Devadatta got up glad and elated, bowed, circumambulated the Buddha and left with his followers. He then entered Rājagaha and won people over with the five points, saying, "The ascetic Gotama doesn't agree to them, but we practice in accordance with them." The foolish people with little faith and confidence said, "These Sakyan monastics are practicing asceticism and living with the aim of self-effacement. But the ascetic Gotama is extravagant and has chosen a life of indulgence." But the wise people who had faith and confidence complained and criticised Devadatta: "How can Devadatta pursue schism in the Sangha of the Buddha? How can he break its authority?"

    The monks heard that criticism, and the monks of few desires criticised Devadatta in the same way and told the Buddha. He had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned Devadatta: "Is it true, Devadatta, that you are doing this?" "It's true, sir." The Buddha rebuked him and laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — Devadatta asks the Buddha to make five ascetic practices compulsory, expecting the refusal he then uses to win a following, and the Buddha lays down the rule that a monk who pursues schism is to be corrected, and offends only if he will not stop.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk pursues schism in a united Sangha or persists in taking up a legal issue conducive to schism, the monks should correct him like this: 'Venerable, don't pursue schism in the united Sangha or persist in taking up a legal issue conducive to schism. Stay with the Sangha, for a united Sangha—in concord, in harmony, having a joint recitation—is at ease.' If that monk continues as before, the monks should press him up to three times to make him stop. If he then stops, all is well. If he does not stop, he commits an offense entailing suspension."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu is trying to cause a schism in the Saṅgha, he should be admonished up to three times to make him stop. If he does not stop, he incurs a saṅghādisesa."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu agitate for a schism in a united Community, or should he persist in taking up an issue conducive to schism, the bhikkhus are to admonish him thus: 'Do not, venerable sir, agitate for a schism in a united Community or persist in taking up an issue conducive to schism. Let the venerable one be reconciled with the Community, for a united Community, on courteous terms, without dispute, with a common recitation, dwells in peace.' And should that bhikkhu, thus admonished by the bhikkhus, persist as before, the bhikkhus are to rebuke him up to three times for the sake of relinquishing that. If while being rebuked up to three times he relinquishes that, that is good. If he does not relinquish (that), it entails initial and subsequent meetings of the Community."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Should any monk agitate for a schism in a community in concord, or should he persist in taking up an issue conducive to schism, he should be admonished thus by the monks: 'Do not, Venerable Sir, agitate for a schism in a community in concord, or persist in taking up an issue conducive to schism. Let the venerable one be reconciled with the community, for a community in concord, in complementary terms, free from dispute, having a common recitation, dwells in peace'. And should that monk, informally admonished thus by the monks persist as before, he is to be formally admonished up to three times by the monks so as to desist. If being formally admonished up to three times he desists, that is good. If he does not desist, it entails initial and subsequent meetings of the community."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should endeavor for the schism of a united community, or having undertaken, should persist in upholding a legal issue conducive to schism, [then] that bhikkhu should be spoken to thus by the bhikkhus: 'Venerable, do not endeavor for the schism of the united community, or having undertaken, persist in upholding a legal issue conducive to schism. Let the venerable one convene with the community, for a united community, which is on friendly terms, which is not disputing, which has a single recitation, dwells in comfort,' and if that bhikkhu being spoken to thus by the bhikkhus should persist in the same way [as before], [then] that bhikkhu is to be argued with up to three times by the bhikkhus for the relinquishing of that [course], [and if that bhikkhu,] being argued with up to three times, should relinquish that [course], then this is good, [but] if he should not relinquish [it]: [this is a case] involving the community in the beginning and in the rest."6

    The five renderings agree on what the rule requires. The recitation shortens a long rule to its essence, and what it drops is the correction itself, the words the monks are to say, which carry the rule's purpose and open the Explanation. The word that matters is the verb for the Saṅgha's act, samanubhāsati: Brahmāli's "press", Thanissaro's "rebuke", Ñāṇatusita's "argued with", and Ajahn Brahm's "formally admonished". His rendering follows Thanissaro's sentence closely, and it alone labels the two stages the rule implies, the monk "informally admonished" and then "formally admonished up to three times". The Vibhaṅga sets out those stages in full, and they are discussed below, under Procedure.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule protects the unity of the Saṅgha, and the Story shows it attacked from an unexpected side. Devadatta did not attack the practice; he asked for more of it, five austerities he presented as serving what the Buddha praised, made compulsory, and counted on a refusal he could use, "for people have confidence in austerity." The Buddha's answer left the first three to "those who wish" and pointed to what he had already allowed for the other two, and it was that room the schism set out to break. The monks who correct a would-be schismatic tell him, "Stay with the Sangha, for a united Sangha—in concord, in harmony, having a joint recitation—is at ease", and the word for ease, phāsu, is the word the Vinaya uses for the second of its reasons for every training rule, "for the comfort of the Sangha".7 A schism achieved dishonestly ranks, by the Aṅguttara Nikāya as Thanissaro cites it, with matricide, patricide, killing an arahant and maliciously shedding a Buddha's blood among the five most heinous acts.8 Yet the rule answers the dispute with a correction, not a penalty, and the correction comes three times, then three more, then from the whole Saṅgha, with the same promise at every stage: "If he stops, all is well."

    This is the first of four rules, Sg 10 to 13, in which the offence falls inside an act of the Saṅgha; the procedure is set out here once, and the Explanation is organised around what the Saṅgha does and the monk's part in it. Thanissaro reads the rule as two related offences: pursuing schism, with two factors, intention and effort; and persisting in a legal issue conducive to schism, with effort alone, each completed when the monk will not stop through a properly performed formal act.9 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual makes the two alternative forms of a single factor, acting to divide the Saṅgha, beside the Saṅgha's act and the monk's refusal to stop.10 I follow Thanissaro's division, since it marks that a monk can incur the second offence with no aim of schism at all, and I treat the Saṅgha's act under its own label.

    Intention

    Pursuing schism is defined by its aim: "thinking, 'What can I do to split, separate, and divide them?' he searches for a faction and puts together a group."11 The second offence carries no such aim. A stubborn monk may hold to a position conducive to schism without yet intending one, and the rule reaches him too; Thanissaro thinks it works best before the two have joined, for once a faction has formed around a disputed point it is free to protest the Saṅgha's act, and unanimity becomes hard to reach.12 Nor does a good motive take a monk outside the rule. One who sincerely champions what he holds to be the true Dhamma and Vinaya is to debate it, defer to the learned elders, and take it to other monasteries or a chosen committee; where even a vote would only harden the factions, the dispute is to be left unresolved. If he will not let it go and the Saṅgha presses him, he incurs the saṅghādisesa, in Ajahn Brahm's words, "just the same".13 The motive decides something else, his own fate if a schism is accomplished, which is the Cullavagga's concern and not this rule's.14

    Effort

    To pursue schism is to look for a following and bind a group, and it need not involve any point of doctrine: Thanissaro's example is a monk who arranges special meals for his friends alone, the faction-making that Pc 32 also restrains.15 To persist in a legal issue conducive to schism is to take up and proclaim one of the eighteen grounds the Vibhaṅga refers to, listed in the Cullavagga, each a misrepresentation one way or the other of the Dhamma, the Vinaya, what the Buddha said, practised or laid down, or what is an offence and how heavy it is.16​ 17 The Manual does not take the list as a strict limit: any dispute that may lead to schism belongs here, though lesser matters seldom stir enough feeling to do it.18 In that it widens what the Vibhaṅga's definition keeps to the eighteen grounds, and Thanissaro, following the Vibhaṅga, keeps the second offence to them, though he warns that even minor disputes can become schismatic.19 Not every disagreement is such an issue: a friendly difference of interpretation is not, a heated and abusive one is.20

    The Saṅgha the rule protects is a united one, "those belonging to the same Buddhist sect and staying within the same monastery zone".21 It need not be united around the true Dhamma, and this is the case that teaches the rule's method. A monk who gathers a following by explaining Vinaya as Vinaya in a community whose practice has gone astray can still be legitimately pressed, and incurs the full offence if he does not stop. Thanissaro draws the lesson: such a monk should aim to win over the whole community, not a clique; failing that, seek help from other communities, or leave as amicably as he can.22

    What the rule heads off is a schism completed. By the Cullavagga, as Thanissaro sets it out, that needs a united community of at least nine monks, a dispute over one of the eighteen grounds, at least four monks on each side, and the two sides conducting separate Pāṭimokkha recitations, Invitations or other transactions within the same territory; short of that, the division is a crack (rāji) and not a split (bheda).23 A dispute can be fought bitterly without becoming one, as at the Second Council, and once a dispute has become a major issue the rule gives way to the Cullavagga's procedures for settling disputes.24

    Procedure

    We now come to what the Saṅgha does. The Vibhaṅga sets out three stages, and the monk can end the matter at any of them by stopping. First, the monks who see or hear of his efforts correct him in the rule's own words, up to three times, and those who hear and say nothing commit an offence of wrong conduct; the Commentary, Thanissaro reports, binds every monk within half a yojana (the Sub-commentary excepting those unable to go), and only in person, never by letter or messenger.25 If he stops, all is well; if not, he commits an offence of wrong conduct. Second, he is brought into the Saṅgha, "even if he has to be pulled", and corrected there up to three times more, with the same outcome. Third, a competent and capable monk presses him by a formal act of one motion and three announcements, which each monk who approves meets with silence. After the motion he commits an offence of wrong conduct, after each of the first two announcements a serious offence, and when the last announcement is finished, the saṅghādisesa.26​ 27 The saṅghādisesa then annuls the offence of wrong conduct and the serious offences. The commentaries extend that sentence, by the Great Standards, into a principle for every rule; Thanissaro, noting that the Vibhaṅga states it for these four rules and not in the parallel procedure of Pc 68, takes it to be theirs alone.28

    A monk who would not stop through the third announcement will probably not acknowledge the offence either, which gives the Community grounds to suspend him; Thanissaro suggests that testing how stubborn a divisive monk is may have been the original purpose of these four rules. The Community may also authorise a member to tell the laity what he has done (Pc 9), and send word to any community he tries to join. But if he proves to have been right about the Dhamma and Vinaya, its action draws attention to its own conduct, so a Community does well to reflect on its own practice before using the rule.29 During the Rains a monk may leave his residence to help end an attempt at schism elsewhere, and may leave one where he does not wish to be present for it.30

    Perception

    The monk's view of the procedure does not matter; its validity does. If the procedure is legitimate, he commits the saṅghādisesa whether he perceives it as legitimate, is unsure of it, or perceives it as illegitimate; if it is illegitimate, he commits an offence of wrong conduct however he perceives it.31 Legitimate means, as Ajahn Brahm explains, that the act meets the requirements binding on every act of the Saṅgha, such as every monk within the monastery zone being present or having sent his consent.32 That even the illegitimate act leaves an offence is, for Thanissaro, of a piece with the Buddha's counsel at Kosambī, that a monk who does not see his offence, but whom learned and impartial monks hold guilty, should confess it out of confidence in them rather than risk schism.33

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    "If he stops", in the non-offence clause, covers the private corrections: a monk who stops before they end commits nothing at all. One who holds out past them incurs an offence at each further stage, and on Thanissaro's account these are nullified only when the full offence falls.34

    The words the monks are given to say are the whole rule in small. They do not condemn him; they ask him back, to a Saṅgha that is at ease because it is one. Thanissaro's counsel to the monk who is sure he is right is the same invitation from the other side: his aim in expounding the Dhamma and Vinaya "should never be to create a faction. Instead, it should be to convince all who are sincere to join in the pursuit of correct practice."35

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — for a monk who hears of the attempt and says nothing; for the monk pursuing schism who does not stop after the private corrections, after the corrections in the Saṅgha, or after the motion; and for one who does not stop when the procedure pressing him is illegitimate.36​ 37
    • Thullaccaya — after each of the first two announcements of a legitimate procedure; with the dukkaṭa after the motion, annulled when the saṅghādisesa falls.38

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he has not been pressed; if he stops; if he is insane; if he is deranged; if he is overwhelmed by pain; or if he is the first offender.39

    Related Offences

    • Sg 11 — the monks who side with one pursuing schism, pressed by the same procedure.
    • Sg 12, Sg 13 — the same procedure, for a monk who will not be admonished and for one who criticises his banishment.
    • Pc 68 — the same pressing for a monk who holds a pernicious view, where the lesser offences are not said to be annulled.
    • Pc 32 — the group meal, one way a faction is gathered.
    • Pc 9 — telling the unordained of a monk's serious offence, which the Saṅgha may authorise.

    Related Suttas

    • AN 8.7 — Devadatta "bound for the plane of misery, bound for hell, and he will remain there for an eon, unredeemable", overcome by gain, honour and evil desires: the eight conditions that overcame the monk who split the Saṅgha, and the hell to which they bound him.40
    • Iti 18 — schism in the Saṅgha as the one thing that arises for the harm and suffering of many, and the verse, which the Cullavagga also carries: "Having divided a united Sangha, / He boils in hell for an eon."41
    • MN 128 — the Kosambī monks "stabbing each other with verbal daggers", and Anuruddha's companions "blending like milk and water, viewing each other with kindly eyes"; the dispute and the concord between which the rule's correction works.42
    • AN 5.78 — the fifth future peril: the Saṅgha "now dwelling at ease—in concord, harmoniously, without disputes, with a single recitation", and the schism to come, when "it is not easy to attend to the Buddhas' teaching". It is the ease the correcting monks' words name.43
