    NP 16

    Story (Vatthu)

    While the Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery, a certain monk was given wool as he was walking through the Kosalan country on his way to Sāvatthī. He bound it into a bundle with his upper robe and carried on. People who saw him teased him, "Venerable, how much did it cost you? How much will the profit be?" As a result he was humiliated. When he arrived at Sāvatthī, he threw the wool to the ground. The monks asked him why. "People have been teasing me because of this wool." "But how far have you carried it?" "Over 40 kilometers."1

    The monks of few desires complained and criticized him, "How could a monk carry wool more than 40 kilometers?" After rebuking him, they told the Buddha. He had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned the monk: "Is it true, monk, that you did this?" "It's true, sir." The Buddha rebuked him — "Foolish man, how could you do this? This will affect people's confidence" — and laid down the rule.2

    Synopsis — a monk carrying gifted wool down the road to Sāvatthī is teased by people asking what it cost him and what profit he will make; the Buddha caps how far a monk may carry raw wool himself.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If wool is given to a monk who is traveling, he may receive it if he wishes. If he receives it and there is no one else to carry it, he may carry it himself for at most 40 kilometers. If he carries it further than that, even if there is no one else to carry it, he commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu receives unspun wool while travelling and he carries it for more than fifty kilometres, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should wool accrue to a bhikkhu as he is going on a journey, he may accept it if he so desires. Having accepted it, he may carry it by hand — there being no one else to carry it — three yojanas (48 km.=30 miles) at most. If he should carry it farther than that, even if there is no one else to carry it, it is to be forfeited and confessed."5

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Travelling further than 3 yojana carrying unspun wool which one acquired while on the journey is a nissaggiya pācittiya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "Now, if sheep's wool should become available to a bhikkhu who is travelling on a main road, by a bhikkhu who is wishing [so, it] can be accepted, having accepted [it, it] can be carried with his own hand for three yojanas at the most when there is no one present who can carry it; if he should carry it further than that, even when there is no one present who can carry it, [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture."7

    The renderings agree on what the rule requires and part only over the number. The Pāli sets the limit at tiyojana, three yojanas, which Thanissaro gives as 48 kilometres, Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga as 40 and his recitation as fifty, while Ajahn Brahm and Ñāṇatusita keep the yojana itself; what a monk should take from it is discussed below, under Effort. The wool is "wool", "unspun wool" or "sheep's wool", and the animal is taken up under Object. Ñāṇatusita adds that haritabbāni, "to be carried", has no causative sense: the rule is about the wool he carries himself.8

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule protects a monk's standing as a renunciant on the road, where a bundle of goods made one look like a trader. The monk in the Story was given his wool and carried it in a bundle, and the people who saw him asked what it had cost and what profit it would bring, until he threw it down at Sāvatthī in humiliation; the Buddha's rebuke was that "this will affect people's confidence."9 The rule does not stop him accepting the wool, "if he wishes"; it limits only how far he carries it himself.

    The offence has three factors: object, effort, and intention.10 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual has three too, the wool, receiving it while travelling, and carrying it himself; I take Thanissaro's division and treat the Manual's journey condition under Object, where the sources divide over it.11

    Object

    Wool not made into anything: unwoven, not made into a requisite, and covered even in the smallest amount.12 The Manual takes this to follow from the word itself, eḷakalomāni, "goat hairs", where Ñāṇatusita renders it "sheep's wool"; the rule does not depend on which animal it was.13​ 14 The Commentary, as Thanissaro reports it, excludes woollen cloth, woollen felt, woollen yarn and even raw wool tied up with a thread, though he notes that this last point contradicts the origin story, where the monk carried his wool tied up in a robe. In the other direction it includes even the wool a monk puts in his ear for an earache, or wraps around scissors to keep them from rusting, "so a bhikkhu should be careful not to travel more than three yojanas with such items."15 How the wool comes to him does not matter: from a Saṅgha, a group, a relative or a friend, discarded, or got with his own property.16

    Disputed — whether the rule covers only wool received while travelling.

    The rule speaks of wool given "to a monk who is traveling", which the Vibhaṅga defines as "to one who is walking on a road", and the Manual makes the consequence a factor: if he received it before setting out, there is no offence; Ajahn Brahm states the same, "no matter how far one carries these."17​ 18​ 19 Thanissaro grants that the wording "seems to indicate" the narrower scope, but reports the Sub-commentary's conclusion that the rule applies to wool acquired anywhere, drawn, on his account, from the non-offence clauses' giving no exception for wool got in any other way; and the factors he himself sets out carry no journey condition.20 The rule's wording, with the Manual and Ajahn Brahm, stands on one side; the Sub-commentary, in Thanissaro's report, on the other. The Manual's reading seems to me the closer to the rule as worded, though the question is open, and a monk who does not carry unmade wool far, wherever he got it, has nothing to decide.

    Effort

    Carrying it himself beyond the limit. The Vibhaṅga counts by the footstep: with the first foot over the limit, wrong conduct; with the second, the full offence. Standing within the limit and dropping the wool beyond it is the full offence too, and so is placing it in another's vehicle or among another's goods without their knowledge and letting it travel past the mark.21 Getting someone else to carry it is no offence, and the Vibhaṅga's "no one else to carry it" counts anyone, "either a woman or a man, either a householder or a monastic."22​ 23 Perception gives no shelter: carried past the limit, the wool is the full offence whether he perceives it as more, is unsure, or perceives it as less.24 The reverse error, under the limit while perceiving it as over or in doubt, is wrong conduct, and since none of the texts says for what, Thanissaro argues from the Commentary on a parallel passage at NP 1 that it falls on using the wool.25

    How far is three yojanas? A yojana is the distance a team of oxen draws a cart, and so it has no fixed length; Ajahn Brahm gives estimates running from Warder's four and a half miles to Childers's twelve, and takes, with the Vinayamukha, about sixteen kilometres, which three times over is Thanissaro's 48 as well.26 The Manual takes the common estimate of ten miles and rounds three yojanas to fifty kilometres, "a suitably round number given the uncertainty in the estimate", while Brahmāli's appendix of technical terms follows Rhys Davids at about thirteen kilometres, and that is the 40 of his full translation.27​ 28 Ñāṇatusita puts the yojana at about eleven kilometres, which would make the limit nearer thirty-three.29 No one of these figures can be shown to be right, and a monk who would keep the rule on all of them keeps to the lowest, three times Warder's four and a half miles, about twenty-two kilometres.

    Intention

    The Vibhaṅga excuses a monk who carries the wool to the limit meaning to stay there and then takes it further, and Thanissaro draws the factor from this: the offence falls only on one who crosses the mark for motives other than looking for a place to stay.30​ 31

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    He may carry the wool the full three yojanas, or less, or carry it there and back. Wool that was taken from him and got back, or relinquished under this rule and given back, he may carry on, and Ajahn Brahm reads this as another three yojanas.32​ 33 Once relinquished and returned, the wool is his to use as he pleases, for in itself it is not an unallowable thing.34

    The monk in the Story had nothing to fear from the gift itself. What the rule takes from him is the bundle on his back, and it leaves him the gift, with someone else to carry it.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — the first foot beyond the limit, the full offence falling with the second.35
    • Dukkaṭa — wool not carried beyond the limit, perceived as carried beyond it or in doubt; on Thanissaro's argument from NP 1, for using it.36

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he carries it 40 kilometers; if he carries it less than 40 kilometers; if he carries it 40 kilometers and then carries it back; if he takes it 40 kilometers with the intention of staying there, but then takes it further; if he gets back what had been taken from him and then takes it further; if he gets back what he had given up and then takes it further; if he gets someone else to carry it; if it is a finished article; if he is insane; if he is the first offender.37

    Related Offences

    • NP 17 — the companion wool rule: the same unmade wool, in the hands of an unrelated bhikkhunī.
    • NP 1 — the relinquishment procedure this rule refers to, and the parallel passage from which Thanissaro argues that the perception dukkaṭa falls on use.
    • NP 11 — the anchor of the felt-blanket rules, for which wool is the material.

    A note on sourcing

    The Story, definitions, permutations and non-offence clauses are from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation, with its endnote on the measure and his appendix of technical terms for the thirteen-kilometre yojana; the Manual gives the wool, the journey factor and the fifty-kilometre estimate. Thanissaro's BMC1 gives the factors, the Commentary's inclusions and exclusions, the Sub-commentary's conclusion on scope, the perception argument from NP 1 and the intention factor; Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes gives the journey scope, the yojana estimates and the returned wool; the Vinayamukha's estimate is taken through Ajahn Brahm and the Manual, not from the work. Ñāṇatusita is cited for the rendering, haritabbāni and the yojana, and the Commentary and Sub-commentary as Thanissaro reports them. The Concise BMC adds nothing, and the class on this rule is read but not cited.
