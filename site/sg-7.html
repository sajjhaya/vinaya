    Sg 7

    Story (Vatthu)

    At one time when the Buddha was staying at Kosambī in Ghosita's Monastery, a householder who was a supporter of Venerable Channa said to him, "I'll have a dwelling built for you, venerable, if you would find a site for it." While Channa was clearing a site for that dwelling, he felled a tree that served as a shrine and was revered by village, town, district, and kingdom.

    People complained and criticized him, "How could the Sakyan monastics fell a tree that serves as a shrine and is revered by village, town, district, and kingdom? They are hurting one-sensed life." The monks heard, and the monks of few desires complained in the same way, rebuked Channa, and told the Buddha. He had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned Channa, who admitted it. The Buddha rebuked him: "Foolish man, how could you do this? People perceive trees as conscious. This will affect people's confidence …" Then he laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — Venerable Channa, clearing a site for a dwelling a supporter has offered to build, fells a tree revered as a shrine; the Buddha lays down a rule requiring the Saṅgha's approval of the site for a large dwelling with an owner.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "When a monk builds a large dwelling with a sponsoring owner and intended for himself, he must have monks approve a site where no harm will be done and which has space on all sides. If a monk builds a large dwelling on a site where harm will be done and which lacks space on all sides, or he does not have monks approve the site, he commits an offense entailing suspension."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "It may be that a bhikkhu is building a large dwelling for himself with a sponsoring owner. If he does not get the Saṅgha to approve the site, he incurs a saṅghādisesa."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "When a bhikkhu is having a large dwelling built—having a sponsor and destined for himself—he is to assemble bhikkhus to designate the site. The site the bhikkhus designate should be without disturbances and with adequate space. If the bhikkhu should have a large dwelling built on a site with disturbances and without adequate space, or if he should not assemble the bhikkhus to designate the site, it entails initial and subsequent meetings of the Community."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Building a dwelling having a sponsor, without having obtained the Saṅgha's approval is a saṅghādisesa."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "By a bhikkhu who is having a large dwelling built, which has an owner, [and] is designated for himself, bhikkhus are to be brought to [it] for appointing the site. By those bhikkhus a site not entailing harm [to any creatures and] having a surrounding space is to be appointed. If a bhikkhu should have a large dwelling built on a site entailing harm [to creatures], [and] not having a surrounding space, or if he should not bring bhikkhus to [it] for appointing the site: [this is a case] involving the community in the beginning and in the rest."6

    The five renderings repeat Sg 6's pattern, and the recitation and Ajahn Brahm again leave the harm and space out of the rule. What matters here is one word. Sāmika is "a sponsor" for Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm and "an owner" for Ñāṇatusita, and Brahmāli's "sponsoring owner" takes a side: the word "generally means 'owner'", he notes, and he adds "sponsoring" only because the monk, though he does not own the dwelling, is still its builder and intended user.7 The reading decides which buildings the rule reaches, and is taken up under Object. "Large", for its part, is not a size: the word-analysis says a large dwelling is simply "one with a sponsoring owner".8

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule protects the site, and what others hold sacred on it, even where the building itself is freely given. Channa did not beg; a supporter offered him the dwelling. What went wrong was the site, a tree a whole kingdom revered, felled by a monk clearing ground for his own lodging, and the Buddha's answer is the procedure of Sg 6 without its size limit.

    The rule mirrors Sg 6: all explanations for it, Thanissaro says, may be inferred from that one, the only difference being that, since the dwelling here has a sponsor, no begging is involved and there is no need to limit its size.9 The factors are therefore Sg 6's less the size, and we turn only to what differs.

    Object

    The dwelling is defined as Sg 6's hut is, "plastered inside or plastered outside or plastered both inside and outside", and the dispute over what counts as plastered belongs to Sg 6.10 It has no limit of size, upper or lower, as Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual says;11 Brahmāli explains in class that the owner, not the monk, decides how large a building is.12 The site must be free of harm and have space on all sides, the inspection and approval run exactly as at Sg 6, and the transaction is again a motion and one announcement, though the word-analysis in Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga reads three here too.13

    Disputed — what sāmika means, a sponsor or an owner, and so which buildings the rule covers.

    Thanissaro reads it as a sponsor, anyone who underwrites the cost so that the monk need not beg: otherwise a donor who pays for a hut and hands it over to the monk when it is finished would fall under neither rule, and the non-offence clauses of NP 10 use the word for a donor who does not keep the robe; so a hut built from funds a monk keeps with a steward comes here as well. And by the Commentary's definition of "for himself", a hut whose sponsor keeps ownership of it is not under the rule at all.14 The Manual answers both him and Ajahn Brahm by name: the word means "owner", and nothing in the Vibhaṅga's definition suggests otherwise; a sponsor can come forward because one asked, so "sponsor" leaves no clear line between this rule and the last; Sg 6's strict size makes sense when people hand over their property and this rule's lack of one when the owner keeps it; Thanissaro's gap closes if the donor owns the building at least until it is finished; and at NP 10 the owners are those of the robe fund.15 The two readings part on a case the Manual calls the usual one in the West. A hut built for a particular monk on land owned by a monastery or a trust is, for Brahmāli, exactly this rule's case, needing the Saṅgha's approval; for Thanissaro, an owner who keeps it takes it outside the rule.16 The Commentary glosses the word as "without donor", the strongest support for the sponsor reading; Brahmāli answers that one can be both owner and donor, as when a donor gives the use of a hut and keeps the ownership.17 I lean to Brahmāli, since "owner" is the word's ordinary sense, Ñāṇatusita's grammar allows it,18 and it gives the two rules a clean boundary. On the site, a monk unsure which reading his Community follows loses nothing by having it approved, which is wrong under neither; but the reading also decides which rule, and so whether the size limit, governs a hut begged from several donors, which Ajahn Brahm gives to this rule and the Manual keeps under Sg 6; on where that boundary falls, the Vibhaṅga is silent.

    Effort

    The monk builds or has the dwelling built, and the offence and its steps come as at Sg 6: wrong conduct for each act, a serious offence for the last but one, the saṅghādisesa on completion, whether he finishes his own work or another's.19 Brahmāli's lecture gives the cases. A monk living alone whose supporter offers to build and keep a dwelling for him is inside the rule, even if the monk hinted at it, or asked; so is an abbot who tells his monks to build him a hut, though the monastery owns it, because he orders it and it is for him.20 Where a supporter builds a dwelling to give to a monk who takes no part in building it or getting it built, Thanissaro says the rule does not apply.21

    Intention

    The dwelling must be intended for himself, as at Sg 6.22 A dwelling built for an office, such as an abbot's hut that passes to whoever is abbot next, is built for another; but Brahmāli warns against using this as a trick, since an abbot for life who calls his own hut "the abbot's kuṭi" is being "a bit dodgy".23 Ajahn Brahm describes the normal practice as dedicating a sponsored dwelling to the Saṅgha of the four quarters, present and yet to come, and such a dwelling needs no approval.24

    Channa's ambition was not his mistake; his eye was. He saw a site for his dwelling where the people saw a shrine, and the Buddha's rebuke names what he missed: "People perceive trees as conscious." The inspection this rule requires puts other eyes on the ground before a monk builds there.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Saṅghādisesa — a dwelling finished on a site the Saṅgha has not approved.25
    • Thullaccaya — the last act but one in building it.26
    • Dukkaṭa — each earlier act; a finished dwelling on a site where harm will be done, and another on a site that lacks space; failing to go or send word on hearing that the builders are ignoring the requirements; for monk-builders who ignore them, one for each.27

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if it is a shelter, a cave, or a grass hut; if it is built for someone else; if it is anything other than a dwelling; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.28

    Related Offences

    • Sg 6 — a hut begged for by the monk himself, with no owner but him, where the size limit applies and the plastering question is argued.
    • NP 10 — the robe-fund whose donors Thanissaro and Brahmāli read differently in the dispute above.
    • Pc 11 — damaging plants, which felling a tree to clear a site would also breach.
    • Pc 19 — the further limits on building the same "large dwelling".
