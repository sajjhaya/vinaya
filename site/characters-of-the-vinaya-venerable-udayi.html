    Venerable Udāyī

    Udāyī enters the Vinaya giving advice. Venerable Seyyasaka had grown thin and pale, dissatisfied with the spiritual life, and Udāyī told him to eat, sleep and bathe as much as he liked, and when lust overwhelmed him, to masturbate. Asked whether that was allowable, he said, "Yes, I do it too."1 It is Sg 1, and it is the only rule of the five sexual saṅghādisesas that Udāyī did not occasion by his own act. The next four are all his.

    They are set in two places, his dwelling and the houses of the families he visited. At Sg 2 he showed a brahmin and his wife over his beautiful wilderness dwelling and, walking round the inner room, came up behind the wife and touched her all over; the brahmin left praising him, and his wife answered, "What's superb about him? He touched me all over just like you do."2 At Sg 3 a group of women came to see the dwelling and he spoke to them of their private parts. At Sg 4 a widow he had taught offered him the four requisites, and he asked instead for "what's hard for us to get", and then, when she agreed, spurned her. At Sg 5 he arranged marriages among the families he visited, and one of them went badly: a courtesan's daughter, given away on his word, was treated for a month like a daughter-in-law "but then like a slave", and when her mother came to him a second time he said, "Go yourself; I'm not going."3

    Brahmāli reads the stories that follow as one movement: "As rules were laid down and his avenues for expressing his defilements were gradually cut off, he would venture further afield trying to find satisfaction for his urges through all sorts of dubious conduct."4 The two aniyatas show it in two steps. Visākhā, invited to a family Udāyī supported, found him sitting alone with the family's new daughter-in-law on a concealed seat, and warned him; he did not listen, and Aniyata 1 followed. Told that a concealed seat was forbidden, he sat with the same girl in private on an open one. Visākhā found him again, warned him again in nearly the same words, and again he did not listen, and Aniyata 2 followed.5

    The Nissaggiyas and Pācittiyas take him into the company of nuns. His former wife had gone forth, and they visited one another often: at NP 4 she washed a robe he had soiled, and at Pc 30 the two of them sat alone together in private. At NP 5 he pressed the nun Uppalavaṇṇā, who had come to offer the Buddha meat, for her sarong, though she told him, "It's hard for women to get material support, and this is one of my five robes."6 At Pc 7 he taught women by whispering in their ears, so that a mother-in-law and her daughter-in-law each wondered whether he was the other's lover; at Pc 26 he sewed a nun a robe with a picture worked into the middle of it and arranged for her to wear it in procession. Pc 61 belongs here less certainly. The Vibhaṅga names its archer, who shot crows because he disliked them and set their heads out on stakes, as Udāyī, and Brahmāli, who counts it among this man's rules as evidence that he was also cruel, still writes "a certain Udāyī".7 The identification is probable, and no more. The big Udāyī of Pc 89, who had to stretch his sitting mat "as if an old hide", is not counted here: Brahmāli does not count him, and that story offers nothing but the name.

    Taken in order, these stories show something about how the Vinaya was made. Each rule answers one deed, and none anticipates the next; the rule against a concealed seat did not reach an open one until Udāyī found the gap. The training rules a monk now keeps as a list were laid down as a sequence, and in this sequence one man's mind is visible, testing each new rule for what it still allowed. Two other things are constant. Wherever the Buddha questions him, he does not deny what he has done. And the rebuke he hears is the Vibhaṅga's usual one, which in these stories falls on the same man again and again, and its concern is other people: "This will affect people's confidence."8

    Visākhā puts it in words a monk can carry with him. "Venerable, it's not appropriate for you to sit down in private alone with a woman on a concealed seat suitable for the deed. You may not be aiming at that act, but it's hard to convince people with little confidence."9 Udāyī heard that twice and listened neither time. A good part of the rules that bear his name say it again.
