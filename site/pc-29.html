    Pc 29

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Rājagaha in the Bamboo Grove, the squirrel sanctuary. At that time the nun Thullanandā was associating with a family from which she received a regular meal. On one occasion the head of that family had invited some senior monks. On the same day Thullanandā robed up in the morning, took her bowl and robe, and went to that family. She asked the head of the family, "Why have you prepared so much food?"

    "'Cause, venerable, I've invited the senior monks."

    "But who are those senior monks?"

    "Venerable Sāriputta, Venerable Mahāmoggallāna, Venerable Mahākaccāna, Venerable Mahākoṭṭhika, Venerable Mahākappina, Venerable Mahācunda, Venerable Anuruddha, Venerable Revata, Venerable Upāli, Venerable Ānanda, and Venerable Rāhula."

    "But why do you invite such inferior monks instead of the great ones?"

    "Who are these great monks?" "Venerable Devadatta, Venerable Kokālika, Venerable Kaṭamodakatissaka, Venerable Khaṇḍadeviyāputta, and Venerable Samuddadatta."

    While this conversation was taking place, the senior monks entered. Thullanandā said, "Is it true that you've invited these great monks?" "Just before you called them inferior and now you call them great." And that lay person threw her out of the house and made an end of her regular meal.1

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised Devadatta: "How could Devadatta eat almsfood knowing that a nun had it prepared?" They told the Buddha, who questioned him, established that it was true, and rebuked him — "Foolish man, how could you do this? This will affect people's confidence" — and laid down a preliminary ruling.2​ 3

    Soon afterwards a monk who had earlier left Rājagaha returned to see his family. Because it was a long time since he had last returned, people prepared food for him respectfully. And the nun who was associating with that family said to them, "Give food to that monk." The monk thought, "The Buddha has prohibited us from eating almsfood knowing that a nun had it prepared," and being afraid of wrongdoing, he did not accept it. And because he was unable to walk for alms, he missed his meal. After returning to the monastery, he told the monks what had happened, and they in turn told the Buddha. Soon afterwards the Buddha gave a teaching and addressed the monks: "Monks, I allow you to eat almsfood knowing that a nun had it prepared if the householder had intended to prepare it anyway." He then amended the training rule.4

    Synopsis — Thullanandā tries to redirect a family's meal from the eleven great disciples to Devadatta and his companions and is thrown out of the house; the Buddha forbids eating food a nun has procured, and when a monk keeping the new rule refuses his own family's food and goes hungry, he exempts food the householders meant to give in any case.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk eats almsfood knowing that a nun had it prepared, except if the householder had intended to prepare it anyway, he commits an offense entailing confession."5

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu eats almsfood knowing a bhikkhunī has had it prepared, except if the donors had intended to give it anyway, he incurs a pācittiya."6

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu knowingly eat almsfood donated through the prompting of a bhikkhunī, except for food that householders had already intended for him prior (to her prompting), it is to be confessed."7

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Eating almsfood knowing that it was obtained at the instigation of a bhikkhunī is an offence of Pācittiya."8

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should knowingly eat alms-food which a bhikkhunī has caused to be prepared, except through previous arrangement of householders, [this is a case] involving expiation."9

    The renderings agree on the act and part over two words. The first is paripācita, rendered "had it prepared", "prompting", "at the instigation of" and "caused to be prepared"; Ñāṇatusita argues from its form that it means causing someone else to cook.10 All of them describe a nun talking householders into cooking, which is what the word-analysis describes, so the choice changes nothing. The second is the exception, the householder who "had intended to prepare it anyway" in Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga and the donors who "had intended to give it anyway" in his recitation.11 Whether the third of the donors it defines marks an act on the occasion or a standing relation between donor and monk is this rule's live question, discussed below under Object. The almsfood itself is "any of the five cooked foods", where Thanissaro and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual say "staple", a rendering Brahmāli's own appendix gives up; I say "cooked food".12​ 13

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A monk's meals should come to him from the donors' own faith, not from a nun's advocacy, and this rule keeps him from eating one she has steered his way, for the well-being of the Saṅgha and the confidence of the people. Thullanandā's attempt shows what such steering looks like: she told a householder who had invited Sāriputta, Mahāmoggallāna and the other great disciples that he should have invited Devadatta and his companions instead, and it was the householder who threw her out of the house. The rule was laid down over Devadatta's eating what she had procured, and the Buddha's rebuke names its cost to people's confidence.14

    The chapter leaves the road here. Pc 27 and Pc 28 were about being seen travelling with a nun; this rule is about eating, and it is the first in the chapter about food passing from one Saṅgha to the other. It looks forward to the chapter on food at Pc 31–40, and sideways to Pd 2. Thanissaro gives three factors, object, perception and effort; the Manual has five, giving the nun's instigation and the exception lines of their own.15​ 16 Thanissaro's three are followed here.

    Object

    The object is food a nun has had prepared, and the food must be one of the five cooked foods, cooked grain, kummāsa, sattu, fish and meat, which the Vibhaṅga defines at Pc 35. Any other edible is no offence.17​ 18 How the five map onto a modern meal the sources answer differently. The Manual prefers "current standards", and in class Brahmāli gave potatoes and pasta as examples; Thanissaro, arguing from the Great Standards, allows any grain cooked as a staple, but warns that bread, pasta and potatoes were not always staples in the Buddha's India and so do not always fit.19​ 20​ 21 A monk should know that the modern extension is Brahmāli's reasoning.

    The nun's having it prepared is defined by example. She tells people who did not already mean to give that this monk is a reciter, learned, an expert on the discourses or the Monastic Law, and that they should give to him or cook for him.22 Two things follow. Her method is to praise a particular monk and urge that he be given food, not to ask for anything for herself, so the rule catches something that from outside looks like ordinary appreciation. General encouragement to support the Saṅgha names no monk, and on our reading of the definition's "this monk" it is not what the rule describes.23 And the factor depends on what the donors meant to do before she spoke: if they already wanted to give, nothing she says fulfils it.24​ 25 A nun ordained by both Saṅghas is grounds for the pācittiya; one ordained on the nuns' side only, for wrong conduct; a trainee nun or a novice nun, for nothing.26​ 27

    The exception defines the donors who already meant to give: "they are relatives or they have invited or they give regularly."28

    Disputed — whether the third of those, pakatipaṭiyattā, exempts a monk's regular supporters whatever prompted the meal, or only food they had already prepared before the nun spoke.

    Read as an act, the word means that the householder "has already prepared the food in question" for the monk, of his own accord, before the nun spoke; that is Thanissaro's rendering.29 Brahmāli takes it as a standing relation, and answers him by name in the Manual: pakati normally means "usual", so the word would seem to mean "regular donors"; his translation of the word-analysis has "they give regularly."30​ 31 On Thanissaro's reading the exemption is narrow and tied to the occasion; on Brahmāli's it covers a monk's regular supporters as such. Brahmāli's reading makes the three a list of one kind, relatives, those who have invited and those who give regularly, each a settled relation to the monk, and that seems to us a point in its favour. But it is his grammar against Thanissaro's rendering, with no third source arguing either; a monk who eats such a meal only when the householder had prepared it for him of his own accord before the nun spoke is safe on both.

    Perception

    Here the rule turns the other way from the two before it. "Knowing", jānaṃ, is in the rule, so knowledge is part of the offence: a monk who does not perceive that a nun had the food prepared commits nothing, whether or not she did.32 The Vibhaṅga defines knowing as at its other rules with the word: he knows by himself, or others have told him, or the nun has told him.33 Where she did have it prepared and he is unsure, it is wrong conduct; where she did not, but he thinks she did or is unsure, it is wrong conduct too.34​ 35

    Effort

    The offence falls in the eating, and in two stages. Receiving the food with the intention of eating it is wrong conduct, and every mouthful swallowed is a pācittiya.36 Nothing is required of the monk toward the nun: unlike Pd 2, where the monks must tell a nun directing the serving to stop, this rule has no reproof in it, and the monk who eats knowingly has simply offended.37 The second sub-story keeps the rule from becoming a reason to refuse one's own family. A monk who turned down the food his family had prepared for him, because a nun had told them to give it, went without his meal, and the Buddha's answer was the exception: what the householders meant to give anyway, a monk may eat, whoever spoke up for him.38

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • An offence of wrong conduct for receiving the food with the intention of eating it.39
    • A pācittiya for every mouthful swallowed.40
    • An offence of wrong conduct where the food was had prepared by a nun ordained on one side only.41
    • An offence of wrong conduct where a nun did have it prepared and the monk is unsure whether she did.42
    • An offence of wrong conduct where no nun had it prepared, but the monk perceives that one did or is unsure.43

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if the householder had intended to prepare it anyway; if a trainee nun has it prepared; if a novice nun has it prepared; if it is anything apart from the five cooked foods; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.44 Nor is there an offence where he does not perceive that a nun had it prepared.45

    Related Offences

    • Pd 2 — failing to stop a nun who is directing the serving at a meal; the rule most like this one in spirit, and the one that carries a duty to speak, where this rule penalises only the eating.
    • Pd 1 — accepting food with one's own hand from an unrelated nun in an inhabited area; the other rule on food passing between the two Saṅghas.
    • Pc 35 — where the five cooked foods that are this rule's object are defined.
    • Pc 27, Pc 28 — the travel pair before it, where perception does not mitigate; here it does, because the rule says "knowing".
    • Pc 4 — the perception pattern, which fails here in the lenient direction.

    Related Suttas

    • MN 21 — Moliya Phagguna, "associating overmuch with bhikkhunīs", so that a word against the nuns in his presence angered him and a word against him angered them: the partisanship between a monk and the nuns that this rule keeps out of the alms meal.46
