    Pc 77

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. The monks from the group of six intentionally made the monks from the group of seventeen anxious. They said, "The Buddha has laid down a rule that a person who is less than twenty years old shouldn't be given the full ordination. And you were less than twenty when you got the full ordination. Could it be that you're not fully ordained?"1

    They cried. Other monks asked them why, and they said, "The monks from the group of six intentionally make us anxious."2

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised them, "How can the monks from the group of six do this?" The Buddha questioned them, established that it was true, rebuked them, and stated the rule.3

    Synopsis — The weapon is a question, and it is the one question about himself a monk cannot answer from the inside. The rule that follows does not ask whether the question was a lie, because it was not one.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk intentionally makes a monk anxious, thinking, 'In this way he will be ill at ease at least for a moment,' and he does so only for this reason and no other, he commits an offense entailing confession."4

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu intentionally makes a bhikkhu anxious, only for the purpose of making him uncomfortable, he incurs a pācittiya."5

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu intentionally provoke anxiety in (another) bhikkhu, (thinking,) 'This way, even for just a moment, he will have no peace' — doing it for just that reason and no other — it is to be confessed."6

    Brahm

    "Deliberately provoking worry in a monk, by suggesting that he may have broken a rule of Vinaya, in order to make him uncomfortable is an offence of Pācittiya."7

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should deliberately provoke worry for a bhikkhu [thinking], 'Thus there will be discomfort for him, even [if only] for a short time,' having made just this the reason, [and] not another, [this is a case] involving expiation."8

    The renderings agree on the offence and differ in one place that matters. Kukkucca is "anxious" or "anxiety" in Brahmāli and Thanissaro and "worry" in Ajahn Brahm and Ñāṇatusita. The clause the whole offence hangs on is etadeva paccayaṁ karitvā anaññaṁ, "having made just this the reason and no other". Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga, Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita keep it whole; the recitation compresses it to "only", which is fair.9​ 10 Ajahn Brahm's line drops it, keeping only "in order to make him uncomfortable", and writes in the Vibhaṅga's mechanism instead, "by suggesting that he may have broken a rule of Vinaya".11 A reader of his rule alone would not know that a mixed motive is a defence, and that is the most important thing about the rule. It is taken up below, under Intention.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    Under this rule a monk answers for his brother's peace of mind, at the place where it is easiest to break: his confidence that he is a monk at all. The group of six asked the group of seventeen whether their ordination was valid, and the question was cruel because it could not be answered from the inside. They cried. Nothing false was said, and the rule does not ask whether anything was; what it censures is the wish to unsettle. A Saṅgha whose members sow doubt in one another for its own sake cannot live at ease, nor keep the confidence of which the rebuke speaks.

    Thanissaro gives the offence four factors: object, effort, result and intention.12 Whether the third belongs is disputed, and it is taken up in its place.

    Object

    The monk made anxious must be fully ordained, and perception of his ordination makes no difference, as under Pc 42.13​ 14 Anyone else is grounds for an offence of wrong conduct, and Thanissaro says this "apparently includes bhikkhunīs", the hedge being his.15

    Effort

    The Vibhaṅga illustrates the effort with four remarks: "It would seem that you were less than twenty years old when you were given the full ordination"; that you have eaten at the wrong time; that you have drunk alcohol; that you have been sitting in private with a woman.16 Thanissaro notices that most of them can be committed unknowingly and the last cannot, but that the last is close enough to an offence that the suggestion would still unsettle "an ignorant bhikkhu".17 The Vinaya-mukha, as Thanissaro quotes it, names the kind of remark the rule has in view: contingencies "impossible to know", which are enough to set an anxious monk worrying.18 The remark need not be true, nor the act it names an offence. The group of seventeen were the instigators of the rule against ordaining under twenty, and so, Thanissaro observes, not subject to it; any remark insinuating that a monk may have broken a rule fulfils the effort, even if what it names is no offence at all.19 Ajahn Brahm makes the same point about the validity of their ordination: the age limit had not yet been set when they were ordained.20

    Every specimen is framed as a surmise, maññe, "it would seem", and the framing is, I think, why the rule was needed. A monk who says "you are not really ordained", knowing it to be false, has lied, and the offence is Pc 1, as Ajahn Brahm notes.21 A monk who says "it would seem that you are not really ordained" has, on its face, only voiced a doubt. And so the offence here lies not in the content of the sentence but in what it was said for.

    Result

    Disputed — whether the full offence requires that the other monk be made anxious in fact, or whether the attempt completes it.

    Thanissaro makes result a factor, the anxiety actually arising, and gives two reasons: he cites the Commentary's gloss of the rule's verb as "generates", and the Vibhaṅga to Pc 55, a rule in some ways parallel, states explicitly that result is not a factor there, where the Vibhaṅga here says no such thing.22 Ajahn Brahm runs the same parallel the other way: by analogy with Pc 55, "it seems" to him that the offence is committed whether the monk is worried or not.23 I lean to Ajahn Brahm's reading, because the rule makes the offender's reason its whole condition — "just this the reason and no other" — and a rule conditioned that tightly on the agent's purpose is not naturally read as waiting on the other monk's reaction. But Thanissaro's argument from the silence of this rule's Vibhaṅga is not answered by anything in the texts, and my lean is a judgment about the rule's shape, not theirs. The practical difference is that on his reading a monk who tries and fails has not committed the pācittiya, and on the other he has.

    Intention

    "Intentionally" is defined by the Vibhaṅga's strongest formula for deliberateness, knowing and having decided, which Thanissaro notes is shared with Pj 3, Sg 1 and Pc 61.24​ 25 But the weight of the rule sits in the clause that follows. The Vibhaṅga defines "only for this reason and no other" flatly, as having no other reason for making him anxious, and it is the only factor Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual thinks worth stating.26​ 27 Thanissaro draws the consequence: the intention to cause anxiety "has to be the sole motive".28 A mixed motive is therefore a complete defence, not a mitigation. Ajahn Brahm's examples show the malicious case: telling a monk who thinks too much that the monks at his ordination probably performed the procedure improperly, or telling a new monk "maliciously" that a wet dream can sometimes be a saṅghādisesa.29 Thanissaro notes at Sg 1 that an emission in sleep can carry the full offence after all, when the monk made a fully intentional effort toward it before falling asleep, so the remark is not quite false;30 its wrong lies wholly in saying it to a man who cannot yet tell the difference, and in wanting him to lose sleep over it.

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The exemption is the offence with one sentence added. There is no offence if, "not wanting to make him anxious", a monk puts the same four remarks and then says, "Find out about it, so that you don't get anxious later".31 Thanissaro gives the case that shows why this is not a loophole. The other monk may well suffer a moment of anxiety, but the purpose is to spare him a greater one later — to find, say, after he has become a preceptor and ordained many others, that his own ordination was invalid — and so there is no offence in raising it "in a timely and compassionate fashion".32 Ajahn Brahm's mirror case is a new monk who misunderstands a careful explanation of the four pārājikas and loses sleep over it: there is no offence, for no one meant him to.33 "Find out about it" makes the difference between putting a man in a position to settle a question and putting him in a position to worry about it. Elsewhere the Vinaya gives the senior monk the opposite of the group of six's work as a duty: when his student becomes anxious, he "should dispel it or have it dispelled, or he should give him a teaching".34

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — intentionally making anyone not fully ordained anxious, however he is perceived.35
    • Dukkaṭa (Parivāra) — for the effort, while the remark is being made; the pācittiya follows when it has been made.36
    • Pācittiya (Pc 1) — where the anxiety is provoked by an outright lie, not by a surmise.37

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if, not wanting to make him anxious, he puts the four remarks and then says, "Find out about it, so that you don't get anxious later"; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.38

    Related Offences

    • Pc 72 — disparaging the training rules: the one other rule of the Pātimokkha whose own wording carries kukkucca, there as what the minor rules are accused of causing and here as what a monk may not cause.
    • Pc 65 — giving the full ordination to a person under twenty: the rule the insinuation invokes, whose origin story is the group of seventeen's own.39
    • Pc 55 — frightening a monk. Ajahn Brahm draws the difference cleanly: here the aim is "to flounder in doubt with regard to Vinaya", there to arouse fear.40
    • Pc 1 — deliberate lying: the rule that catches the same insinuation once the hedge is dropped.
    • Pc 16, 42, 78 — with this rule, the four pācittiyas whose rule carries "just this the reason and no other", as Brahmāli's endnote to the Parivāra groups them.41
    • Pj 3, Sg 1, Pc 61 — with this rule, the rules whose word-analysis defines "intentionally" by the same formula; Pc 61 also shares this rule's Pāli name, Sañciccasikkhāpada.42
    • Pc 76, 78 — the rules on either side, each laid down at Sāvatthī on account of the group of six's dealings with a fellow monk.43
    • Bi Pc 155 — the nuns' counterpart, the same rule with the genders changed.

    Related Suttas

    • MN 128 — monks "viewing each other with kindly eyes", the ease among them that a question meant to unsettle a monk's confidence in his own ordination destroys.44

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; the group of six's speech is quoted in their own words, because the rule's effort is that speech, and the complaint, interrogation and rebuke are given in the standing summary form. The rule carries no endnotes. The Manual, the recitation, the class (session 031, 23 July 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1 and BMC2, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were all checked; the Concise BMC condenses BMC1 here. The Commentary is Thanissaro's report of it, and the Vinaya-mukha, which is not used in this book, is known here only as Thanissaro quotes it. MN 128 is quoted from Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation.
