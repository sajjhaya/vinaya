    Pc 85

    Story (Vatthu)

    At one time the Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. The monks from the group of six entered the village at the wrong time, sat down in the public meeting hall, and talked about all sorts of worldly things: about kings, gangsters, and officials; about armies, dangers, and battles; about food, drink, clothes, and beds; about garlands and perfumes; about relatives, vehicles, villages, towns, and countries; about women and heroes; gossip; about the departed; about various trivialities; about the world and the sea; about the various kinds of existence.1

    People complained and criticised them, asking how the Sakyan monastics could enter the village at the wrong time, sit down in the public meeting hall, and talk about such worldly things — "They're just like householders who indulge in worldly pleasures!" The monks heard the complaints, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised the group of six. The Buddha rebuked them and laid down the first version of the rule, which made entering an inhabited area at the wrong time an offence with no exception.

    Soon afterwards a number of monks walking through the Kosalan country on their way to Sāvatthī came one evening to a certain village. People saw them and said, "Venerables, please enter the village." But knowing that entering a village at the wrong time had been prohibited by the Buddha, and being afraid of wrongdoing, they declined. And so thieves robbed them. They went on to Sāvatthī and told the monks, who told the Buddha, and the Buddha allowed a monk to enter at the wrong time after informing someone.

    Soon afterwards a monk walking through the Kosalan country on his way to Sāvatthī came one evening to a certain village. People saw him and said, "Venerable, please enter the village." Knowing that entering at the wrong time without informing anyone had been prohibited, and being afraid of wrongdoing, he declined. And so thieves robbed him. He went on to Sāvatthī and told the monks, who told the Buddha, and the Buddha allowed a monk to enter after informing an available monk.

    Soon afterwards a monk was bitten by a snake, and another monk went to the village to get fire. He then remembered that the Buddha had prohibited entering a village at the wrong time without informing an available monk, and being afraid of wrongdoing, he did not enter. They told the Buddha, who allowed a monk to enter without informing an available monk if there is some appropriate urgent business, and laid down the rule in its final form.2

    Synopsis — The group of six spend their afternoons gossiping in the village meeting hall and the Buddha forbids entering an inhabited area between noon and dawn; the rule is then relaxed three times over, as scrupulous monks keeping it are twice robbed and once prevented from fetching fire for a snakebitten friend.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk, when another monk is available, enters an inhabited area at the wrong time without informing him, except if there is some appropriate urgent business, he commits an offense entailing confession."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu, without having informed an available bhikkhu, enters an inhabited area between noon and dawn, except if the matter is urgent, he incurs a pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu, without taking leave of an available bhikkhu, enter a village at the wrong time—unless there is a suitable emergency—it is to be confessed."5

    Brahm

    "Entering an inhabited area between noon and dawn without having asked permission from another monk, unless there is no monk available to ask and except in times of emergency, is an offence of Pācittiya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu, not having asked (permission of) a bhikkhu who is present, should enter a village at the wrong time, except with an appropriate urgent duty, [this is a case] involving expiation."7

    The renderings diverge at several words and two of them need a note. Gāma is "an inhabited area" in both of Brahmāli's renderings and in Ajahn Brahm, and "a village" in Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita — but Thanissaro immediately widens his own word to cover "larger inhabited areas, such as towns and cities", so the sources agree on the substance and differ on where to put the widening. Brahmāli argues the general rendering in his appendix of technical terms, on the ground that the Vinaya regularly sets gāma against arañña, "wilderness", where "village" is plainly too narrow, and that the word-analysis elsewhere lets even a single house or a long-parked caravan count as one.8 The second word is anāpucchā: Brahmāli has "without informing", Thanissaro "without taking leave", Ajahn Brahm "without having asked permission", and the difference matters, since what a monk owes the other monk is discussed below, under Effort.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    Between noon and dawn a monk's place is not the village, and the rule guards that: his practice from afternoons that drift into the company of householders, and the laity's confidence that a monk is not one more of them. The group of six sat in the public hall talking of kings, armies, food, women and the rest, and people said, "They're just like householders who indulge in worldly pleasures!"9 What the rule asks in the end is not that a monk never go, but that he tell another monk first, and the origin story shows the Saṅgha learning that shape by injury. The first version forbade the act outright; monks who obeyed it were twice robbed, and a third could not fetch fire for a companion bitten by a snake. Each amendment is a repair.

    Thanissaro gives two factors, object and effort, and the second carries nearly everything.10

    Object

    An inhabited area. If it is enclosed, everywhere inside the enclosure counts; if it is not, the reach is the vicinity of its buildings, which Thanissaro gives the Sub-commentary as fixing at two clod's throws, roughly thirty-six metres.11​ 12 One consequence follows for a monk living in a monastery that stands inside a town: the area this rule governs begins at the vicinity of the nearest buildings outside it, not at his own gate.13

    Effort

    Entering at the wrong time, without informing an available monk, and without appropriate urgent business. The wrong time has a fixed definition: from the passing of midday until the next dawn.14 The rule thus takes up where Pc 46 leaves off, for that rule governs the morning, from dawn to noon, on days when a monk has been invited to a meal.15 Perception does not soften the offence: a monk who enters at the wrong time incurs the full penalty whether he knows the time, doubts it, or believes it is still the right time.16

    What the monk owes the other monk is information, not consent. Thanissaro reports the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī as reading taking leave as the bare act of saying "I am going into the village", or anything to that effect. The monk is not asking permission, though a monk who hears him and thinks the errand improper is free to say so, and treating that response with disrespect has its own penalty under Pc 54.17 Thanissaro gives the Commentary as adding that monks going in together need only inform one another, and that a monk who finds nobody in the monastery to inform need not inform the first monk he meets after leaving it.18 A junior monk still living in dependence is outside all of this: the Khandhaka protocols require him to ask his mentor's permission to enter an inhabited area at any hour, right time or wrong.19

    Disputed — how unavailable a monk has to be before he counts as unavailable. The Vibhaṅga's own gloss is circular, an available monk being one such that "he is able to inform him and then enter", and Ajahn Brahm holds that neither it nor the Commentary defines the term.20​ 21

    Four answers are on the record. Thanissaro, under Pc 46, gives the Commentary as making availability nearness: a monk met on the way out, within ordinary speaking range. Thanissaro rejects this as contradicting the Vibhaṅga, and reads availability as defeated only by an obstacle, a state of the other monk: he is asleep, sick, or receiving important visitors; short of these, "one is obliged to go out of one's way to inform him".22 Ajahn Brahm reads it as reasonable distance: a short detour to a hut does not excuse a monk, a few kilometres does.23 Brahmāli adds that a monk meditating in his hut should not be treated as available, since otherwise it "defeats the purpose of solitude in a meditation monastery"; his practical rule, to inform someone who happens to be about, comes close to the Commentary's.24 I find Brahmāli's the most persuasive, because it asks what the requirement is for. Thanissaro's is the one argued from the Vibhaṅga's own words, and a monk who goes out of his way to inform anyone not asleep, sick or with visitors is safe from offence on all four, though it asks more than Brahmāli would.

    Appropriate urgent business is read broadly. The Vibhaṅga's own case is the snakebite, where the errand was to fetch fire; Thanissaro's modern equivalents are fetching a doctor for a sick monk, or getting help when a fire has broken out.25 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual counts even the threat of rain, citing the Commentary; Thanissaro reports the same Commentary placing a gathering storm among the dangers, which the Vibhaṅga exempts separately.26​ 27

    The requirement's point is a small friction placed in front of errands, not their disclosure. Brahmāli put it better than any of the written sources: telling another monk that one is going into town with no good reason for it should feel a little awkward, and the point is "to have a slight sense of shame about just going into town if there isn't any good reason for doing so".28

    And so a gap opens, which Ajahn Brahm and Thanissaro both notice. Ajahn Brahm points out that this rule and Pc 46 between them do not close the day: the wrong time begins at noon, and the offence is defined as entering, so a monk who walks into town in the late morning and stays all afternoon has entered at the right time and never enters again. He offers two possible repairs, extending the wrong time backward to the end of the meal or reading the rule as being about being in an inhabited area rather than entering one, and adopts neither.29 Thanissaro names the same stretch, the morning on a day without an invitation, as covered by neither rule, and answers it with a policy, not a reading: inform a fellow monk whenever possible, "even during periods not covered by the rules".30 The letter leaves the gap open and the spirit closes it: a monk who times his arrival to keep the afternoon free has understood the rule exactly well enough to evade it.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • An offence of wrong conduct where it is in fact the right time but the monk perceives it as the wrong time, or is unsure of it.31
    • An offence of wrong conduct, on the Concise BMC's reading of the Khandhakas, where a monk living in dependence enters an inhabited area without his mentor's permission, and likewise where a mentor gives that permission for an errand for which it is not appropriate.32
    • No penalty attaches to the worldly talk itself, the subject of the origin story; a monk who makes a habit of both can instead be made the subject of a formal act of censure.33

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if there is some appropriate urgent business; if he enters after informing an available monk; if he enters without informing anyone when there is no available monk; if he is going between monasteries; if he is going to the nunnery; if he is going to the dwelling place of the monastics of another religion; if he is returning to the monastery; if the road goes via an inhabited area; if there is an emergency; if he is insane; if he is the first offender.34

    The four exempt destinations have a common character, and Ajahn Brahm names it: in each case the monk's business is not in the inhabited area among laypeople, even where the building he is going to stands inside one.35 The road exemption has a limit, which Thanissaro reports from the Commentary: a monk who leaves the road to go into the village proper should inform an available monk if there is one. For emergencies the same source gives lions or tigers approaching, or clouds building up and threatening a storm.36

    Related Offences

    • Pc 46 — visiting families before or after an invited meal; it governs the hours from dawn to noon on days a monk is invited to a meal, which this rule leaves alone, and the two are read together.37
    • Pc 7 — teaching Dhamma to a woman; Thanissaro sends the reader there for the discussion of worldly talk, which this rule's origin story raises.
    • Pc 54 — disrespect; the penalty a monk incurs if he treats with contempt the remonstrance of the monk he has just informed.
    • Pc 37 — eating at the wrong time; vikāla carries the same definition, from the passing of midday until dawn.

    Related Suttas

    • Snp 2.14 — "A bhikkhu should not wander at the wrong time … For ties stick to one given to untimely wandering": the afternoon in the village hall that began this rule, and the worldly ties the people's complaint points to.38

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register, with all four substories kept because each one produces a clause of the final rule; the list of worldly talk is the source's own itemised device and is preserved as such. The Manual, the recitation, the class (session 032, 30 July 2012), Brahmāli's appendix of technical terms for gāma, Thanissaro's BMC1, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were all checked. The Commentary, the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī and the Sub-commentary are given as Thanissaro and the Manual report them. Ajahn Brahm's retelling has the snakebite monk turn back after leaving the monastery, where the Vibhaṅga has him not enter at all; it is left standing. In class Brahmāli recalled the fourth substory as a monk escaping a fire, not fetching one, hedging it as he said it; the Vibhaṅga's version stands. Snp 2.14 is quoted in Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation.
