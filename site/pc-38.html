    Pc 38

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. Venerable Belaṭṭhasīsa, Venerable Ānanda's preceptor, was staying in the wilderness. After walking for alms he brought plain boiled rice back to the monastery, where he dried it and stored it. Whenever he got hungry, he moistened it and ate it. As a result, he went to the village for alms only after a long time.1

    The monks asked him, "Why do you only go for alms after such a long time?" And he told them.

    "But do you eat food that you've stored?"

    "Yes."2

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised him, "How can Venerable Belaṭṭhasīsa eat food that he has stored?" The Buddha questioned him, established that it was true, rebuked him, and stated the rule.3

    Synopsis — A forest-dwelling elder dries his almsfood and keeps it, moistening it when he is hungry, so that he needs to walk for alms only rarely; two questions from the other monks establish what he has been doing, and the rule that follows makes eating food from an earlier day an offence — one of the few rules whose original offender was an arahant, and whose fault was frugality.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk eats fresh or cooked food that he has stored, he commits an offense entailing confession."4

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu eats food stored overnight by a bhikkhu, he incurs a pācittiya."5

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu chew or consume stored-up staple or non-staple food, it is to be confessed."6

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Eating stored up food is an offence of Pācittiya."7

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should chew uncooked food or eat cooked food [while] keeping [it] in store, [this is a case] involving expiation."8

    Everything difficult about this rule is in its first word, sannidhikārakaṃ, and the renderings divide along the line of the dispute. Thanissaro's "stored-up" and Ajahn Brahm's "stored up food" describe the food; Ñāṇatusita's "[while] keeping [it] in store" describes what the monk is doing as he eats.9 Brahmāli's two statements of the rule also differ, over who does the storing: his Vibhaṅga translation has "food that he has stored", making the storer the eater, and his recitation has "food stored overnight by a bhikkhu".10​ 11 I follow the recitation, which states both of the rule's boundaries, the night and a monk as agent. "By a bhikkhu" is indefinite, the eater or any other monk, which is Thanissaro's "oneself or another" in three words.12 With the agent settled, what the line leaves open is what stored means, and that is the dispute taken up under Object.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A monk lives from one day's alms to the next, and this rule keeps him there, so that his food never becomes a store that frees him from the almsround and from his daily dependence on those who give. The monk who occasioned it was an arahant.13 Venerable Belaṭṭhasīsa, Ānanda's preceptor, dried his almsfood and moistened it when he was hungry, so that he walked for alms only after a long time, and Thanissaro notes that he did it "out of frugality rather than greed".14​ 15 The rule was laid down all the same, and Brahmāli gave the reason in the tradition's terms: storing food is a fault by convention, not a fault in the eyes of the world, since "everybody stores food".16

    Thanissaro gives the rule an object and an effort.17 The object comes first, since the dispute is there.

    Object

    The object is food that has been stored, and the Vibhaṅga's whole definition is one line: "received today and eaten on the following day."18 The day runs from dawn to dawn, so, as Ajahn Brahm points out, whatever is eaten between dawn and noon must have been accepted since that dawn.19 Which dawn is Pc 37's question, and so is the consequence that the two rules together leave no hour at which alms received before dawn could be eaten.

    Disputed — whether food received on an earlier day, then given up, may be eaten when it is offered again on a later day.

    On Thanissaro's reading it may not. He takes sannidhikārakaṃ as a description of the food, so that stored food simply is food received on a previous day, and a monk who eats it commits the offence "regardless of whether one has abandoned desire for it"; the Commentary's own allowance for food given up to novices he rejects as imported from NP 23.20 The Concise BMC's appendix argues that the word is an adverb of manner, as it is, it holds, everywhere else in the Canon, and that the rule's exemptions, "if he both stores and eats it during the right time", name the monk as the one who stores; on that reading food given up is stored by no monk and is outside the rule.21​ 22 Brahmāli reads the word at NP 23 as an act of storing that implies ownership, and in class put the rule in those terms: "you can only store what you consider to be yours".23​ 24 The grammatical case seems to me the stronger; but the Commentary is much older than either modern party, and the Vibhaṅga's line will bear both readings.

    In practice, Brahmāli's criterion is the one a monk can use. At his monastery the remains of every meal go back to the kitchen, and the monks are to regard that food as no longer theirs; once given up, it is not being stored.25 Ajahn Brahm allows the same leftover when a novice or lay person keeps it and offers it again on their own initiative.26 A monk who keeps his leftovers has stored them on every reading.

    Effort

    The effort is eating the food, and perception does not mitigate. Thanissaro spells out what that means in a monastery. A monk who eats food another monk received yesterday commits the offence whether or not he knows; no trace of yesterday's food may be left on the bowl he eats from today; and lay and novice attendants need to know that leftovers are not to be served to the monks on a later day.27 Receiving the food intending to eat it is wrong conduct, and every mouthful a pācittiya.28 Whether "it" there is food already stored or food one plans to store, Thanissaro notes, has been asked; he reports the Commentary taking the first, and Ajahn Brahm reads it the second way.29​ 30

    The rule is most often met at two further points. A tonic kept for days may be eaten by itself, but mixed with food it takes the date of its oldest part: salt kept for many days, added to today's meal, makes the meal food received many days ago, and Ajahn Brahm adds that "it is unallowable to drink coffee received yesterday with milk received today."31 This is the point on which the Second Council ruled, when carrying salt in a horn to season unsalted food was found to be "an offense entailing confession for eating what has been stored".32 And a separate prohibition, in the Khandhaka, concerns premises, not receiving: food stored indoors in a monastery may be eaten only if it was kept in a building the Saṅgha has designated as a store, and otherwise eating it is wrong conduct, while medicines and tonics may be kept anywhere.33​ 34 Food that lay people leave in such a store has not been received by anyone, and may be offered and eaten on whatever morning it is offered.35

    Thanissaro makes no claim to know why the frugal elder was rebuked; but he offers a guess. Food stored would let a monk avoid the almsround, and with it the daily reminder of his dependence on others, while the laity would lose their daily contact with the monks and their chance to give.36 The almsround Belaṭṭhasīsa was saving himself was the point.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — receiving post-midday, seven-day or lifetime tonics for the purpose of food, and a further dukkaṭa for every mouthful.37
    • Dukkaṭa — where the food has not been stored but he perceives that it has, or is unsure. Where it has been stored, all three perceptions carry the full pācittiya, on the pattern of Pc 4.38
    • Dukkaṭa, under the Khandhaka — eating food stored indoors in a monastery building not designated as a store, whether or not it was received on an earlier day.39​ 40

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he both stores and eats it during the right time; if he both stores and eats post-midday tonics during the remainder of the day; if he both stores and eats seven-day tonics during the seven-day period; if he uses lifetime tonics when there is a reason; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.41

    Storing is not itself the offence. A monk travelling with an unordained companion where alms cannot be had may carry that person's food while the companion carries his, and a monk may tell an unordained person to store food that has not been formally received.42​ 43 There is no exemption for illness, and Thanissaro adds that the Buddha once suspended the rule in famine and later reinstated it in a way that leaves no provision for suspending it again.44

    Related Offences

    • Pc 37 — eating at the wrong time; read with this rule it means a monk cannot accept alms before dawn.
    • Pc 40 — where formal receiving is defined, the act by which food becomes capable of being stored.
    • NP 23 — the seven-day tonics, whose rule also uses sannidhikārakaṃ, and from whose exemptions the Commentary is said to import its novice allowance.
    • Pc 35 — where fresh and cooked food are defined and where tonics taken as food carry the same pair of offences.
    • Pc 4 — the rule whose perception pattern this one follows.

    Related Suttas

    • AN 5.80 — among the perils to come, monks who "bond closely with monastery workers and novices" and then "engage in the use of various kinds of stored-up goods", which Thanissaro offers as another possible reason for the rule: communities that kept vast stores of food, he observes, have tended to grow lax.45​ 46
