    Pc 1

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī. At that time Hatthaka the Sakyan had been beaten in debate. While talking with the monastics of other religions, he would assert things after denying them, and deny things after asserting them; he evaded the issues, lied, and made sham appointments.1 The monastics of other religions complained and criticised him: "When Hatthaka talks with us, how can he assert things after denying them, deny things after asserting them, evade the issues, lie, and make sham appointments?"

    The monks heard the complaints, and went to Hatthaka and asked, "Is it true, Hatthaka, that you're doing this?"

    "These monastics of other religions should be beaten, whatever it takes! They shouldn't be allowed to win."

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised him: "How can Hatthaka assert things after denying them, deny things after asserting them, evade the issues, lie, and make sham appointments?" After rebuking him in many ways, they told the Buddha. Soon afterwards the Buddha had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned Hatthaka: "Is it true, Hatthaka, that you're doing this?"

    "It's true, sir."

    The Buddha rebuked him: "Foolish man, how can you do this? This will affect people's confidence." Then he laid down the rule.

    Synopsis — Hatthaka the Sakyan lies to avoid losing his debates with other sectarians, defends it when challenged, and the rule against a monk's deliberate lying follows.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk lies in full awareness, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "Lying in full awareness is a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "A deliberate lie is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Lying is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "In deliberate false speech, [there is a case] involving expiation."6

    The five renderings agree, and differ only in how they carry sampajāna, "fully aware". Brahmāli keeps it as a phrase of its own, "in full awareness"; Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita fold it into the adjective "deliberate"; Ajahn Brahm leaves it unstated, since for him a lie is deliberate by definition.7 The word carries half of the analysis, the three knowings discussed below under Intention, and Brahmāli's rendering keeps it in view as a condition.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule keeps a monk's word worth trusting, for the sake of his own path and of the confidence of those who hear him. The Pātimokkha's own introduction calls lying an obstruction, and the Buddha's rebuke in the Story names the other cost: "This will affect people's confidence."8 Hatthaka knows what he is doing: he lies to win, "whatever it takes", and defends the tactic openly when it is put to him. The rule asks nothing about the subject of a lie or its consequences, only about the speaker's awareness and aim. And so it stands behind the rules that give a particular lie its own penalty, a false claim to attainment or a false charge of a grave offence, and catches every lie they do not name.9

    Thanissaro gives the offence two factors, taken from the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī's summary of the Vibhaṅga's long permutations: intention, the aim to misrepresent the truth, and effort, the effort to make another person know what one wants to convey on the strength of that aim.10 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual also gives two, the intention to deceive and full awareness, knowing before, while and after speaking that one is lying.11 Thanissaro's two serve here, since full awareness is the deliberateness his intention already requires; it is treated there, beside the word on which the two analyses part.

    Effort

    To make another know is to convey the falsehood, through any sense and by any means. The Vibhaṅga frames it through the senses: saying one has seen what one has not seen, or has not seen what one has, and the same for hearing, for what is smelled, tasted or touched, and for what is known by the mind; its permutations add the claim made while one is unsure or does not remember.12 Thanissaro reports the Commentary as counting writing and gesture as well as speech, and Ajahn Brahm agrees that a monk can lie in a letter, "or even by a 'nod and a wink'".13​ 14 Silence is another matter. The Pātimokkha's introduction tells a monk who remembers an offence at the recitation and does not disclose it, "you have lied in full awareness", but the Mahāvagga gives that lie a dukkaṭa, which Thanissaro reads as showing that silence alone does not reach the full offence here.15​ 16

    Must the lie succeed? It need not, for result is not a factor: whether anyone understands the lie or is deceived by it makes no difference, and the offence is complete in the telling.17 Nor is passing on misinformation a lie: a monk who truthfully reports what he was told, or a mistaken belief he has genuinely formed, misrepresents nothing, since he reports his own state of mind accurately. If he says he believes it when he does not, he has lied.18

    The hard cases lie between the plain lie and the plain truth. Ajahn Brahm puts innuendo, exaggeration and half-truth there, and judges them by two questions, "whether the truth has been significantly distorted and whether there was a deliberate intention to deceive"; an exaggeration of the kind common in lay speech, uttered without deliberation, is not this offence.19

    Intention

    The aim fulfils the factor whatever the motive. A white lie, told to spare someone a truth too harsh to bear, falls under the rule as surely as a self-serving one, and Thanissaro's counsel is that a monk shielding someone from a hard truth "has to be very skillful in phrasing his statements".20

    Here the two analyses part on a single word. Thanissaro's factor is the aim to misrepresent the truth; Brahmāli's is the aim to deceive, and the difference decides the rule's one live question.

    Disputed — whether a false statement made only to amuse, with no wish to deceive, is an offence under this rule.

    The non-offence clause exempts one who speaks davā, "playfully", and one who speaks ravā, "too fast", and the Vibhaṅga glosses the first as speaking quickly.21 Thanissaro takes the gloss as limiting davā to haste, in keeping with suttas that class a lie told for a laugh as a lie. MN 61 is among them, where Rāhula is to train himself, "I will not utter a deliberate lie, even for a laugh"; on his reading, then, an outrageous joke is an offence.22 Brahmāli reads davā as fun, since ravā already means haste and davā plainly means fun at Pc 2; the gloss sahasā need not mean "hurriedly" but can be read sa + hasā, "with fun". His case rests on intention: speech without the aim to deceive "is not covered by this rule".23 Ajahn Brahm agrees that an outrageous lie told as a joke, not expected to be believed, is "no offence of Pācittiya".24 No text settles it, and the counsel is my own: only a monk who tells no false joke at all is safe on every reading; of jokes, the one no one could believe is the safer kind, and a monk who deceives because he finds deceiving funny may have, on Brahmāli's own test, the aim to deceive, and so be back under the rule.

    Full awareness is the rule's other word, and the Vibhaṅga spells it out as three knowings: "before he has lied, he knows he is going to lie; while lying, he knows he is lying; after he has lied, he knows he has lied."25 Its permutations add that he may also misrepresent his view, belief, acceptance and sentiment of what is true, but the three knowings already complete the offence. Brahmāli draws a practical test from them. A monk who, once the words are out, begins to wonder whether that was a lie has probably not lied, for he was not aware from the start that he was lying; a real lie is known as one in the telling, not discovered by later misgiving.26

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • A dukkaṭa for the monk who, hearing the Pātimokkha recited and remembering an unconfessed offence, deliberately stays silent about it — a lie by silence, penalised under the Khandhaka and short of the full offence here.27
    • A dukkaṭa, on Thanissaro's reading of Mv.III.14 (a promised rains residence), for making a promise with pure intentions and then breaking it; because the texts allow no exemption for circumstances beyond one's control, a bhikkhu should be careful how he states his plans.28
    • Where a lie falls under a more specific rule, that rule's penalty governs in place of this one: a false claim to a superior human state entails a thullaccaya (or, if specific, a pārājika) under Pj 4; a false charge of a pārājika, a saṅghādisesa under Sg 8; a false charge of a saṅghādisesa, a pācittiya under Pc 76; a false charge of a lesser offence, a dukkaṭa under the relevant rule (Ajahn Brahm, following the Vinaya-mukha, would make it a pācittiya here; see Pc 76).29

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    The Vibhaṅga exempts one who speaks playfully and one who speaks too fast, glossing them as speaking quickly and as meaning to say one thing and saying another, together with one who is insane and the first offender.30 Whether a joke that deceives no one belongs here is the question disputed under Intention.

    Related Offences

    • Pj 4 — the gravest lie: a false claim to a superior human state. A specific false claim is a pārājika; an unspecific one, a thullaccaya — either way handled there, not here.
    • Sg 8 — falsely charging a bhikkhu with a pārājika, hoping to have him disrobed; a saṅghādisesa.
    • Sg 9 — the same false charge built on a trumped-up pretext; the mirror of Sg 8.
    • Pc 2 — abusive speech, the next rule; davā, "in fun", which Brahmāli reads into this rule's non-offence clause, appears plainly there.
    • Pc 3 — tale-bearing, which reaches a true report; a false one is a lie under this rule, as Thanissaro reports the Vinaya-mukha.31
    • Pc 13, 24, 76 — the other rules that assign their own penalty to a particular kind of false or improper speech (criticising a Community official; imputing worldly motive to those who exhort the bhikkhunīs; an unfounded charge of a saṅghādisesa), and so displace this general rule for the lie they name.

    Related Suttas

    • Iti 25 — the rule's own word, sampajānamusāvāda, lying in full awareness, named as the one transgression after which no evil deed is ruled out: "That is, monks, lying in full awareness."32
    • MN 61 — "when one is not ashamed to tell a deliberate lie, there is no evil, I say, that one would not do"; the training given to Rāhula, "I will not utter a falsehood even as a joke", is wider than the rule, whose reach over a joke is the question disputed under Intention.33
    • SN 42.13 — among those rewarded in this life for breaking the precepts is a man who "made the king laugh with a lie": a falsehood meant to entertain is still a lie, one side of the question disputed under Intention.34
