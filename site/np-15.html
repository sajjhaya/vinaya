    NP 15

    Story (Vatthu)

    While the Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery, he addressed the monks: "Monks, I wish to do a solitary retreat for three months. No one should visit me except the one who brings me almsfood." The monks agreed, and soon afterwards the Saṅgha at Sāvatthī made an agreement of its own: anyone who visited him, apart from the one who brought his almsfood, must confess an offence entailing confession.

    Just then Venerable Upasena of Vaṅganta and his followers came to the Buddha, bowed, and sat down, and the Buddha greeted them, as it is the custom for Buddhas to greet newly arrived monks. He asked one of Upasena's students, "Do you like rag robes, monk?" "I don't like rag robes, sir." "Why then do you wear them?" "My preceptor wears them, and so I do it too." The Buddha asked Upasena how he trained his followers, and Upasena told him: "When anyone asks me for the full ordination, I tell him this: 'I stay in the wilderness, I eat only almsfood, and I wear rag robes. If you do the same, I'll give you the full ordination.' If he agrees, I ordain him. Otherwise I don't."

    "Good, Upasena, you train your followers well. But do you know the agreement made by the Sangha at Sāvatthī?" When the Buddha had told him what it was, Upasena said, "Sir, let the Sangha at Sāvatthī be known for this agreement. We, however, don't lay down new rules, nor do we get rid of the existing ones. We practice and undertake the training rules as they are." "Good, Upasena. One should not lay down new rules, nor should one get rid of the existing ones. One should practice and undertake the training rules as they are. And, Upasena, I allow those monks who stay in the wilderness, who eat only almsfood, and who wear rag robes to visit me whenever they please."

    Some monks were waiting outside the gateway to make Upasena confess an offence, but when he told them what the Buddha had said, they accepted it. And when the monks heard of the Buddha's allowance, longing to see him, they discarded their blankets and undertook the practice of staying in the wilderness, eating only almsfood, and wearing rag robes. Soon afterwards, when the Buddha and a number of monks were walking about the dwellings, he saw discarded blankets here and there. He asked, "Who owns these discarded blankets?" The monks told him. He gave a teaching and laid down a training rule "for the following ten reasons: for the well-being of the Sangha, for the comfort of the Sangha, for the restraint of bad people, for the ease of good monks, for the restraint of the corruptions relating to the present life, for the restraint of the corruptions relating to future lives, to give rise to confidence in those without it, to increase the confidence of those who have it, for the longevity of the true Teaching, and for supporting the training."1

    Synopsis — the Buddha allows monks who keep the wilderness, almsfood and rag-robe practices to visit him in his retreat; monks discard their blankets to qualify, and he sees them lying about the dwellings; he requires a new sitting blanket to incorporate a piece of an old one.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk is having a sitting blanket made, he must incorporate a piece of one standard handspan from the border of an old blanket in order to make it ugly. If he has a new sitting blanket made without incorporating a piece of one standard handspan from the border of an old blanket, he commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "When a bhikkhu is making a new sitting-blanket, or having one made, if he does not include a piece from an old blanket one standard hand-span in diameter, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "When a bhikkhu is having a felt sitting rug made, a piece of old felt a sugata span (25 cm.) on each side is to be incorporated for the sake of discoloring it. If, without incorporating a piece of old felt a sugata span on each side, a bhikkhu should have a new felt sitting rug made, it is to be forfeited and confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Having a nisīdana-santhata made without including a piece from an old santhata is a nissaggiya pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "By a bhikkhu who is having a sitting-rug made, a sugata-span from the border of an old rug is to be taken for making [it] stained. If a bhikkhu, without having taken a sugata-span from the border of an old rug, should have a new sitting rug made, [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture."6

    The renderings agree on what is required and differ over the piece's shape and its purpose. The recitation's "in diameter" makes the piece round and Thanissaro's "on each side" makes it square, where the Vibhaṅga allows either; the Pāli says only that the span is taken from the old rug sāmantā, which Ñāṇatusita reads as an adverb, "from the border", and not as a description of the span.7 The purpose clause, dubbaṇṇakaraṇāya, is Brahmāli's "to make it ugly", Thanissaro's "for the sake of discoloring it" and Ñāṇatusita's "for making [it] stained"; the rule does not depend on which, and Ajahn Brahm's line leaves it out.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule trains a monk in care for what has been given to him in faith. The monks in the Story threw their blankets away to qualify for an allowance, and the Buddha found them lying about the dwellings; his rule makes every new sitting blanket carry a piece of an old one.8 The rule gives its own reason, "in order to make it ugly", and Thanissaro reports the Commentary's second: to teach monks to make good use of old requisites, "so as to maintain the good faith of those who donated them."9​ 10 Ajahn Brahm reads the same purpose from the Story, "to use up the material in old santhata so as not be wasteful."11 The Story itself frames the rule with the Buddha's praise of Upasena for laying down no new rules for the Saṅgha and dropping none: "One should not lay down new rules, nor should one get rid of the existing ones. One should practice and undertake the training rules as they are."12 The Buddha then allowed monks who keep those practices to visit him, and it was the monks who turned that allowance into a reason to throw their blankets away; the rule that follows is given "for the following ten reasons".

    The offence has three factors, object, effort and result. Only the object, Thanissaro observes, needs explanation; the effort and result are those of NP 11.13

    Object

    A new sitting santhata, made without the piece of old felt, and intended for one's own use.14 The Vibhaṅga defines the sitting blanket as "one with a border", and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual states what that means for a monk now: a sitting cloth, made of felt material.15​ 16 A sitting cloth of ordinary woven cloth is Pc 89's and not this rule's; it is when the sitting cloth is a felt that the old piece is owed. Whether the felt sitting rug counts as a sitting cloth under Pc 89 as well is a question the Commentary answers yes and the Sub-commentary no, on Thanissaro's report, and he takes the Commentary's side, since the Vibhaṅga defines both by their border (see Pc 89).17

    The piece. It is "one standard handspan", cut round or rectangular from the border of an old blanket, and the Vibhaṅga lets him either set it in one place or pull it apart and strew it through the new felt.18 A circle a span across is the smaller of the two shapes, so the Manual takes it as the minimum, about 25 cm on Thanissaro's estimate of the standard span; what that span comes to is argued at Pc 87.19 The Vibhaṅga's own gloss on the purpose is practical: the piece is set in "to make it strong".20

    An old blanket. One "even worn once".21 The Commentary, Thanissaro reports, rewords this as sat on or lain on once, which he takes to reflect how felt was used in the commentators' day.22

    When there is none. If he cannot get a piece of a full span, a smaller one will do, and if he can get none at all, he may make the new one without.23 The rule asks a monk to use what is there, not to find what is not.

    Effort and Result

    As at NP 11: making it, or having it made, through any of the four ways of finishing; wrong conduct for the effort; and the full offence when he gets it, or finishes it if he makes it himself.24​ 25 It is relinquished by the procedure of NP 1 and given back, and, as at NP 14, he may then use it, since, Ajahn Brahm notes, in itself it is not an unallowable thing.26 Making one without the old piece for someone else is wrong conduct, but using one that someone else has made is no offence.27​ 28

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    To the exemptions Thanissaro adds one the texts do not state: it seems logical that there would be no offence in a felt blanket with no border that is not to be sat on.29

    The Buddha found the old blankets lying about the dwellings; the rule puts a piece of one into every new sitting blanket.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — for the effort of making the sitting blanket, or having it made, without the old piece, before it is got.30
    • Dukkaṭa — making one without the old piece, or having one made, for someone else.31

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he makes one incorporating a piece of one standard handspan from the border of an old blanket; if he is unable to get hold of such a piece and he makes one incorporating a smaller piece; if he is unable to get hold of a smaller piece and he makes one without; if he gets what was made by another and then uses it; if he makes a canopy, a floor cover, a cloth screen, a mattress, or a pillow; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.32

    Related Offences

    • NP 11 — the anchor of NP 11–15, where the santhata and the shared factors are set out.
    • NP 14 — the six-year rule, whose santhata, like this one, may be used once it is returned.
    • Pc 89 — the size of the sitting cloth, and its border.
    • Pc 58 — the mark on a new robe, which Ajahn Brahm reads with this rule as one principle, making a new cloth requisite unattractive.33

Thanissaro's BMC1 gives the factors, the Commentary's purpose, the Pc 89 question and the borderless felt; Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes reads the rule with Pc 58. Ajahn Brahm also gives the use of the returned santhata and its second purpose, and Ñāṇatusita sāmantā; the Commentary is cited from Thanissaro's report of it. The Concise BMC adds nothing, and the class on this rule is read but not cited.

When he gets the sitting blanket, it becomes subject to relinquishment"; relinquishment as in NP 1.↩︎

    31. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, NP 15, permutations: the full offence for the four ways of finishing, as at NP 11; "If he makes one, or has one made, for the sake of another, he commits an offense of wrong conduct."↩︎

    32. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, NP 15, non-offenses: "There is no offense: if he makes one incorporating a piece of one standard handspan from the border of an old blanket; if he is unable to get hold of such a piece and he makes one incorporating a smaller piece; if he is unable to get hold of such a smaller piece and he makes one without; if he gets what was made by another and then uses it; if he makes a canopy, a floor cover, a cloth screen, a mattress, or a pillow; if he is insane; if he is the first offender."↩︎

    33. Ajahn Brahm, Vinaya Notes, vol. 1, Nissaggiya Pācittiya 15: "When establishing this rule, the Buddha said that the piece from the old santhata should be included so as to make the new nisīdana-santhata unattractive. However, it is also clear from the Origin Story that another purpose was to use up the material in old santhata so as not be wasteful." The reading of NP 15 and Pc 58 as one principle is his at Pc 58 (see that rule).↩︎
