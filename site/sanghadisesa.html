    Saṅghādisesa

    The thirteen saṅghādisesa rules and the four pārājikas together make up the garukāpatti, the "heavy offences". Thanissaro gives two reasons for the name: the offences are serious in themselves, and the procedures of penance, probation and rehabilitation are "burdensome by design, not only for the offender but also for the Community of bhikkhus in which he lives", which is meant as a further deterrent to anyone tempted to transgress.1 Every class from the nissaggiya pācittiyas on is lahukāpatti, the "light offences", cleared by confession.2

    A pārājika ends a monk's status outright, with no way back in this life. A saṅghādisesa can be recovered from, but only through a demanding formal process, and not by confessing and moving on as a light offence is cleared. The weight of that process is part of what makes the offence heavy, and a monk should know what it asks of him before he reads the thirteen rules one at a time.

    The Two-Part Structure: Concealment and Rehabilitation

    A bhikkhu who commits a saṅghādisesa offence is bound to inform a fellow bhikkhu before the next dawn.3 What happens next depends on whether he does.

    If he reports it in time, he asks a Community of at least four bhikkhus to impose a six-day period of penance, mānatta, on him.4 Penance begins at the convenience of the Community that gives it, not at once.5 For the six days he is partly stripped of seniority and must observe 94 restrictions, which BMC2 sets out in detail. The four most important are:6

    1. He must not live under the same roof as a regular bhikkhu, one who has not himself committed a saṅghādisesa offence.
    2. He must live in a monastery with at least four regular bhikkhus.
    3. If he goes anywhere outside the monastery, four full-fledged bhikkhus must go with him, unless he is escaping dangers or going to a place he can reach within a day where there are regular bhikkhus of the same affiliation.
    4. Every day he must tell all the bhikkhus in the monastery that he is observing penance, and the precise offence for which it was imposed. He must tell visiting bhikkhus too, and the bhikkhus of any other monastery he goes to.

    If on any day he neglects one of these four, that day does not count toward the six, and he incurs a dukkaṭa each time he fails to observe any of the 94.7

    If he conceals the offence past the deadline, the way back is longer. He must first undergo probation, parivāsa, for as many days as he concealed the offence, under restrictions similar to those of penance. Only when probation is complete may he ask for the six days of mānatta, so concealment adds to the process and replaces no part of it.8

    What Counts as Concealment

    The line between concealment and innocent delay matters to a bhikkhu trying to act in good faith. The Commentary to Cv.III, as Thanissaro reports it, sets the factors of concealment at ten, arranged in five pairs, each pairing a fact about the situation with the bhikkhu's perception of that fact:9

    1. He has committed a saṅghādisesa offence and perceives it as an offence. The factor is met even if he thinks it a lesser offence.
    2. He has not been suspended and perceives that he has not. A suspended bhikkhu is in affiliation with no bhikkhus, and so cannot undergo the procedure at all.
    3. There is no obstacle, such as a flood, a forest fire or dangerous animals, and he perceives that there is none.
    4. He is able to inform another bhikkhu and perceives that he is able. A bhikkhu suitable to be informed is of common affiliation, in good standing (for example, not himself undergoing penance, probation or suspension) and not on uncongenial terms with the offender. The Cullavagga counts going insane after the offence as being unable to inform anyone.10
    5. He wants to conceal the offence, and so conceals it.

    Affiliation (saṁvāsa) is the line these conditions draw. Bhikkhus of common affiliation hold their uposatha and Invitation together; bhikkhus of separate affiliation (nānāsaṁvāsa) do not. The Canon gives two ways into separate affiliation, being suspended by a united Community or making oneself separate, without saying what the second means (Mv.X.1.10). Thanissaro reads it first as siding with a suspended bhikkhu, the route of the Abhayagiri split (see "Commentaries"), and finds two more in history. A group that cannot settle a dispute may leave the territory and found a Community elsewhere, which is no schism, since the two no longer carry out transactions in one territory: so the Jetavana bhikkhus left the Abhayagiri in the fourth century CE. Or bhikkhus who doubt their own ordination lineage may seek reordination elsewhere and keep themselves apart, as the nineteenth-century reform sects of Sri Lanka and Thailand did. Bhikkhus of separate affiliation may sit in on most of each other's transactions without being counted, but may not join each other's uposatha or Invitation. Once the dispute that divided them is settled, they can become of common affiliation again.11

    If any factor is lacking, there is no penalty for not informing another bhikkhu that day. Thanissaro gives four cases of non-disclosure that are not concealment:12

    • A bhikkhu does not suspect that he has committed an offence, and realises only much later, after reading or hearing about the rules in more detail, that it was a saṅghādisesa.
    • A bhikkhu living alone in a forest commits the offence in the middle of the night. Afraid of snakes or other wild animals in the dark, he waits until daylight to go and inform a fellow bhikkhu.
    • A bhikkhu living alone in a forest finds that the only other bhikkhu within a day's travel is a personal enemy who would use the disclosure to smear his name, so he travels a further day or two to a congenial bhikkhu.
    • A bhikkhu means to inform someone before dawn but falls asleep, and either wakes too late or wakes in time but remembers the offence only after dawn.

    Once the first four pairs, eight factors in all, are present, the deadline holds: a bhikkhu who does not inform another bhikkhu before the next dawn incurs a dukkaṭa and undergoes the penalty for concealment.13 A bhikkhu who conceals what he takes to be a saṅghādisesa, when it is in fact a lesser offence, still incurs a dukkaṭa for concealing it.14

    Where a bhikkhu cannot reach another bhikkhu in person before dawn, Brahmāli holds that a telephone call to the monastery is enough.15 Ajahn Brahm gives the same advice in an older form, for a solitary bhikkhu who must travel several days to reach the nearest bhikkhu: "in such a case these days, it would be advisable to send a letter by post as soon as possible to inform another bhikkhu."16 What the rule asks for is timely disclosure to a fellow bhikkhu, by whatever means are at hand.

    Final Rehabilitation: Abbhāna

    Once mānatta, and any probation before it, is complete, the bhikkhu may ask a Community of at least twenty bhikkhus to grant him rehabilitation, abbhāna.17 The recitation itself states what happens when the number falls short: "If there are fewer than twenty bhikkhus in the saṅgha, that bhikkhu is not rehabilitated and those bhikkhus are at fault."18 Twenty bhikkhus in one place can be hard to find, and the wait for rehabilitation, in Thanissaro's words, "can be especially long in an area where the twenty bhikkhus needed for the quorum are hard to find."19 Brahmāli recalls a saṅghādisesa brought to Bodhinyana Monastery by a visiting monk, for which bhikkhus had to be brought in from the local Sri Lankan and Burmese vihāras to make up the twenty.20 The twenty are needed for the rehabilitation alone; the penance itself is imposed by a Community of four, as above. Once rehabilitated, the bhikkhu returns to his previous state as a regular bhikkhu in good standing.21

    A bhikkhu may also have more than one saṅghādisesa to make amends for, or commit a further one while undergoing penance or probation for the first. BMC2 sets out how several offences are combined into one penance or one probation, and the transactions for "sending back to the beginning" a bhikkhu who commits a further offence along the way. It is a complicated part of the procedure, and a monk need only know that it exists until he needs it.22

    One practice shows what penance is for. The Canon allows a bhikkhu to set his penance aside when many bhikkhus come to stay, since he could not notify them all each day, and to take it up again when they have gone.23 The Commentary goes further: it describes a bhikkhu taking up his penance near dawn, notifying a few bhikkhus at a spot outside the monastery, and setting the penance aside again at dawnrise, so that for the rest of the day he is a regular bhikkhu. Thanissaro judges that these recommendations "seem aimed at getting around many of the designed difficulties of penance simply on the basis of a technicality", and advises a bhikkhu in a busy monastery to find a smaller one where he can observe his penance in full.24 Brahmāli gives the reason in class. Mānatta is "supposed to be really embarrassing, that's the whole point". The embarrassment is mental, not physical, and it makes a monk think about what he has done; a monk who works his way out of it makes the procedure pointless.25

    A Note on Confession Generally

    The saṅghādisesa's deadline stands out against the way the light offences are cleared. They are confessed to a bhikkhu of the same affiliation who is not himself guilty, unconfessed, of the same offence, and the formula is given in the Pācittiya overview.26 Because a bhikkhu with an unconfessed offence may not listen to the Pātimokkha, the tradition has grown up of confessing just before the recitation.27 A bhikkhu who knows he has an unconfessed offence while the Pātimokkha is being recited must tell a neighbouring bhikkhu when the recitation reaches the rule, and promise to confess it afterwards; if he tells no one, he incurs a dukkaṭa.28

    The formula names the offence: "I have fallen into an offense of such-and-such a name." The form now most used in Thailand gathers many offences "of various sorts" into one statement, which also covers offences committed unknowingly. Because every bhikkhu repeats it before the recitation, Thanissaro observes, "the procedure has become little more than a formality", and he reports the Vinayamukha's recommendation that a bhikkhu who knows of a particular offence first mention it to the other bhikkhu in their own language.29 The confession's second half is the undertaking of restraint in the future.
