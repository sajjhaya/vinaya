    Pc 45

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. Venerable Upananda the Sakyan went to the house of a friend and sat down in private alone with his wife.

    The husband complained and criticised him, "How could Venerable Upananda sit down in private alone with my wife?"

    The monks heard the complaints of that man. The monks of few desires complained and criticised Upananda, and the Buddha questioned him, established that it was true, rebuked him, and stated the rule.1​ 2

    Synopsis — The story is the previous rule's with one phrase changed: the seat is no longer concealed and the monk is alone with the woman rather than screened with her. It is the fourth of five consecutive rules whose offender is Upananda.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk sits down in private alone with a woman, he commits an offense entailing confession."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu, aiming at privacy, sits alone in private with a woman, he incurs a pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu sit in private, alone with a woman, it is to be confessed."5

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Sitting or lying down in a private place alone with a woman is an offence of Pācittiya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should take seat together a woman, one [man] with one [woman], privately, [this is a case] involving expiation."7

    The renderings agree, and this is a lean mirror of Pc 44, whose translation discussion covers the shared vocabulary. One point is this rule's own. Every rendering but Ñāṇatusita's says "alone", which is accurate and hides the consequence. The Pāli is eko ekāya, and his "one [man] with one [woman]" keeps its shape but still not its consequence; the Vibhaṅga glosses it "just the monk and the woman".8​ 9 What the phrase excludes is any third person at all, so the person whose presence exempts may be a man or a woman, where at Pc 44 it must be a man. No rule line carries this, and each source restores it in its commentary; it is taken up under Effort.10​ 11​ 12

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    Open ground does not make a monk's company with a woman any less private when no one can see or hear them, and this rule carries Pc 44's protection of his celibate life, and of the laity's confidence in him, out from behind the screen. The husband's complaint is the previous rule's with one phrase changed: "How could Venerable Upananda sit down in private alone with my wife?"13

    This rule is the second of the pair, and most of it is Pc 44's. Sitting, lying and who arrives second, privacy to the eye and the ear, the perception grid and the lesser objects, the standing and preoccupied exemptions, and the whole question of what "aiming at privacy" means are word for word the same.14 What follows is only what differs: the woman, the place and the companion.

    Object

    The woman must here be old enough to understand. The word-analysis adds a sentence Pc 44's lacks: she can tell good speech from bad, the decent from the indecent.15 Pc 44's definition reaches a newborn and this one does not, or, as Ajahn Brahm puts it, she is "one old enough to understand flirtatious speech".16 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual does not carry the narrowing: it refers the word to Sg 2, whose definition is the wide one, and so in effect imports Pc 44's object here. His own Vibhaṅga translation is the firmer ground, and the narrowing rests on it.17

    Effort

    The place is private but not concealed. Pc 44 requires a seat screened by a wall, a door or the like; this rule requires only that no one can see or hear the two of them.18 Thanissaro's examples are a bench in a deserted park, a glassed-in porch and an open-air pavilion, and he takes the line the two aniyatas suggest: a place convenient for intercourse falls under Pc 44, and one that is not falls here.19 Ajahn Brahm adds a modern case, a car parked in an empty street.20

    On the companion, the sources set this rule apart from Pc 44. The Vibhaṅga prints the same non-offence clause as Pc 44, a male companion who understands, and every source that addresses it reads it more widely here.21 The Manual argues from the rule's own eko ekāya, which the Vibhaṅga defines as just the monk and the woman, that another woman is enough.22 Thanissaro reports the Commentary saying so explicitly, and he gives the reason that makes it strong: the clauses are "strangely enough" identical, but otherwise there would be no reason for two separate rules on the same subject.23​ 24 Ajahn Brahm and the Concise BMC say the same: a man or a woman will do.25​ 26 The conditions on that person are Pc 44's, as Thanissaro reports the Commentary: he or she must know what is properly and improperly said, be awake, be neither deaf nor blind, and be within sight, about six metres, though one distracted or drowsy still counts.27

    Intention

    As at Pc 44. Ajahn Brahm gives the case of a monk sitting alone on a bench in an empty garden when a woman comes and sits beside him. There is no offence, since he did not seek it, "though one should get up and leave."28

    A bench is also where a monk may reach for the wrong rule. Sharing a long seat with a woman is forbidden by a Khandhaka rule, not by this one, and Ajahn Brahm states it separately.29 Brahmāli kept the two apart in class and carried the bench rule to a modern case: the seats of an aircraft are separate seats, not a bench, even with a woman in the next one.30 For the pair as a whole he offered a simpler rule than any of their details, to "always have a man around", which leaves nothing to remember.31

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — sitting in private alone with a paṇḍaka, a female spirit, a female ghost, or a female animal in the form of a woman.32
    • Dukkaṭa — where the person is not a woman but he perceives her as one, or is unsure of it. Where the person is a woman, all three perceptions carry the full pācittiya, as at Pc 44.33

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he has a companion who understands, a man or a woman, for the reason given above; if he stands and does not sit down; if he is not seeking privacy; if he sits down preoccupied with something else; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.34​ 35

    The first clause is given as every source reads it and not as the Vibhaṅga prints it, which says "a male companion who understands", as at Pc 44.

    Related Offences

    • Pc 44 — the anchor of the pair, where the seat is concealed and the companion must be a man; everything not set out above is there.
    • Aniyata 1, 2 — the anchors for raho and the two kinds of seat, and the source of the test that divides this rule from Pc 44.
    • Sg 2 — where the Manual sends this rule's reader for mātugāma; its definition is Pc 44's wide one, not this rule's.
    • Kd 16:13.2 — the Khandhaka rule against sharing a long seat with a woman, which a monk may reach for here and which is not this one.36
    • Pc 7, Pc 30, Pc 67 — the other rules about a monk and a woman unobserved.

    Related Suttas

    • SN 35.127 — the reflection on women as mothers, sisters and daughters (see Pc 44).37
    • SN 47.6 — the quail that strays outside its own resort (see Pc 44).38

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; it is Pc 44's story with one phrase changed, given in full because a reader should not have to assemble a Story from another page. I treat this rule as a lean mirror of Pc 44, carrying only what differs. Brahmāli's recitation, the Manual and the class transcript (session 027, 7 June 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1, Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were checked; two class uses are kept, the aircraft seat and the simple rule for the pair, and the rest of the class's points are carried by the written sources. The Commentary is known here only through Thanissaro and the Concise BMC.
