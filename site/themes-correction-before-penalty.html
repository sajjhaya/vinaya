    Correction before Penalty

    When the Buddha told Sāriputta and Moggallāna to go to Kīṭāgiri and banish Assaji and Punabbasuka, whose corrupting of its families is told in The Monk's Place in Lay Affairs, the two chief disciples hesitated: "Sir, how can we do a procedure of banishment against these monks? They're temperamental and harsh." The Buddha's answer was practical: "Well then, take many monks." The banishment was done, and the two monks neither mended their ways nor asked for forgiveness. "Instead they abused and reviled them, and they slandered them as acting from favoritism, ill will, confusion, and fear."1 The rule laid down then, Sg 13, does not make corrupting families a saṅghādisesa. The offence it names is the refusal to stop slandering the banishment, and it falls only after the monk has been corrected, first by the monks and then by the Saṅgha.

    Sg 13 shares that shape with the three rules before it and with one Pācittiya. Sg 10 meets a monk who pursues schism, Sg 11 the monks who back him, Sg 12 a monk who will not be corrected, and Pc 68 a monk who holds that the obstacles do not obstruct. Brahmāli's recitation of Sg 10 gives the shape in two sentences: the monk "should be admonished up to three times to make him stop. If he does not stop, he incurs a saṅghādisesa."2 The Vibhaṅga gives the stages. In each rule the monks who see or hear of it go to him first, and the rule gives them their words. "And they should correct him a second and a third time. If he stops, all is well." If he does not, he commits an offence of wrong conduct and is brought before the Saṅgha, "even if he has to be pulled into the Sangha", corrected there three times more, and only then pressed by a motion and three announcements. He commits a further offence of wrong conduct after the motion and a serious offence after each of the first two announcements, and the saṅghādisesa falls only when the third is finished; it annuls the lighter offences on the way.3

    The non-offence clause adds the two cases that matter most: "if he has not been pressed; if he stops".4 Pc 68 runs the same course to a lighter penalty, with wrong conduct at each step: "If he gives up that view, all is well."5

    The deed that opened each case is told elsewhere. Devadatta's five points and his four supporters, Channa's "Who are you to correct me?" and Ariṭṭha's view are told in Characters of the Vinaya, and in every one of them the rule waits until the monk has been told and has refused. Thanissaro suggests what the waiting is for. Under Sg 10 he writes that this "may have been the original intention behind the protocols outlined in this and the remaining three saṅghādisesa rules: to give the Community a clear opportunity to test how stubborn a divisive or recalcitrant bhikkhu is and to end his affiliation with them if he proves this stubborn."6 On that reading the penalty falls on stubbornness the Saṅgha has proved, never on stubbornness it suspects, and every stage of the proof is a chance to stop.

    Sg 12 gives the reason in the words the monks are to say: "Be easy to correct, venerable, not incorrigible. And please give legitimate correction to the monks, and the monks will do the same to you. For it's in this way that the Buddha's community has grown, that is, through mutual correction and mutual clearing of offenses."7 Correction runs both ways, and the suttas ask as much of the monk who gives it as of the monk who receives it. Sāriputta tells the monks that one who wishes to reprove another "should first establish five things in himself": "I will speak at a proper time, not at an improper time; I will speak truthfully, not falsely; I will speak gently, not harshly; I will speak in a beneficial way, not in a harmful way; I will speak with a mind of loving-kindness, not while harboring hatred." The one reproved, for his part, "should be established in two things: in truth and non-anger."8 The Vibhaṅga's procedure is that teaching given a form: the monks speak first, privately and three times, and the Saṅgha speaks only when they have failed.

    Set side by side, these rules show how far the Pātimokkha goes before it imposes a penalty it could impose at once. Four of the thirteen saṅghādisesas, and one pācittiya, are reached only through the monks' own correction and then the Saṅgha's motion and announcements. The monk may stop at any point, and if he stops when the monks first speak to him, he has no offence at all. Sāriputta and Moggallāna went to Kīṭāgiri to banish two monks who would not listen, and the rule laid down afterwards gives every monk after them a chance at each stage to listen before the penalty falls. The words a monk can take from these rules are the Vibhaṅga's own, spoken between the private correction and the formal one: "If he stops, all is well."

    Rules — Sg 10, Sg 11, Sg 12, Sg 13, Pc 68

    1. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Sg 13, origin story: "Sir, how can we do a procedure of banishment against these monks? They're temperamental and harsh." "Well then, take many monks."; "Even so, they did not conduct themselves properly or suitably so as to deserve to be released, nor did they ask the monks for forgiveness. Instead they abused and reviled them, and they slandered them as acting from favoritism, ill will, confusion, and fear."↩︎

    2. Brahmāli, Pātimokkha recitation translation (May 2025), Saṅghādisesa 10: "If a bhikkhu is trying to cause a schism in the Saṅgha, he should be admonished up to three times to make him stop. If he does not stop, he incurs a saṅghādisesa."↩︎

    3. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Sg 10, definitions: "They should correct him like this"; "And they should correct him a second and a third time. If he stops, all is well"; "If he does not stop, he commits an offense of wrong conduct"; "That monk, even if he has to be pulled into the Sangha, should be corrected like this"; "After each of the first two announcements, he commits a serious offense"; "For one who commits the offense entailing suspension, the offense of wrong conduct and the serious offenses are annulled."↩︎

    4. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Sg 10, non-offenses: "There is no offense: if he has not been pressed; if he stops; if he is insane; if he is deranged; if he is overwhelmed by pain; if he is the first offender."↩︎

    5. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 68, definitions: "If he gives up that view, all is well"; "That monk, even if he has to be pulled into the Sangha, should be corrected like this".↩︎

    6. Thanissaro, BMC1, Sg 10, Further steps: "In fact, this may have been the original intention behind the protocols outlined in this and the remaining three saṅghādisesa rules: to give the Community a clear opportunity to test how stubborn a divisive or recalcitrant bhikkhu is and to end his affiliation with them if he proves this stubborn."↩︎

    7. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Sg 12, final ruling: "Be easy to correct, venerable, not incorrigible. And please give legitimate correction to the monks, and the monks will do the same to you. For it's in this way that the Buddha's community has grown, that is, through mutual correction and mutual clearing of offenses."↩︎

    8. AN 5.167, Reproving (AN III 196–200), Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation: "Friends, a bhikkhu who wishes to reprove another should first establish five things in himself"; "I will speak at a proper time, not at an improper time; (2) I will speak truthfully, not falsely; (3) I will speak gently, not harshly; (4) I will speak in a beneficial way, not in a harmful way; (5) I will speak with a mind of loving-kindness, not while harboring hatred"; "Friends, a person who is reproved should be established in two things: in truth and non-anger."↩︎
