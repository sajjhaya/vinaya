    The Group of Six and the Group of Seventeen

    The group of six appear in the origin stories of 57 of the rules in this book, more often than anyone else, and in most of them they appear only as the monks who did it. Brahmāli calls them "an empty vessel into which all sorts of wrong conduct was projected": real monks, no doubt, but used, at least in some places, to supply a perpetrator when a rule needed one.1 Read one at a time, their stories tell a reader little about them. Read beside the stories of another group, the seventeen, they tell a good deal.

    The seventeen first appear in an origin story at Pc 15, and the Vibhaṅga introduces them by their friendship: "the monks from the group of seventeen were friends", who lived together and left together. On one of those departures they left bedding out in a dwelling of the Saṅgha, and termites ate the furniture.2 Their other faults are of the same kind. At Pc 37 they went to a hilltop fair in Rājagaha, were bathed and fed by the people there, brought the food back and offered it to the group of six, who asked where it came from and then reported them for eating at the wrong time.3 At Pc 53 King Pasenadi watched them from his stilt house playing in the river Aciravatī, and Queen Mallikā said, "Great king, no doubt the Buddha hasn't laid down a rule. Either that, or these monks are ignorant."4

    Pc 65 may tell where they came from. "At that time in Rājagaha there was a group of seventeen boys who were friends, with Upāli as their leader." Upāli's parents decided that the Sakyan monastics "have pleasant habits and a happy life", the boys went forth together, and soon afterwards they were up before dawn crying, "Give us congee, give us a meal, give us fresh food."5 The Vibhaṅga does not say these are the same seventeen. But at Pc 77 the group of six frighten the seventeen with this very charge: "you were less than twenty when you got the full ordination. Could it be that you're not fully ordained?"6 It seems reasonable to read the two groups of seventeen as one, and the monks of the other stories as the boys of this one.

    The group of six are consistent in how they treat them. At Pc 17 they waited until the seventeen had finished repairing a large dwelling for the rains, and then told them to leave. "The dwelling is large. You can stay here and so can we." "Leave, this dwelling is ours," and they took them by the neck and threw them out.7 At Pc 52 they tickled one of the seventeen until he could not catch his breath, and he died. At Pc 55 they frightened them, at Pc 60 they hid their bowls and robes, at Pc 74 they struck them in anger, and at Pc 75 they raised their hands as if to strike.8 The stories are short, and in nearly every one of them the seventeen cry.9 At Pc 60 the Vibhaṅga sets the two groups side by side: "The monks from the group of six laughed, but the monks from the group of seventeen cried."10

    The two threads, then, show whom a good part of the Pācittiyas on how monks treat one another were laid down to protect. The faults of the seventeen are a young person's faults: carelessness with the Saṅgha's things, food, play. The faults committed against them are a bully's. The rules against throwing a monk out, frightening him, hitting him, raising a hand to him, hiding his things and making him anxious were laid down, in these stories, because the same monks did those things to the same younger ones, and they make the monastery safe for the monk who has least standing in it. The reason given at Pc 65 for the age of twenty points the same way. One under twenty, the Buddha says, "is unable to endure cold and heat; hunger and thirst … and rude and unwelcome speech."11 The seventeen were the monks least able to bear what the six gave them. Not every story runs that way: in a case study at Pj 3, the seventeen overpower one of the six, intending a legal procedure against him, and he dies.

    If the group of six are partly a device, the device is used with care. It puts the rules against cruelty in the hands of the same perpetrators and gives them the same victims, and it asks the reader to stand with the monks who cried.
