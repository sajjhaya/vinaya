    Harm Graded by the Mind

    Among the case studies of Pj 3 is that of a father and son who had gone forth together. When the time was announced for a gathering, the son said, "Go, sir, the Sangha is waiting for you," took his father by the back and pushed him, and the father fell and died. The son went to the Buddha and was asked, "What were you thinking?" He said, "I didn't mean to kill him, sir," and the Buddha said, "There's no offense for one who isn't aiming at death." The Vibhaṅga then tells the story twice more, and each time the son pushes his father "aiming to kill him". In the second telling the father dies, and the son has committed an offence entailing expulsion. In the third the father falls and lives, and it is a serious offence.1 The words, the push and the fall are the same in all three. The verdicts run from no offence to the heaviest there is, and they follow what the son meant and what came of it.

    The Vibhaṅga sets the same pair beside it at Āḷavī, where the monks were preparing a site for a dwelling and one lifted a stone up to another above him. In the first case the stone slipped from the upper monk's grip and killed the monk below: "There's no offense when it's unintentional." In the second the upper monk dropped it on his head "aiming to kill him".2 At a dining hall a pestle fell and killed a boy, and the monk, who had reached for a pestle when another fell, said, "I didn't intend it, sir."3 Where the Vibhaṅga records the Buddha's question in these cases, it is the question he put to the son, "What were you thinking?", and the answer decides the case.

    What the mind is aimed at also sets which offence falls, and the Vibhaṅga shows it with a pit. A monk who digs a pit thinking, "Whatever falls into it, will die," commits wrong conduct for the digging. If a person falls in and dies, the offence entails expulsion; if a spirit dies, it is a serious offence; if an animal dies, it entails confession.4 The last of these is Pc 61, the rule against killing animals, which reaches a monk who "intentionally kills a living being" and exempts him "if it is unintentional; if he is not mindful; if he does not know; if he is not aiming at death".5 Pc 20 and Pc 62, on pouring out or using water with living beings in it, make it an offence entailing confession only for the monk who "knows" that the water contains them.6

    The aim is not the same as the motive. In the first of Pj 3's case studies the monks praised death to a sick monk "out of compassion", and he died. They had aimed at his death, and the Buddha told them, "You've committed an offense entailing expulsion." Nor is not aiming the same as not caring. An alms-collecting monk sat down on a bench and crushed a boy hidden under an old cloth. There was no offence entailing expulsion, "But you shouldn't sit down on a seat without checking it. If you do, you commit an offense of wrong conduct."7 The kind wish did not lighten the first verdict, and the innocent mind did not excuse the carelessness in the second.

    The permutations that follow many rules sort the cases by what the monk believed. At Pc 10, on digging the earth, a monk who digs earth taking it for earth commits the offence; if he is unsure, it is wrong conduct; if he takes it for something else, there is no offence. And if what he digs is not earth but he takes it for earth, he still commits wrong conduct.8 In that last case nothing has been harmed at all. The offence falls on a mind that was willing to do what the rule forbids. The table runs the other way too: an act that breaks the rule, done by a monk who did not know, is no offence.

    Even at the edge of the will the Vibhaṅga weighs it. At Sg 1 monks who had an emission in a dream worried that they had committed an offence entailing suspension, since a dream "is not without intention". The Buddha agreed that it is not, and said, "but it's negligible," and the rule was restated: "Intentional emission of semen, except while dreaming, is an offense entailing suspension."9

    One case at a time, these are verdicts. Laid side by side, they show the Vibhaṅga grading every harm along two lines at once: what happened, and what the monk meant. The heaviest offence needs both, the act done and the death aimed at. Where one is missing the offence falls by a grade, or to wrong conduct, or away altogether. This is, it seems fair to say, the Vinaya applying in its own measures what the Buddha taught in AN 6.63: "It is volition, bhikkhus, that I call kamma. For having willed, one acts by body, speech, or mind."10 Asked what he was thinking, the son had an answer, and the answer decided his case.

    Rules — Pj 3, Sg 1, Pc 10, Pc 20, Pc 61, Pc 62

    1. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pj 3, case studies: "Go, sir, the Sangha is waiting for you"; "What were you thinking?" "I didn't mean to kill him, sir." "There's no offense for one who isn't aiming at death"; "and seizing him by the back, he pushed him, aiming to kill him"; "You have committed an offense entailing expulsion"; "There's no offense entailing expulsion, but there's a serious offense."↩︎

    2. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pj 3, case studies: "Because the other monk did not grasp it properly, it fell on the head of the monk below, who died"; "There's no offense when it's unintentional"; "The other monk dropped the stone on his head, aiming to kill him."↩︎

    3. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pj 3, case studies: "When he took hold of a pestle high up, another pestle fell down, hitting a boy, who died"; "I didn't intend it, sir." "There's no offense when it's unintentional."↩︎

    4. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pj 3, permutations, A pit: "If a monk digs a non-specific pit, thinking, 'Whatever falls into it, will die,' he commits an offense of wrong conduct"; for a person, "If they die, the monk commits an offense entailing expulsion"; for "a spirit, ghost, or animal in human form", "If it dies, the monk commits a serious offense"; for an animal, "If it dies, the monk commits an offense entailing confession."↩︎

    5. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 61, final ruling: "If a monk intentionally kills a living being, he commits an offense entailing confession"; non-offenses: "There is no offense: if it is unintentional; if he is not mindful; if he does not know; if he is not aiming at death; if he is insane; if he is the first offender."↩︎

    6. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 20, final ruling: "If a monk pours water that he knows contains living beings onto grass or clay, or has it poured, he commits an offense entailing confession"; Pc 62, final ruling: "If a monk uses water that he knows contains living beings, he commits an offense entailing confession."↩︎

    7. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pj 3, case studies: "Out of compassion, the monks praised death to him. He died"; "You've committed an offense entailing expulsion"; "On one occasion an alms-collecting monk sat down on a bench, crushing a boy who was concealed by an old cloth"; "There's no offense entailing expulsion. But you shouldn't sit down on a seat without checking it. If you do, you commit an offense of wrong conduct."↩︎

    8. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 10, permutations: "If it is earth, but he is unsure of it, and he digs it or has it dug, … he commits an offense of wrong conduct"; "If it is earth, but he does not perceive it as such, … there is no offense"; "If it is not earth, but he perceives it as such, he commits an offense of wrong conduct."↩︎

    9. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Sg 1, origin story: "We had an emission while dreaming, which is not without intention"; "It's true, monks, that a dream is not without intention, but it's negligible"; final ruling: "Intentional emission of semen, except while dreaming, is an offense entailing suspension."↩︎

    10. AN 6.63, Penetrative (AN III 415), Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation: "It is volition, bhikkhus, that I call kamma. For having willed, one acts by body, speech, or mind."↩︎
