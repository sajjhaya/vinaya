    Ariṭṭha and Kaṇṭaka

    Three rules in a row are about one sentence. "As I understand the Teaching of the Buddha, the things he calls obstacles are unable to obstruct one who indulges in them."1 At Pc 68 it is said by the monk Ariṭṭha, "the ex-vulture-hunter"; at Pc 70 by the novice Kaṇṭaka, word for word. The Vibhaṅga does not say where the novice found it. Between them stands Pc 69, which is about neither man's view but about the company Ariṭṭha kept.

    The monks who heard Ariṭṭha's view went to him first. They did not report him; they asked him whether it was true, and when he said it was, they argued with him from the Teaching: "No, Ariṭṭha, don't misrepresent the Buddha, for it's not good to misrepresent him. The Buddha would never say such a thing." They gave him the ten similes for worldly pleasures, the skeleton, the piece of meat, the grass torch and the rest, down to the snake's head. "But even though the monks corrected Ariṭṭha like this, he stubbornly held on to that bad and erroneous view."2 Only then did they go to the Buddha, who questioned him before the Saṅgha and gave him the same similes again: "Foolish man, who do you think I have taught like this? … by misunderstanding you have misrepresented me, hurt yourself, and made much demerit."3 The rule repeats the monks' correction in its own wording, so that the reply to the view is part of the rule against it. Thanissaro reports the Commentary's view that the rule most commonly meets a monk who believes "there is nothing wrong in a bhikkhu's having sexual intercourse in defiance of Pr 1"; the Vibhaṅga itself gives the view only in Ariṭṭha's words.4

    The Middle Length Discourses tell the same meeting at greater length. Asked whether Ariṭṭha "kindled even a spark of wisdom in this Dhamma and Discipline", the monks say he has not, and Ariṭṭha "sat silent, dismayed, with shoulders drooping and head down, glum, and without response". The sutta then gives the simile of a man who catches a snake by its coils or its tail, so that it turns and bites him: "Why is that? Because of his wrong grasp of the snake."5 The Khandhakas give the end. The Saṅgha did "a legal procedure of ejection against the monk Ariṭṭha for not giving up that bad view", and "He then disrobed."6

    Pc 69 is about the monks who stayed with him. The group of six "lived with the monk Ariṭṭha, and they did formal meetings and shared a sleeping place with him", knowing that he had not given up the view.7 Pc 70 follows the same course with the novice. Kaṇṭaka was corrected by the monks as Ariṭṭha had been and held to the view as Ariṭṭha had; he was questioned and rebuked in the same words, and the Buddha had him expelled: "From today, Kaṇṭaka, you may not refer to the Buddha as your teacher. … Go! Away with you!" And the group of six "befriended Kaṇṭaka, and they were attended on by him, lived with him, and shared a sleeping place with him. Yet they knew that he had been expelled."8

    Between them, the three rules show how the Saṅgha meets a wrong view, and in what order. First the monks go to the man and reason with him from the Teaching. Then the Saṅgha presses him formally, and the words it uses are the rule's own. Only when the view is held against all of that does the Saṅgha set him apart, and Pc 69 and Pc 70 then hold the setting apart in place, by making the monks who keep the man company answerable for it too. The group of six were not accused of the view. Their fault was to make the separation empty. One may reasonably read in this that a view is kept alive by the company it keeps, and that the Vinaya treats the one who gives it shelter as part of the problem.

    The sentence itself begins with a claim of understanding, and the sutta puts the fault there. Ariṭṭha did not set out to contradict the Buddha. He thought he had understood him, and in thinking so he took hold of the teaching by the end that bites. The rule asks a monk to hear his own reading of the Teaching, when it makes the obstacles harmless, as the sign that he is holding the snake by the tail: "Because of his wrong grasp of the snake."

    Rules — Pc 68, Pc 69, Pc 70

    1. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 68, origin story: "At that time the monk Ariṭṭha, the ex-vulture-hunter, had the following bad and erroneous view: 'As I understand the Teaching of the Buddha, the things he calls obstacles are unable to obstruct one who indulges in them.'"; Pc 70, origin story: "At that time the novice monastic Kaṇṭaka had the following bad and erroneous view".↩︎

    2. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 68, origin story: "Is it true, Ariṭṭha, that you have such a view?"; "But even though the monks corrected Ariṭṭha like this, he stubbornly held on to that bad and erroneous view, and continued to insist on it."↩︎

    3. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 68, origin story: "Foolish man, who do you think I have taught like this?"; "And yet, foolish man, by misunderstanding you have misrepresented me, hurt yourself, and made much demerit."↩︎

    4. Thanissaro, BMC1, Pc 68: "The Commentary notes that this training rule deals with a bhikkhu who holds to the view that the fifth category is not an obstacle, the most common example being the bhikkhu who believes that there is nothing wrong in a bhikkhu's having sexual intercourse in defiance of Pr 1."↩︎

    5. MN 22, Alagaddūpama (MN I 130–134), Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation: "Has this bhikkhu Ariṭṭha, formerly of the vulture killers, kindled even a spark of wisdom in this Dhamma and Discipline?"; "sat silent, dismayed, with shoulders drooping and head down, glum, and without response"; "saw a large snake and grasped its coils or its tail. It would turn back on him and bite his hand or his arm or one of his limbs … Why is that? Because of his wrong grasp of the snake."↩︎

    6. Kd 11:32 and 11:34, Brahmāli's translation: "Soon afterwards the Sangha did a legal procedure of ejection against the monk Ariṭṭha for not giving up that bad view, prohibiting him from living with the Sangha. He then disrobed."↩︎

    7. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 69, origin story: "At that time the monks from the group of six lived with the monk Ariṭṭha, and they did formal meetings and shared a sleeping place with him. Yet they knew that he was saying such things, that he had not made amends according to the rule, and that he had not given up that view."; definitions: "Who has not made amends according to the rule: who has been ejected and not readmitted." The origin story does not say when, in relation to the ejection, the six lived with him.↩︎

    8. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 70, origin story: "From today, Kaṇṭaka, you may not refer to the Buddha as your teacher. And, unlike other novices, you can no longer share a sleeping place with the monks for two or three nights. Go! Away with you!"; "Soon afterwards the monks from the group of six befriended Kaṇṭaka, and they were attended on by him, lived with him, and shared a sleeping place with him. Yet they knew that he had been expelled."↩︎
