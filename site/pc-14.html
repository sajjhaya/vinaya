    Pc 14

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī, in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. During the winter the monks put furniture out in the open to warm themselves in the sun. When the time for departure was announced, they departed without putting it away, having it put away, or informing anyone, and the furniture was rained on.

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised them: "How could those monks put furniture out in the open and then depart without putting it away, having it put away, or informing anyone? The furniture was rained on." They told the Buddha, who questioned the monks, confirmed that it was so, and laid down the training rule.

    Soon afterwards, monks who had been out in the open brought the furniture back inside even though it was not the rainy season. The Buddha saw this, and after giving a teaching he addressed the monks: "Monks, during the eight months outside of the rainy season, I allow you to store furniture under a roof cover or at the foot of a tree or wherever crows or ravens don't leave droppings."1

    Synopsis — Monks leave Saṅgha furniture out in the open to catch the winter sun and depart without putting it away or telling anyone, so that it is rained on; the Buddha forbids leaving Saṅgha furniture out and abandoning it, and later allows it to be stored outdoors during the eight dry-season months, in places safe from rain and droppings.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk takes a bed, a bench, a mattress, or a stool belonging to the Sangha and puts it out in the open or has it put out in the open, and he then departs without putting it away, having it put away, or informing anyone, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu puts furniture belonging to the Saṅgha out in the open, and then abandons it, he incurs a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu set a bed, bench, mattress, or stool belonging to the Community out in the open—or have it set out—and then on departing neither put it away nor have it put away, or should he go without taking leave, it is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Leaving Sangha furniture which one was using out in the open (where it might be damaged) is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu, having [himself] laid out a bed or seat or mattress or stool belonging to the community in the open air, or having [someone else] laid [it] out, [and] then, when departing, should not take [it] away or should not have [it] taken away or should go without asking [someone to do so], [this is a case] involving expiation."6

    The renderings agree on the rule and differ at two words, neither of which changes it. The four pieces of furniture are rendered a little differently: Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual has "cushion" for the Vibhaṅga translation's "mattress", Ñāṇatusita "seat" for "bench", and the recitation folds all four into "furniture".7 The other word is anāpucchaṃ, "without informing anyone" in Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga, "without taking leave" in Thanissaro, "without asking [someone to do so]" in Ñāṇatusita. None of them takes it as asking permission: the one told may be a monk, a novice or a monastery worker who will see to the furniture, and, as Thanissaro observes, nothing in the Canon suggests that a monk needs a novice's or an attendant's permission for what he does.8​ 9 The recitation's "abandons" compresses the whole effort clause into one word, and the Vibhaṅga's definitions restore it, as discussed under Effort.10

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule asks a monk to look after what the Saṅgha holds in common, so that the gifts its supporters have given are not spoiled through his carelessness, for the well-being of the Saṅgha that depends on them. The monks of the origin story had carried the furniture out to warm themselves in the winter sun, left when the time for departure was announced, and let it be rained on. Ajahn Brahm calls the purpose obvious: to keep the Saṅgha's furniture from "being damaged by any means", by rain, dew, sun or droppings.11

    Thanissaro analyses the offence into three factors: object, effort and intention.12 The Manual sets out five lines that fall inside the first two. Thanissaro's three are the better guide, since they keep the purpose of setting the furniture out, which decides the offence, as a factor of its own.13

    Object

    Any bed, bench, mattress or stool belonging to the Saṅgha is grounds for the full offence.14 The Manual takes the bench to include a chair, the standard furniture for sitting in a monastic dwelling, and the cushion to include anything called a cushion or mattress, so that the rule reaches the chairs a modern monastery sets out on its lawns.15 Perception is not a mitigating factor (Thanissaro points to Pc 4): whether the monk takes the furniture for the Saṅgha's, for an individual's, or is unsure, leaving the Saṅgha's furniture out is the same offence.16​ 17 The lesser furnishings, a mat underlay, a bedspread, a floor cover, a straw mat, a hide, a foot-wiping cloth or a plank bench, are grounds for wrong conduct, and so is furniture of either kind that belongs to another individual. A monk's own furniture is grounds for nothing.18 Thanissaro reports the Commentary adding that leaving out the furniture of someone whose belongings a monk may take on trust is no offence, and the Sub-commentary that furniture a donor gave for use in the open, a stone or concrete bench, is not grounds for one either. A long commentarial essay on storing brooms he sets aside: brooms were known in the Buddha's day and he did not include them, so extending the rule to them by the Great Standards does not bind.19

    Effort

    The effort is to set the furniture out in the open, or have it set out, and then to depart without putting it away, having it put away, or informing a monk, a novice or a monastery worker. Departing has a measure: going beyond a stone's throw of an average man.20 Thanissaro reckons this at about eighteen metres, and the Manual at roughly twenty, though it prefers "leaves the immediate vicinity" as leaving room for an intelligent application of the rule.21​ 22 Departing means leaving the furniture, not leaving the monastery, and unlike the next rule an intention to come back does not help: once past the stone's throw, the effort is complete even if he means to return at once.23 Ajahn Brahm puts it more leniently: no offence where the furniture is left somewhere safe from damage and the monk means to return in good time. He cites the non-offence clauses for it, and adds that in rainy weather leaving furniture out is an offence at any time.24 To my reading Thanissaro's is the stricter, and a monk who puts the furniture away before walking off is safe on both.

    Putting it away depends on the season. During the four months of the rains, furniture not in use belongs under a full roof, in a storeroom or a dwelling. For the other eight months the Buddha allowed it to be kept under a roof cover, at the foot of a tree, or "wherever crows or ravens don't leave droppings".25​ 26 Thanissaro reports two qualifications, the Commentary's implication that this holds only where there is a real dry season and the Sub-commentary's that a monk who sees an unseasonable storm coming should not leave furniture in such half-open places, and he infers from the Vibhaṅga to Pc 15 that even in the dry season the allowance lasts only while the monk goes on living in the monastery.27

    Who puts it away, when someone else set it out? If an unordained person set it out at the monk's request, it remains the monk's responsibility; if another monk did, the responsibility is his.28 Thanissaro reports how the Commentary follows the handover through. A junior monk who sets out a seat at a senior's request is responsible until the senior sits on it, puts a robe or a shoulder bag on it, or lets him go. At an open-air meeting the hosts answer for the seats until the visitors take their places, and in a series of Dhamma talks each speaker answers for the teaching seat from the moment he sits in it until the next speaker does.29

    Intention

    The full offence needs furniture set out for some purpose other than drying it in the sun. A monk who leaves furniture out to dry, meaning to put it away when he comes back, commits no offence.30​ 31

    What stays with a monk from this rule is less the stone's throw than the chain of responsibility it sets up. At every moment some one person answers for each piece of the Saṅgha's furniture, and the question to ask before walking away from a chair left in the open is simply whose it now is.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • A dukkaṭa for leaving out and abandoning the lesser Saṅgha furnishings: a mat underlay, bedspread, floor cover, straw mat, hide, foot-wiping cloth or plank bench.32
    • A dukkaṭa for the same neglect of furniture of either kind that belongs to another individual, whatever the monk takes it to be; there is no offence where it is his own.33​ 34

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he departs after putting it away; after having it put away; after informing someone; while he is sunning it; if the furniture is obstructed; if there is an emergency; if he is insane; if he is the first offender.35 In Thanissaro's report the Commentary illustrates obstruction with a senior monk making him get up and taking the seat, or a tiger, a lion, a ghost or an ogre settling on it, and takes the emergency as a danger to life or to the celibate life that leaves no time to put the furniture away. Thanissaro adds that there is no offence once someone else has taken possession of or responsibility for it.36

    Related Offences

    • Pc 15 — bedding left out in a Saṅgha dwelling; the same machinery, differing on three points: the bedding is left in a dwelling, not in the open; "departing" means leaving the monastery; and the intention to return does mitigate.
    • Pc 16, 17 — the next two lodging rules: crowding a senior monk out of his sleeping place, and throwing a monk out of a Saṅgha dwelling in anger.

"If it is a mat underlay, a bedspread, a floor cover, a straw mat, a hide, a foot-wiping cloth, or a plank bench… he commits an offense of wrong conduct." Another individual's furniture, a dukkaṭa; "If it belongs to himself, there is no offense."↩︎

    34. Thanissaro, BMC1, Pc 14, "Object": "Perception as to whether the item belongs to the Community is not a mitigating factor here (see Pc 4)." Furnishings of one with whom one has an arrangement to take belongings on trust are no offence; "The Sub-commentary adds that any furnishings a donor presents for the Community to use out in the open—e.g., stone or concrete benches—are likewise not grounds for an offense." On the broom essay: "brooms were known in the time of the Buddha and yet he chose not to include them under this rule—there is no reason to regard them as binding."↩︎
