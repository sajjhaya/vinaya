    Pc 92

    Story (Vatthu)

    At one time the Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time there was a handsome and graceful monk called Venerable Nanda, the Buddha's cousin. He was four fingerbreadths shorter than the Buddha, but he wore a robe that was the same size as the Buddha's.

    When the senior monks saw him coming in the distance, they thought it was the Buddha and got up from their seats. But when he came close, they realised who it was, and they complained and criticised him: "How can Venerable Nanda wear a robe the same size as the Buddha's?"

    The Buddha asked Nanda whether it was true, and he said that it was. The Buddha rebuked him: "Nanda, how can you do this? This will affect people's confidence." After rebuking him, he laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — Nanda, nearly the Buddha's height, wears a robe the size of the Buddha's and is taken for him by the senior monks; the rule that follows forbids a robe of the Buddha's robe size or larger.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk has a robe made that is the standard robe size or larger, it is to be cut down, and he commits an offense entailing confession. This is the standard robe size: nine standard handspans long and six wide."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu makes a robe larger than nine by six standard hand-spans, or has one made, it should be cut down to size and he incurs a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu have a robe made the measurement of the sugata robe or larger, it is to be cut down and confessed. Here, the measurement of the Sugata's sugata robe is this: nine spans—using the sugata span—in length, six spans in width. This is the measurement of the Sugata's sugata robe."4

    Brahm

    "Making a robe the size of, or larger than, a 'Sugata's' robe is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should have a robe made which has the sugata-robe measure or [one] which is more [than that], [this is a case] involving expiation with cutting off [the robe]. This is the Sugata's sugata-robe measure here: nine spans of the sugata-span in length, six spans across. This is the Sugata's sugata-robe measure."6

    Two differences run through the five renderings, and one of them changes the rule. The Pāli forbids a robe of the measure "or larger", and four renderings keep that — Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation, Thanissaro, Ajahn Brahm and Ñāṇatusita. The recitation says "larger than", which lets a robe of exactly the measure through. That wording is exact at the three rules before this one, where the Pāli speaks of exceeding the measure, and it has travelled here, where the Pāli does not; the point is taken up below, under Object. The second difference is whose robe the measure belongs to. The rule closes on "the Sugata's sugata-robe measure", and Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita keep the possessive, as Ajahn Brahm does with "a 'Sugata's' robe"; Brahmāli's "standard robe size" drops it, in keeping with his rendering of sugata as "standard" wherever it qualifies a measure. That question too belongs under Object.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A monk's robe is not to make him look like more than he is, and the rule guards both his own modesty and the respect that belongs to the Buddha, which in the story was given by mistake to someone else. Nanda, the Buddha's cousin, four fingerbreadths shorter than him, wore a robe the size of the Buddha's; the senior monks saw him coming in the distance, took him for the Buddha and rose from their seats, and the Buddha rebuked him with "This will affect people's confidence."7 Like the other measured requisites, the robe is limited, in Ajahn Brahm's words, "to promote simplicity and avoid extravagance", and this rule adds a second reason the story makes plain.8

    This is the last of the Pācittiyas and the last of the measured cloths, and it takes everything but its object and the missing exemption from Pc 89. Making the robe or having it made is wrong conduct for the effort, the full offence falls when the monk receives it, and it must be cut down before he confesses; the four combinations of his own hands and another's all carry the full offence, and making one for someone else, or using one another person made, is wrong conduct.9​ 10 That machinery is set out at Pc 86, and how long a standard handspan is, at Pc 87. The object has three parts: the size, the robe, and whose robe the size was.

    Object

    A robe of nine standard handspans by six, or larger. The rule reaches the measure itself and not only what exceeds it, and its non-offence clause bears this out: at the three rules before it a monk is exempt if he "makes it the right size", while here he is exempt only if he makes it smaller.11 Ajahn Brahm and the Concise BMC both state the consequence, that a robe must be less than the standard size.12​ 13 A robe of exactly the measure is therefore an offence, which the "larger than" of Brahmāli's recitation would miss.

    The Vibhaṅga does not say which robe it means. Thanissaro takes it to be any of the three, and draws an inference worth having: perhaps all three were once much the same size, which would have made keeping a single set far easier than it is now, since a monk could wash one and wear the other two without looking out of place.14 Ajahn Brahm, reading the rule in practical terms, applies it to the upper and outer robes, since a lower robe of that size would trip anyone who wore it.15

    Whose robe the measure belongs to is the question the story answers most directly. The limit is named for a particular robe, the Buddha's, and Nanda is taken for him because he wears one of that size. Brahmāli, who renders sugata as "standard" where it qualifies a measure, concedes in his note to the story that the narrative requires "Buddha", while holding that the narrative does not decide what the word means in a measure.16 Thanissaro uses the story's heights to corroborate his scale, putting the Buddha at about two metres and Nanda a little below, tall but "not outlandishly so".17 On Ajahn Brahm's report of it, the Commentary's Buddha, three times the size of an ordinary man, would give the rule a robe of some 6.75 by 4.5 metres.18

    Disputed — whether the robes monks wear today break this rule, and if they do, what a monk should do about it.

    At a handspan of 25 cm, 2.25 by 1.5 metres, they do: a robe that size, Thanissaro notes, is "much smaller than present-day upper and outer robes", and the Concise BMC agrees.19 Since a monk who wears the right size stands out, Brahmāli's advice in class is to follow the Commentary's larger standard at this rule alone: "you have to be inconsistent, basically".20 His appendix prefers broader criteria to a number, reading the standard measures as ample enough for every monastic to live within, and for those who want one offers 60 cm, three ordinary handspans as the commentaries reckon it, "if only very approximately"; this robe then comes to 5.4 by 3.6 metres, and the multiplication is mine.21 Ajahn Brahm reports two readings from the Vinaya-mukha: one he calls very reasonable, which gives the same 2.25 by 1.5 metres, "too small for the Thai style"; and one it proposes "though not clearly", 3 by 2 metres, "larger, but still reasonable". He holds that no authority fixes the measure and that a monk "should be frugal, making one's robe just big enough to wear neatly".22​ 23 The question is open. I would have a monk follow Ajahn Brahm's counsel and make his robe just big enough to wear neatly, which keeps the rule's purpose whichever measure is right, though not, on the smaller measure, its letter.

    Effort and intention

    As at Pc 86.24

    And so the Pācittiyas end on a question of likeness as much as luxury. The complaint in the story is not that Nanda's robe was costly, or that it dragged in the dirt as the rains cloths of the rule before it did. It is that his robe was the Buddha's size, so that senior monks rose from their seats for the Buddha and found themselves standing for Nanda. Whatever the measure comes to in centimetres, that much a monk can keep without a tape measure: his robe should make him look like a monk, and not like his teacher.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • An offence of wrong conduct for the effort of making a robe of the standard robe size or larger, or having one made, the full offence falling on receiving it, at which point it is to be cut down before the offence is confessed.25
    • An offence of wrong conduct where a monk makes one, or has one made, for the benefit of someone else.26
    • An offence of wrong conduct where a monk obtains one made by someone else and uses it without first cutting it down.27

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he makes it smaller than the standard robe; if he gets one made by another that is too large and cuts it down before using it; if he makes a canopy, a floor cover, a cloth screen, a mattress or a pillow; if he is insane; if he is the first offender.28 Unlike Pc 89, Pc 90 and Pc 91, there is no exemption for making it the right size.

    Related Offences

    • Pc 89 — an oversize sitting mat; the anchor for this rule's measure, penalty and the rest of its non-offence clause.
    • Pc 90, Pc 91 — the itch-covering cloth and the rains cloth, the other measured cloths of this chapter.
    • Pc 87 — where the length of a standard handspan is argued.
    • NP 1 — the determination of the three robes, and the practice of keeping a single set, which Thanissaro connects to the sizes this rule implies.
    • Sk 1, Sk 2 — wearing the lower and upper robes evenly all round; the standard Ajahn Brahm proposes for how large a robe need be.
    • Bi Pc 166 — the same rule for the bhikkhunīs, its wording changed only for gender, and likewise the last of their Pācittiyas.29

    Related Suttas

    • AN 8.30 — to a contented monk a rag-robe seems "a chest full of variously colored garments": the simplicity Ajahn Brahm gives as the reason for the measured requisites, in a robe that wants to be no larger than it needs to be.30​ 31

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; where he gives Nanda's height as seven centimetres shorter than the Buddha, the Story gives the Pāli's four fingerbreadths, which his own note supplies, and "in the distance" renders the Pāli's dūratova, which his translation leaves out. Ajahn Brahm finds that the story makes little sense, since the Buddha is elsewhere said to have exchanged his outer robe with Venerable Mahākassapa and shared new robes with Venerable Ānanda; the doubt is recorded here and not woven in. Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual, the recitation, the class (session 032, 30 July 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1 and its Appendix II, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC, Ñāṇatusita and Brahmāli's appendix of technical terms were all checked, and the nuns' rule was read in Brahmāli's translation of their Pātimokkha. Ajahn Brahm's second reading of the unit reaches this book only through his own account of the Vinaya-mukha, which this book does not use directly.
