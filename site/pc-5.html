    Pc 5

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Āḷavī, at the Aggāḷava Shrine. At that time lay followers were coming to the monastery to listen to the Teaching. When the instruction was over, the senior monks went to their own dwellings, but the newly ordained monks lay down right there in the assembly hall together with the lay followers — absentminded, heedless, naked, muttering, and snoring. The lay followers complained and criticised them: "How can the venerables lie down absentminded, heedless, naked, muttering, and snoring?" The monks of few desires heard, and complained: "How can monks lie down in the same sleeping place as people who are not fully ordained?" After rebuking them in many ways, they told the Buddha, who questioned the monks, rebuked them, and laid down a first version of the rule: a monk was not to lie down in the same sleeping place as a person not fully ordained.

    Later, after wandering on to Kosambī, the Buddha was staying at the Badarikā Monastery. The monks there said to Venerable Rāhula — a novice at the time — "Rāhula, the Buddha has laid down a training rule that we cannot lie down in the same sleeping place as a person who is not fully ordained. Find yourself another sleeping place." Rāhula, unable to find one, lay down in the restroom. Early in the morning the Buddha went to the restroom and cleared his throat; Rāhula cleared his throat too.

    "Who's there?"

    "It's me, sir, Rāhula."

    "Why are you sitting here, Rāhula?"

    Rāhula told him what had happened. Soon afterwards the Buddha addressed the monks: "Monks, I allow you to lie down in the same sleeping place as a person who is not fully ordained for two or three nights." Then he laid down the rule in its final form.1

    Synopsis — Newly ordained monks sleep sprawled among the laity until the lay followers complain; the rule that follows leaves the novice Rāhula with nowhere to sleep but a restroom, and the Buddha relaxes it to allow three nights in shared quarters.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk lies down more than two or three nights in the same sleeping place as a person who is not fully ordained, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu and an unordained person lie down together in the same room for more than three consecutive nights, he incurs a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu lie down together (in the same dwelling) with an unordained person for more than two or three consecutive nights, it is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Lying down at the same time, in the same place, with a novice or layman for more than three nights is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should make use of a sleeping place for more than two nights or three nights together with one who has not been fully admitted [into the bhikkhu-community], [this is a case] involving expiation."6

    The odd "more than two or three nights" keeps the shape of the Story: the Buddha allowed two or three, so the offence begins only past them. Where the renderings part is the place. The recitation says "the same room", Thanissaro "the same dwelling", Ajahn Brahm "the same place", and the choice is not idle, for it carries the first of the rule's two unsettled questions, discussed below under Effort.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule guards two things at once: the confidence of lay people, who should not see a monk asleep among them, and a monk's freedom to shelter those who have no other place. The Story shows both halves. The newly ordained monks lay "absentminded, heedless, naked, muttering, and snoring" before the lay followers who had come to hear the Teaching, and the first rule forbade sharing a sleeping place at all; then Rāhula, a novice with nowhere to go, was found in the restroom, and the Buddha allowed two or three nights.7 Thanissaro gives the same account, through the Vinaya-mukha, and Ajahn Brahm reads the allowance as "a privilege for novices when accommodation was limited".8​ 9

    The offence has two factors in Thanissaro's analysis: object, an unordained person, and effort, lying down together with him in the same dwelling for four nights running.10 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual divides the effort into its parts, and those parts are taken here within Thanissaro's two.

    Object

    For this rule the unordained person is anyone except a fully ordained monk, so that a nun is included; since the next rule, Pc 6, catches every woman, the point matters little.11 Perception does not excuse, on the pattern set out at Pc 4.12

    Effort

    Lying down together. Both must be lying down at the same time, whichever lay down first, and whether or not either sleeps; each time they get up and lie down again is a fresh offence.13​ 14 Sitting is not lying down here: there is no offence if one sits while the other lies, or both sit.15

    The sleeping place. The Vibhaṅga defines it by how far it is roofed and walled. Fully or mostly roofed and walled is the full offence; half of each is a dukkaṭa; a place roofed but unwalled, walled but unroofed, or mostly open on either count is no offence.16​ 17 Thanissaro reports the Commentary as including a tent, and adds that a vehicle would count as well: cars, trains, buses and aircraft.18 Nor, in Brahmāli's view, does a hospital ward escape the rule.19

    Disputed — how far "the same sleeping place" reaches: a single room, or a whole building.

    The Manual takes the defining words to mean a room: channa "quite clearly means walled", and "if one is not in the same room, then one is not sleeping together within the same walled area".20 Brahmāli's own translation, though, renders the pair "fully roofed, fully walled", the other way round.21 In Thanissaro's report the Commentary reads far more widely: every room sharing a common entrance is one dwelling, a hundred rooms of them if need be, and closing a door divides nothing. The Vinaya-mukha, through Thanissaro, would divide a building by its separate residences, each flat or hotel room its own dwelling, and Ajahn Brahm reports its wider extension to anywhere the two "are in plain sight of one another".22​ 23 The Canon does not fix the boundary, and Thanissaro's counsel is to follow one's own Community.24

    Four nights. How are the nights counted? By dawns. If one of the two leaves the dwelling before dawn, the night does not count, and after two nights a monk who leaves before dawn on the third and stays out until dawnrise begins the count again.25​ 26 Once three nights have run, lying down together at any time after sundown on the fourth, even for a moment, is the offence; how far "after sundown" reaches is argued at Pc 6, where it carries the whole rule.27 By Thanissaro's report the Commentary adds that neither the person nor the dwelling need be the same on each night.28 Nor do perception or intention excuse a miscount, and Thanissaro observes that this is a rule a monk may break without knowing it: a novice who comes in each night and leaves before the monk wakes, four nights running, brings him the offence unawares.29

    Disputed — whether leaving before dawn truly breaks a night.

    The Vibhaṅga's non-offence clause grants the break, and Thanissaro holds it to its verb, nikkhamitvā, "having left": rising without leaving the dwelling, as an old Siamese tradition allows, does not break the series.30​ 31 Ajahn Brahm takes the break as real and as achieved by rising alone.32 The Manual doubts the break itself: "A night is not the same as dawn", and one who takes his night's rest in the same room has spent the night there whether or not he rose before first light.33 In class Brahmāli goes further, calling it a technicality and saying of the clause, "I don't think it's the word of the Buddha", while granting that because it stands in the Canon many keep it in good conscience, and that he would not argue the point with them.34 The clause is in the text and his doubt is partly about the clause's meaning and partly about its origin. A monk who relies on the clause has the Canon's words behind him, and one who does not rely on it is safe on every reading.

    Whatever the count, the purpose remains. The Vinaya-mukha, which Thanissaro endorses, counsels keeping that purpose in mind and avoiding sleeping with an unordained person wherever possible; Thanissaro adds that one should avoid sleeping in full view, "in a public park, on a public beach, in an unwalled pavilion", even where no offence would be involved, and Ajahn Brahm draws the same moral: a monk does well to avoid lying down with laymen at all, and not to live at the edge of the count.35​ 36

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • A dukkaṭa where the sleeping place is half-roofed and half-walled.37
    • A dukkaṭa where the other person is in fact fully ordained but the monk perceives him as unordained, or is unsure.38

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he stays together with them for two or three nights, or fewer; if, after two nights, he leaves before dawn on the third and then stays together again; if the place is fully roofed but not walled, fully walled but not roofed, or mostly not roofed or mostly not walled; if one sits while the other lies, or both sit; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.39

    Related Offences

    • Pc 6 — the mirror of this rule for a woman: no three-night allowance there, and the reading of "after sundown" argued in full.
    • Pc 4 — the source of the six-fold perception pattern this rule follows.
    • NP 2 — the source of both factors borrowed in the "single dwelling" dispute: the Commentary's single enclosure and the Vinaya-mukha's separate residence.40
    • Pc 44, 45 — sitting alone with a woman, the situation this rule's "sitting is no offence" clause leaves aside.
