    Sg 9

    Story (Vatthu)

    At one time when the Buddha was staying at Rājagaha in the Bamboo Grove, the monks Mettiya and Bhūmajaka were descending from the Vulture Peak when they saw two goats copulating. They said to each other, "Let's give the he-goat the name Dabba the Mallian and the she-goat the name Mettiyā the nun. We can then say, 'Previously we spoke of what we had heard, but now we've seen Dabba copulating with the nun Mettiyā.'" They gave the goats those names and told the monks so. The monks replied, "Don't say such things. Venerable Dabba wouldn't do that."

    The monks told the Buddha, who had the Saṅgha gathered and asked Dabba whether he remembered doing as those monks said. "Sir, you know what I'm like." He asked a second and a third time and got the same answer. "Dabba, the Dabbas don't give such evasive answers. If it was done by you, say so; if it wasn't, then say that." "Since I was born, sir, I don't recall having sexual intercourse even in a dream, let alone when awake." "Well then, monks, call those monks to account."

    The monks questioned Mettiya and Bhūmajaka, who told them what had happened. "So did you charge Venerable Dabba with an offense entailing expulsion, using an unrelated legal issue as a pretext?" "Yes." The monks of few desires complained and told the Buddha, who questioned the two monks, rebuked them, and laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — Their first charge having failed, Mettiya and Bhūmajaka name two mating goats after Dabba and the nun Mettiyā and report that they have now seen the act; the Buddha lays down a second rule against a charge built on a pretext.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk who is angry and displeased, uses an unrelated legal issue as a pretext to charge a monk with an offense entailing expulsion, aiming to make him leave the monastic life, and then after some time, whether he is questioned or not, it is clear that the legal issue is unrelated and was used as a pretext, and he admits to his ill will, he commits an offense entailing suspension."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu, motivated by anger, uses a ploy to falsely accuse a bhikkhu of a pārājika, aiming to make him disrobe, he incurs a saṅghādisesa."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu—corrupt, aversive, disgruntled—using as a mere ploy an aspect of an issue that pertains otherwise, charge a bhikkhu with a case entailing defeat, (thinking), 'Perhaps I may bring about his fall from this celibate life,' then regardless of whether or not he is cross-examined on a later occasion, if the issue pertains otherwise, an aspect used as a mere ploy, and the bhikkhu confesses his aversion, it entails initial and subsequent meetings of the Community."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Should any monk, malicious, angered, displeased, taking as a mere pretext an aspect of an issue pertaining otherwise, charge a monk with a case involving defeat, (thinking), 'surely with this I may bring about his fall from the holy life', then regardless of whether or not he is cross-examined on a later occasion, if the issue pertains otherwise, an aspect taken as mere pretext, and the monk is motivated by anger, it entails initial and subsequent meetings of the community."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu, corrupted by malice [and] upset, should accuse a bhikkhu with a case involving disqualification, having taken [it] up [with] some point, which is a mere pretext, of a legal issue belonging to another class [thinking]: 'If only I could make him fall away from this holy life!,' [and] then, on another occasion, [whether] being interrogated or not being interrogated, if that legal issue is really belonging to another class, [and] some point, which a mere pretext, has been taken up, and if the bhikkhu stands firm in malice: [this is a case] involving the community in the beginning and in the rest."6

    The five renderings repeat Sg 8's and add one phrase. Brahmāli's "uses an unrelated legal issue as a pretext", Thanissaro's "using as a mere ploy an aspect of an issue that pertains otherwise" and Ñāṇatusita's "some point, which is a mere pretext, of a legal issue belonging to another class" all keep the Pāli's two parts, a real issue of another kind and a point of it seized on. The recitation compresses this to "uses a ploy", and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual makes the ploy a factor, "a literally true statement designed to mislead the listener",7 a reading Ajahn Brahm's synopsis shares. The two framings meet different cases, discussed below, under Effort; the motive and the closing clause are Sg 8's and are discussed there.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule protects a monk from a false charge built on a true fact. Mettiya and Bhūmajaka came back and claimed, this time, to have seen it: "Previously we spoke of what we had heard, but now we've seen Dabba copulating with the nun Mettiyā." What they had seen was real, two goats on the slope of the Vulture Peak; only the names were false. "Some grudges die hard," Thanissaro observes of the Story.8 A charge built from something true can mislead as surely as one made up, and the rule gives it the same penalty.

    Everything else is Sg 8's: the four factors, the dispute over anger, the questions of presence and understanding, the closing clause, the procedure for asking leave and the derived offences are all treated there and are not repeated here.9​ 10 What differs is one part of the effort, and the non-offence clause that answers it.

    Effort

    In place of Sg 8's groundless charge, the charge here takes up some point of a legal issue that is unrelated to the one charged, of another kind.11 Thanissaro puts the difference exactly. Under Sg 8 the accuser has neither seen, heard nor suspected an offence, or he changes the status of his evidence; here he has seen an action that would be an offence if a monk did it, keeps the status of the evidence, and distorts the facts of the case.12 The Vibhaṅga lists ten pretexts, caste, name, family, characteristic, offence, bowl, robe, preceptor, teacher and dwelling, and its illustrations fall into two classes, which Thanissaro names for ease of remembering.13

    Same person, different offence. The accuser sees a monk commit a lesser offence, regards it as some lesser offence, and charges him with a pārājika; the Vibhaṅga's permutations run through every class, from a saṅghādisesa down to wrong speech.14 Thanissaro's example is a monk who strikes another in a fit of anger, meaning only to hurt him. The blow proves fatal, which for lack of an intention to kill is a pācittiya, and one who knows this and charges him with a pārājika commits the full offence.15

    Same offence, different person. The accuser sees someone, a monk or not, do what would be a pārājika if a monk did it, and charges a monk who shares some feature with him: the same name, the same height or complexion, the same preceptor or dwelling, a bowl or robe of the same kind. "I saw this big tall guy stealing the tools, and he looked just like you. It must have been you."16 Ajahn Brahm's case shows the ploy at its plainest, with nothing false in the words at all. A monk, angry with his monastery's Vinaya master, watches a former Vinaya master, now disrobed and married, with his wife, and comes back announcing, "I have now truly seen the old Vinaya-Master committing sexual-intercourse!", hoping to be taken to mean the present one; whether he is believed or not, it is a saṅghādisesa.17 A double ploy, a different person and a different offence, is mentioned in none of the texts, but Thanissaro finds that it fits the Vibhaṅga's definition as well.18

    Where nothing that was seen was an offence at all, the sources file the case differently. Thanissaro's example is a Vinaya teacher overheard quoting, as illustrations for new monks, statements that would count as claims to a superior human state: with no offence there is no issue of another kind to use as a ploy, and a charge of Pj 4 made knowing the context is an unfounded charge under Sg 8.19 Ajahn Brahm gives a different case: the accuser makes no charge of Pj 4 at all; he reports, truly, that he heard the teacher say "I am an Arahant", meaning to be misunderstood, and that is a saṅghādisesa under this rule.20 It fits the Manual's factor for the ploy, "a literally true statement designed to mislead the listener". The two cases are not one case filed two ways: in the first the accuser makes a false charge outright, in the second he says nothing false.

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The non-offence clause is this rule's own: there is no offence if he accuses, or has someone accused, "in accordance with his own perception".21 It answers the effort factor. One who sees the tall man stealing and takes him in good faith for the monk he resembles, or sees the fatal blow and believes it was meant to kill, has no offence however the case turns out, though as under Sg 8 he should ask leave first and should have no intention of insulting.22 And so the rule asks of a monk who has really seen something what Sg 8 asks of one who has seen nothing: to say what he saw, and no more.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • As at Sg 8 — a dukkaṭa for accusing without asking leave, or for asking leave with no grounds; by the Commentary, a dukkaṭa for a charge behind the accused's back; and, by Ajahn Brahm's extension of the Samantapāsādikā's pattern from Pj 1, a thullaccaya for a charge heard but not understood and a dukkaṭa for one not heard (both disputed there).23

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he accuses or has someone accused in accordance with his own perception; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.24

    Related Offences

    • Sg 8 — the same charge made from nothing, with the factors, disputes and procedure this rule shares.
    • Pj 4 — the false claim, whose quoted words, used in teaching, are the case the sources file differently above.

    Related Suttas

    • MN 58 — speech is measured by more than its facts, and a charge built on a pretext does not reach even this: "Such speech as the Tathāgata knows to be true and correct but unbeneficial, and which is also unwelcome and disagreeable to others: such speech the Tathāgata does not utter."25
    • AN 5.167 — the second of Sāriputta's five things to establish before reproving another: "I will speak truthfully, not falsely". The goats were real; the names were not.26
    • Snp 3.10 — the verses on Kokālika's accusation: "One who praises a person deserving blame, / or blames a person deserving praise, / collects bad luck with his mouth, / by which he finds no happiness"; Dabba was one deserving praise.27
