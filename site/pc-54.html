    Pc 54

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Kosambī in Ghosita's Monastery. Venerable Channa was misbehaving. The monks would tell him, "Channa, don't do that; it's not allowable," and he just did it again out of disrespect.

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised him, "How can Venerable Channa act disrespectfully?" The Buddha questioned him, established that it was true, rebuked him, and stated the rule.1

    Synopsis — The rule is not about what Channa did. It is about what he did after he was told, and the origin story is arranged so that the misbehaviour is never named: the reader is given only the correction and the response to it.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk is disrespectful, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "When rightly corrected by a bhikkhu about a breach of a rule laid down by the Buddha, being disrespectful of that bhikkhu or the Dhamma is a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Disrespect is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Acting disrespectfully towards a monk or the Dhamma when being admonished by a fellow monk concerning a Vinaya rule is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "In disrespect, [there is a case] involving expiation."6

    The rule is two words long, anādariye pācittiyaṁ, "in disrespect, a pācittiya", and the renderings divide only by how much of the word-analysis they write back into it.7 Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation, Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita keep it bare; Brahmāli's recitation line and Ajahn Brahm name the admonisher, the occasion and both objects of the disrespect.8​ 9 For the most part neither course changes the rule. The Vibhaṅga itself confines the pācittiya to disrespect shown to a fully ordained monk who corrects one about a rule that has been laid down, so the longer lines say in the rule what the shorter ones leave to the analysis. In one place they say more. "A rule laid down by the Buddha" in the recitation line and "a Vinaya rule" in Ajahn Brahm's each write into the rule one side of the question disputed below, under Object; the Pāli has neither.

    One word in the recitation line could change it. "Rightly corrected" has nothing answering to it in this rule's Pāli, and read as "correctly" it would make the justice of the correction a condition of the offence. The neighbouring rule shows what it is. Pc 71's Pāli has sahadhammikaṁ vuccamāno, "spoken to in accordance with the rule", and Brahmāli's recitation line for it opens with the same English, so "rightly corrected" is his standing formula for a correction properly made, carried across to a rule that shares the clause, and it adds no third condition.10 Whether the correction must also be deserved is a separate question, and its answer lies in the object.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    Correction from his fellows is how a monk goes on being trained after his ordination, and this rule keeps that way open: his own willingness to be taught, and the admonisher's willingness to speak, on which the training of the whole Saṅgha depends. The origin story never says what Channa did. The monks told him it was not allowable, and he did it again out of disrespect; that reply is the whole of the rule's concern.11

    Thanissaro gives the offence two factors, object and effort, and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual folds them into a single one, which changes nothing.12​ 13

    Object

    The pācittiya needs two things on the other side of the exchange: an admonisher who is fully ordained, and a rule that has been laid down. Where a fully ordained monk criticises something not laid down, on the ground that it does not lead to self-effacement, to ascetic practice, to being inspiring, to a reduction in things or to energy, disrespect is an offence of wrong conduct. Where the admonisher is not fully ordained, it is wrong conduct whatever he cites, and what one perceives about his ordination makes no difference in either direction, a pattern Thanissaro refers to Pc 42.14​ 15 Criticism that cites no rule and appeals to none of the five aims is no ground for an offence at all, though Thanissaro adds that it would be wise to avoid showing anyone disrespect, whatever the situation.16

    Disputed — how far "a rule that has been laid down" reaches, which decides where the pācittiya ends and the wrong conduct begins.

    Thanissaro reports the Commentary reading "not laid down" as what is taught in the suttas and the Abhidhamma, so that the lesser offence covers criticism drawn from the other two baskets. Thanissaro gives the pācittiya to any rule formulated in the Vinaya and widens the lesser offence instead: since the Vibhaṅga's usual term for the suttas is "another Dhamma", its wording here seems to him to take in any principle aimed at the five goals, wherever it is expressed.17 Brahmāli narrows the pācittiya from the other end, to rules laid down by the Buddha himself, which for him are above all the Pātimokkha rules.18​ 19 One passage of the Khandhaka bears on his reading. At the invitation ceremony, a monk who is asked to wait until whoever is sick is well before pressing an accusation, and presses it all the same, commits "an offense entailing confession for disrespect", this rule's penalty, for disregarding a Khandhaka procedure.20 The compilers of the Khandhaka, then, read the word as reaching their own rulings. That is evidence about the compilers rather than about the rule as first framed, and Brahmāli's narrowing is a deliberate practical judgment, made to keep later accretion out of a pācittiya. A monk who treats every correction grounded in the Vinaya as within the rule will not go wrong, though that is the safer reading chosen, not the question settled.

    Whether the correction was deserved does not matter. Thanissaro is explicit that even an admonisher who is "really an ignorant fool" or has misread the rule is not to be shown disrespect.21 Ajahn Brahm appears to read it down, giving wrong conduct where the monk who shows the disrespect is "wrongly admonished, not having broken any rule".22 The difference seems to be one of scope, not of doctrine. He cites the permutations' clause on what has not been laid down, and for a correction about no rule at all his wrong conduct is the Vibhaṅga's own. For a correction about a real rule by someone who has the facts wrong, the Vibhaṅga has no separate clause, and the object is met by what was cited, not by whether the citation was deserved. A mistaken correction may be answered. It may not be treated with contempt.

    Effort

    The Vibhaṅga names two kinds of disrespect.23 Disrespect for the person is a thought followed by a refusal: the monk thinks that his admonisher has himself been ejected, or reproved, or blamed, and resolves not to do what he says. The three thoughts concern the admonisher's standing and not his manners, and the Pāli closes on "this one's word will not be acted on".24 The monk is not so much insulting the man who corrected him as ruling him out of court. Disrespect for the rule is stronger than its English name suggests: the monk wonders what can be done so that the rule is lost, perishes or disappears, or he does not want to train in it.

    Either kind may be shown by word or by gesture. Thanissaro's examples for the person run from "Who are you to tell me?" to a slight facial expression of contempt, and for the rule from "That's a stupid rule" to a rude gesture.25 One form needs no words at all, and the origin story records it: doing it again. Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm both count stubborn repetition of the act one was admonished for.26​ 27 Nor need the disrespect be shown to the admonisher's face. None of the texts locate it, and Thanissaro draws the consequence, marking it as his inference, that disrespect expressed behind his back fulfils the factor as well.28

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    Beside insanity and the first offender there is one exemption: "if he says, 'This is how we were taught and tested by our teachers.'" "Tested" renders paripucchā, which Brahmāli's endnote explains as a teacher questioning his student to find out how much the student knows; the plea is a curriculum, not a remark.29 It is the whole practical answer to disagreement. A monk may hold a different reading of a rule, and he may say so; Thanissaro holds that one avoids the offence "simply by stating that one's teachers taught differently", whether the teacher is right or wrong, and finds the Commentary's discussion of which traditions qualify beside the point. Ajahn Brahm adds the next step, to settle the disagreement afterwards by consulting a respected and knowledgeable senior monk.30​ 31

    The Canon gives a model of each way of meeting correction. At the First Council, Ānanda, charged by the senior monks with five faults he could not see, confessed each of them out of faith in the senior monks.32 In the same account Venerable Purāṇa, invited to accept the Council's recitation, answered, "The Teaching and the Monastic Law have been well-recited by the senior monks. Nevertheless, I'll remember what I myself have received from the Buddha." No charge follows and no rebuke.33 Thanissaro gives the reason a monk should want to be the first: Dhp 76 asks him to regard whoever points out his faults as a guide to hidden treasure, and a guide who is shown disrespect is unlikely to point the way again.34 One elder accepted a correction he could not see; the other disagreed without contempt.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — disrespect when a fully ordained monk corrects one about something not laid down, on one of the five aims.35
    • Dukkaṭa — disrespect toward an admonisher who is not fully ordained, whatever he cites and however his status is perceived.36
    • Dukkaṭa, at least — treating with disrespect another monk's remark that one is doing something improper in going, when one takes leave of him; Thanissaro works the case under Pc 46 and again under Pc 85.37

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he says, "This is how we were taught and tested by our teachers"; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.38

    Related Offences

    • Sg 12 — being difficult to admonish: the rule for a monk beyond correction altogether, which Thanissaro names as this rule's escalation; Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm add suspension beyond it.39​ 40
    • Pc 12, Pc 71 — evasive speech under questioning, and finding an excuse not to train in a rule: with this rule and Sg 12, the Pātimokkha's rules on behaving badly under correction. Pc 71 covers the monk who evades a rule without out-and-out disrespect; Venerable Channa is the subject of the origin stories of all three, each set at Kosambī.
    • Pc 2 — insulting speech: Thanissaro reads abuse framed as a command, "Go to hell!" and the like, as falling in most cases under this rule where it does not fit that rule's taxonomy of remarks about the person.
    • Pc 73 — feigning ignorance of a Pātimokkha rule one has broken: the Concise BMC pairs it with this rule.41
    • The sekhiyas — the training rules offended against only when broken out of disrespect. Brahmāli's introductions extend the same treatment to the first and eighth important principles and to the minor rules of the Khandhakas.42

    Related Suttas

    • AN 4.122 — a monk angry at being instructed by monks "young enough to be my sons or grandsons" gives up the training: where the disrespect this rule penalises ends.43
    • MN 65 — Bhaddāli refused to undertake a training precept and later confessed the refusal as a transgression: disrespect for the training, and the way back from it.44
    • Dhp 76 — "a person who points out your faults" is to be seen as one who leads you to hidden treasure, and a guide shown disrespect, Thanissaro adds, is unlikely to point the way again.45​ 46

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; the source never names the misbehaviour, and the complaint, interrogation and rebuke are given in the standing abbreviated form. The Manual, the recitation, Brahmāli's endnote, the class transcript (session 028, 12 June 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1 and BMC2, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were checked, with the Khandhaka passages on the invitation ceremony and the First Council in Brahmāli's translation and AN 4.122 and MN 65 in Bhikkhu Bodhi's, and Dhp 76 in Bhikkhu Suddhāso's. Ajahn Brahm cites the Vinaya-mukha once, for consulting a senior monk; the step is given as his own. One class use is kept, Brahmāli's narrowing of the laid-down rules to the Pātimokkha. The Commentary is known here only through Thanissaro.
