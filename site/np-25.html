    NP 25

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time Venerable Upananda the Sakyan said to his brother's student, "Come, let's go wandering the country."

    "I can't, venerable, my robes are worn out."

    "I'll give you a robe." And he gave him a robe.

    Soon afterwards that monk heard that the Buddha was about to go wandering the country. He thought, "Now I'll go wandering with the Buddha instead." Then, when Upananda said, "Let's go," he replied, "I'm not going with you, but with the Buddha."

    "Well, that robe I gave you is going with me," and he just took it back in anger.

    That monk told other monks what had happened, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised Upananda, "How could Venerable Upananda give away a robe and then take it back in anger?" After rebuking him in many ways, they told the Buddha. He had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned the monks: "Is it true, Upananda, that you did this?" "It's true, sir." The Buddha rebuked him: "Foolish man, how could you do this? This will affect people's confidence …" And he laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — Upananda gives his brother's student a robe so that he will go wandering with him; when the student chooses to go with the Buddha instead, Upananda takes the robe back in anger, and the Buddha makes taking back in anger a robe one has given to a monk an offence entailing relinquishment.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk himself gives a robe to a monk, but then, in anger, takes it back or has it taken back, he commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu gives robe-cloth to a bhikkhu, but then, motivated by anger, takes it back or has it taken back, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu — having himself given robe-cloth to (another) bhikkhu and then being angered and displeased — snatch it back or have it snatched back, it is to be forfeited and confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Having given a robe or some cloth to another monk, taking it back without his consent, because one is annoyed with him, is a nissaggiya pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu, having himself given a robe to a bhikkhu, should, being resentful [and] displeased, snatch [it] away or should have it snatched away [from the bhikkhu], [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture."6

    The renderings agree. The Pāli pairs two words for the state of mind, kupito anattamano, "resentful [and] displeased", which Brahmāli folds into "in anger", and its verb, acchindeyya, is to snatch or rob, which Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita keep and Brahmāli softens to "takes back".7 Ajahn Brahm builds into the rule the condition the non-offence clauses supply, "without his consent". Ñāṇatusita records a reading in several editions, pacchā kupito, "afterwards resentful", which the Sanskrit recensions share: the anger comes after the gift.8

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A gift given is not a hold over the one who received it, and the rule keeps a monk from making it one. Upananda's robe came with a purpose, a companion for the road, and when the student chose to go with the Buddha instead, the gift turned into anger: "Well, that robe I gave you is going with me."9 The Buddha's rebuke names the cost beyond the two monks, "This will affect people's confidence", and the rule draws its line at the anger, leaving the wish to have the robe back alone.

    Why is snatching back a gift not theft? The Commentary's answer, in Thanissaro's report, is that the rule covers a monk who still perceives the robe as rightfully his own, as when he gave it on a condition the other has not met.10 A monk who takes in that state of mind does not take what he perceives as another's, so the taking is not Pj 2; but one who had given the robe up entirely and then snatches it back is judged under Pj 2.11​ 12

    Thanissaro gives the offence three factors, object, effort and intention; Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual defines the anger and the taking back, and builds the perception into the taking: "Perceiving it to be one's own". Thanissaro's three serve here.13​ 14

    Object

    Any of the six kinds of robe-cloth, not smaller than what can be assigned to another, given by the monk himself to a monk.15 Ajahn Brahm's examples show how wide that is: a robe, a sitting cloth, a bag.16 Other requisites given to a monk, and anything given to one not fully ordained, are wrong conduct.17 Whether the other is a monk is a matter of fact: if he is fully ordained, the offence is full however he is perceived, and if not, it is wrong conduct however he is perceived.18​ 19

    Effort

    Taking it back oneself completes the offence. Having another take it is wrong conduct for the asking and the full offence when it is taken, and if one request brings back many robes, all of them are to be relinquished.20​ 21 The taking is without the other's consent: if he gives the robe back of his own accord, there is no offence.22 In Thanissaro's report the Commentary draws a line in the asking. A reminder made gently, "I gave you the robe in hopes that you would study with me", followed by a return costs nothing; a hint that shows anger is wrong conduct for the hint, though the return itself still carries no penalty.23

    Intention

    The monk is moved by anger or displeasure, which the Vibhaṅga defines as "discontent, having hatred, hostile."24 The displeasure need not be great: the only innocent intention the clauses allow is taking on trust, and Thanissaro concludes from this that a little is enough.25 Taking back for any other reason is outside the rule: on trust, as a loan, by mistake for one's own, or to save the robe from loss or damage.26

    Relinquishment. The procedure is NP 1's, in this rule's words, "Venerables, this robe-cloth, which I took back after giving it to a monk, is to be relinquished", and the robe is then given back, to the monk who took it.27 Ajahn Brahm finds it worth remarking that the offender gets the robe in the end.28 Brahmāli's answer in class was that what happens next is a matter between the two monks: where the taking was plainly wrong, giving the robe back to the other would be the fitting thing, but the Vinaya leaves it there.29 The rule sets no price on the robe; it sets one on the anger, and the remedy for the anger is not the Saṅgha's to give.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — asking another to take the robe back, before it is taken.30
    • Dukkaṭa — taking back in anger any other requisite given to a monk.31
    • Dukkaṭa — taking back in anger robe-cloth or any requisite given to one not fully ordained, however he is perceived.32
    • Dukkaṭa — a hint for the robe's return that shows anger (the Commentary, in Thanissaro's report).33
    • Nissaggiya pācittiya (the Sub-commentary, in Thanissaro's report) — giving robe-cloth to a layman who is to be ordained and taking it back in this way after his ordination.34

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if the other person gives it back; if he takes it on trust from them; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.35 Taking on trust is treated at NP 1.

    Related Offences

    • Pj 2 — taking back a gift wholly given up, which is theft.
    • Pc 17 — throwing a monk out of a dwelling in anger, the same kupito anattamano applied to lodgings.
    • Pc 74, Pc 75 — striking a monk, and raising a hand against one, from the same state of mind.

    Related Suttas

    • MN 128 — Anuruddha and his companions "blending like milk and water, viewing each other with kindly eyes" (see Sg 10); a gift between monks belongs to that concord, and taking it back in anger breaks it.36
