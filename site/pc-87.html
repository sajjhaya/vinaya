    Pc 87

    Story (Vatthu)

    At one time the Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. Venerable Upananda the Sakyan was sleeping on a high bed.1

    On one occasion, as the Buddha and a number of monks were walking about the dwellings, they came to Upananda's dwelling. Upananda saw the Buddha coming and said to him, "Sir, please come and see my bed."

    The Buddha turned around on the spot and addressed the monks: "A fool, monks, can be recognized by his sleeping place."2

    After rebuking Upananda in many ways, the Buddha spoke in dispraise of being difficult to support, and laid down the rule.3

    Synopsis — Upananda invites the Buddha to come and admire his high bed; the Buddha turns back without looking at it, and the rule that follows caps the legs of a monk's bed or bench at eight standard fingerbreadths, anything taller to be cut down.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk is having a new bed or bench made, it is to have legs eight standard fingerbreadths long below the lowest frame. If the legs exceed that, they are to be cut down, and he commits an offense entailing confession."4

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu makes a bed or bench with legs longer than eight standard fingerbreadths, excluding the frame, or has one made, then the legs should be cut down to size and he incurs a pācittiya."5

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "When a bhikkhu is having a new bed or bench made, it is to have legs (at most) eight fingerbreadths long—using sugata fingerbreadths—not counting the lower edge of the frame. In excess of that it is to be cut down and confessed."6

    Brahm

    "Having a bed or chair made with long legs is an offence of Pācittiya."7

    Ñāṇatusita

    "By a bhikkhu who is having a new bed or seat made, [a bed or seat] which has legs of eight finger-breadths is to be made, according to the Sugata-finger-breadth, except the lowermost [edge of the] frame. For one who lets it exceed [this measure], [this is a case] involving expiation with cutting down [the legs]."8

    Pīṭha is "bench" in Brahmāli and Thanissaro, "chair" in Ajahn Brahm and "seat" in Ñāṇatusita, and nothing turns on it: Brahmāli's own appendix notes that the word covered everything from a footstool to a seat for two or more people that could also be slept on, and renders it "bench" nevertheless, except in pādapīṭha, "footstool".9 What does turn on a choice is the measure. Four of the five renderings leave sugataṅgula untranslated in substance — "standard fingerbreadths", "sugata fingerbreadths", "the Sugata-finger-breadth" — and hand the reader an unconverted unit. Ajahn Brahm drops the measure from the line altogether, giving only "long legs" and leaving the number to his commentary. The unit's length is not settled; it is taken up below, under Object.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule protects a monk from the comfort that shows, and from the habit of being difficult to support that comes with it. Upananda was sleeping on a high bed, and when the Buddha came past his dwelling he invited him in to see it; the Buddha turned around on the spot and told the monks that a fool can be recognised by his sleeping place, and he spoke in dispraise of being difficult to support before laying down the rule.10 Thanissaro puts its purpose in a line, to keep monks from making and using furnishings that are "high and imposing".11 The object is one a novice already knows: Ajahn Brahm identifies it as the uccāsayana, the high bed that novices undertake to refrain from, and lay people too when they keep the eight precepts.12 The rule does not make a monk keep that precept; it stops him owning the thing the precept is about.

    Everything but the object is shared with Pc 86, where the machinery is set out: making or having made is wrong conduct for the effort, the full offence falls when the monk receives the finished bed, all four combinations of his own hands and another's carry it, and making one for someone else, or using one another person made, is wrong conduct.13​ 14 Thanissaro's three factors are the ones he gives there, object, effort and intention, and only the first needs treatment of its own.15

    Object

    A new bed or bench whose legs, measured from the floor to the underside of the lowest frame, are longer than eight standard fingerbreadths.16 The Vibhaṅga names four ways of building bed and bench alike, and Brahmāli's appendix of furniture, quoting the Samantapāsādikā, describes the first three: rails mortised into the legs, rails made to bite into the legs, and crooked legs; the fourth is the Vibhaṅga's own "detachable legs". The four are an inventory of the furniture of the time, and the rule applies to all of them.17 A bench, on the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī's account in Thanissaro's report of it, is shorter than a bed but not so short as to be square, because the Khandhakas allow a square stool, the āsandika, even with long legs, and a chair with a back and arms, the sattaṅga, likewise.18

    Disputed — how long eight standard fingerbreadths is. The rule fixes a number and leaves the unit to be settled elsewhere, and the sources settle it differently enough that the permitted leg runs from 16 cm to the height of an ordinary bed.

    On one reckoning the answer is 16.7 cm: Thanissaro takes the standard handspan as 25 cm and the fingerbreadth as a twelfth of it, and he supports the scale from this very rule (his appendix, running the argument, puts the legs at 18 cm), for the Khandhakas tell a monk to feel under his bed before setting his bowl there, and legs "much taller than that" would make the groping needless, while legs much shorter would not take even a small bowl.19 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual follows him, at 16 cm.20 Brahmāli's appendix of technical terms, though, quotes the commentarial gloss that a standard fingerbreadth is three of an average man's and puts it at 5 cm, so that eight of them come to about 40 cm; the multiplication is mine.21 Ajahn Brahm finds no authority that fixes the unit and leaves the judgement to each monk, "remembering that the purpose of limiting the sizes of these requisites is to promote simplicity and avoid extravagance", and the appendix likewise prefers the Vinaya's broader test of not being indulgent to any number.22 The texts do not settle the unit; a bed whose legs stand no higher than 16 cm keeps the rule on every reckoning, and what does not depend on the number is the point both Ajahn Brahm and Brahmāli's appendix return to: a monk's bed is not to be a luxury.

    Effort and intention

    As at Pc 86.23

    The rule leaves one question open: whether a monk may sleep on a high bed that is not his. The rule reaches only a bed he makes or obtains, but the Khandhakas reach the sleeping. Monks of the group of six slept on high beds, people walking about the dwellings complained that they were "just like householders who indulge in worldly pleasures", and the Buddha made it wrong conduct; when a monk on a low bed was then bitten by a snake he allowed bed supports, and when those too were made high he capped them at eight standard fingerbreadths, which Brahmāli takes to be this rule's measure.24​ 25 In a layperson's house a monk may sit on what belongs to the householder but not lie down on it, and high and luxurious couches he may not even sit on; Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm read the allowance as covering any furnishing a monk could not own himself.26​ 27​ 28 Thanissaro gives the Commentary as defining high in the Canon's ban on high and great furnishings as above this rule's height, and the cap on bed supports points the same way: the measure of this rule is meant for the height a monk sleeps at, wherever the bed came from.29

    A bed whose legs are too long need not even have them sawn off. Thanissaro gives the Commentary as allowing the monk to bury them in the ground until no more than eight fingerbreadths stand between the floor and the underside of the frame — a solution so practical it is almost funny, and one that shows what is being measured.30 It is not the bed but the man on it, which is why the Buddha, invited to come and admire the thing, turned around on the spot and told the monks that you can tell a fool by where he sleeps.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • An offence of wrong conduct for the effort of making such a bed or bench or having one made, the full offence falling on receiving it, at which point the legs are to be cut down before the offence is confessed.31
    • An offence of wrong conduct where a monk makes one, or has one made, for the benefit of someone else.32
    • An offence of wrong conduct where a monk obtains one made by someone else and uses it without first cutting the legs down.33
    • An offence of wrong conduct, in the Khandhakas, for sleeping on a high bed, and for using bed supports higher than eight standard fingerbreadths.34
    • An offence of wrong conduct for lying down on what belongs to a householder in a layperson's house. The Khandhaka gives the allowance and its exceptions without naming a penalty; the Concise BMC, at Pc 88, lists lying down there as wrong conduct.35​ 36

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he makes it the right height; if he makes it lower than the right height; if he gets one made by another that exceeds the right height and then cuts the legs down before using it; if he is insane; if he is the first offender.37

    Related Offences

    • Pc 86 — a needle case of bone, ivory or horn; the anchor for the making-and-putting-right machinery this rule shares entire.
    • Pc 88 — a bed or bench upholstered with cotton down; the same pair of objects limited by their covering instead of their height, and read with this rule as a pair.
    • Pc 14 — leaving a bed or bench out in the open; the same four construction types define bed and bench there.
    • Sg 6, NP 15, Pc 89, Pc 90, Pc 91, Pc 92 — the other rules measured in standard handspans or fingerbreadths, where the same question about the unit arises.

    Related Suttas

    • DN 1 — among the things for which the world praises the Buddha, that "he avoids using high or wide beds".38

    Upananda the Sakyan (Characters of the Vinaya)

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register, and is short because the source is. Ajahn Brahm's retelling diverges twice: it has Upananda get down from the bed and invite the Buddha to lie on it, which the Vibhaṅga does not say, and it renders the rebuke as a verb of speaking. The Manual, the recitation, the class (session 032, 30 July 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1 with its Appendix II and BMC2's furnishings rules, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC, Ñāṇatusita and Brahmāli's appendices of technical terms and of furniture were all checked, and the Khandhaka passages on high beds and householders' furnishings were read in Brahmāli's translation. The Commentary appears only through Thanissaro's report of it, and the commentarial gloss of the standard fingerbreadth as Brahmāli's appendix quotes it. The conversion of eight standard fingerbreadths to about 40 cm is my arithmetic from the appendix's unit and is declared as such in the text.
