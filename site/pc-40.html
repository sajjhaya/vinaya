    Pc 40

    Story (Vatthu)

    First sub-story. The Buddha was staying near Vesālī, in the hall with the peaked roof in the Great Wood. A monk who used only discarded things was living in a charnel ground. He disliked receiving things from people. Instead he would take whatever was offered to the dead — at the charnel ground, at the foot of trees, at the threshold — and he would use that.1​ 2

    People complained and criticised him, "How can this monk take the offerings to our ancestors and use them? This monk is big and strong. He probably eats human flesh too!"3

    The monks heard the complaints of those people, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised him, "How can this monk eat food that hasn't been given?" The Buddha questioned him, established that it was true, rebuked him, and laid down the rule: a monk who eats food that has not been given commits an offence entailing confession.4​ 5

    Second sub-story. Soon afterwards the monks did not use water or tooth cleaners, because they were afraid of wrongdoing. They told the Buddha. Soon afterwards he gave a teaching and addressed the monks: "Monks, I allow you to use water and tooth cleaners after taking them yourselves." He then restated the rule with the two exceptions in it.6​ 7

    Synopsis — A monk living entirely on what has been thrown away eats the food that families leave out for their dead, and the neighbours draw the obvious conclusion about a large man who feeds at graves. The rule that follows says a monk may eat nothing that has not been given to him; when the monks then stop drinking water and cleaning their teeth, the Buddha adds the two exceptions that keep the rule liveable.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk eats food that has not been given, except for water and tooth cleaners, he commits an offense entailing confession."8

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu consumes unoffered food or drink, except for water, he incurs a pācittiya."9

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu take into his mouth an edible that has not been given—except for water and tooth-cleaning sticks—it is to be confessed."10

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Eating food which hasn't been given is an offence of Pācittiya."11

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should take into the mouth [any] nutriment that has not been given [to bhikkhus]; except water and tooth-wood, [this is a case] involving expiation."12

    The renderings agree on the rule and differ in two places. The Pāli's act is mukhadvāraṃ āhareyya, to convey to the mukhadvāra, the "face-door" in Ñāṇatusita's literal gloss, and Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita keep the mouth in the line, where Brahmāli's two versions both say only "eats" or "consumes".13​ 14​ 15 Nothing changes for this rule, but Pc 37's dispute over what eating is leans on the clause; its weight is taken up below under Effort. The second place is the exception. The Vibhaṅga translation keeps "water and tooth cleaners"; the recitation keeps only water.16 Ñāṇatusita notes that such sticks "are still made in India, and in the Sri Lankan and Thai forest monasteries", and the exception stands in the rule.17

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A mendicant eats only what someone has given him, and this rule makes an act of giving the condition of every mouthful, so that a monk's food always comes to him through another's generosity and never by his own hand, for his own dependence and for the confidence of the people who feed him. The monk in the origin story stole nothing and wanted nothing fine. He ate what families had set out for their dead, and the neighbours drew their own conclusion: "This monk is big and strong. He probably eats human flesh too!"18 No earlier rule reached him, and the one that answers him concerns how a mendicant comes by food, whoever owns it.

    Thanissaro gives the rule two factors, object and effort, and almost all its practical content sits inside the word "given", which the Vibhaṅga defines in one sentence and the Commentary in a treatise.19 I treat the two factors first, and then the giving.

    Object

    The object is "whatever is edible, apart from water and tooth cleaners": in Thanissaro's words, all four classes of food and medicine, so that tonics and medicines must be received as well as meals.20​ 21 Thanissaro adds from the Commentary that water includes snow and hail. Whether boiled or bottled water and ice come under the exception Thanissaro calls a controversial point, on which a monk should follow his Community; Ajahn Brahm proposes the test the Buddha used for sugar mixed with flour: whatever ordinary people call water need not be given.22​ 23​ 24

    Toothpaste raises the same question. Thanissaro would treat it as a medicine to be received, since it contains substances the Canon counts as medicines and is meant to act on the gums.25 Ajahn Brahm takes the other side: to require tooth-sticks and toothpaste to be offered empties the Buddha's allowance of meaning, and he would extend the allowance to anything in the mouth that does not nourish, false teeth included.26 The rule's object is what is eaten, and what a substance does to the gums is not what the definition asks, so I follow him.

    Effort

    The effort is taking the food, which is wrong conduct, and swallowing it, which is a pācittiya at every swallow.27 Perception does not mitigate: a monk who eats food not received commits the full offence whether he knows it, doubts it, or believes it received, and the Commentary's view that belief would at least excuse the taking Thanissaro rejects, since nowhere else does a lesser offence turn on perception where the full one does not.28​ 29 The mouth in the rule's wording is the lips: Thanissaro shows it from Sk 41, where a monk is not to open "the door of the face" before the mouthful reaches it, which cannot be the larynx.30 That is the weight this rule carries in Pc 37's dispute.

    The giving is the rule's centre, and the Vibhaṅga's sentence is the whole canonical definition:

    Given: standing within arm's reach of one giving by body or by what is connected to his body or by releasing, he receives it by body or by what is connected to his body — this is called "given."31

    Three conditions follow, and every modern source sets them out alike. The giver is within arm's reach; the giver gives with the body, with something in contact with it such as a spoon, or by letting go, dropping or tossing the food without contact; and the monk receives with the body or with something in contact with it, his bowl or a cloth.32​ 33 Ajahn Brahm reports the Commentary setting arm's reach at two and a half hattha, running up and down as well as across.34 One canonical amendment sits on top. When rice was spilled at a meal, the Buddha allowed food that falls while being given to be picked up and eaten, "It has been relinquished by the donors."35

    Thanissaro reports the Commentary expanding the three conditions to five: the thing can be lifted by a man of average stature; the giver is within reach; the giver makes a gesture of offering; the giver is a deva, a human being or an animal; and the monk receives by body or by what touches it.36 Two of the five have bred misunderstandings. The first was apparently meant to stop several men lifting a whole table to present it, and it has been read as requiring food to be lifted a certain height, which the Commentary itself contradicts: a small novice who slides a pot of rice along the floor onto a monk's hand has given it.37​ 38 The third has been read as requiring a humble manner, so that some will not accept food from a donor wearing shoes. Ajahn Brahm reports the factor that way and objects that humility belongs to the monk, not the giver; Thanissaro denies that the Commentary means it, since among its own examples is a person riding on a monk's shoulders who picks fruit from a tree and drops it into his hands.39​ 40 The sentence to keep is Thanissaro's: "a bhikkhu should not snatch food that a person happens to be carrying past him without showing any indication that he/she wants him to take the food." Receiving, finally, needs attention. A monk who touches the table meaning to receive has received what is on it; food placed in the hand of a sleeping monk, or one deep in meditation, has not been received; and a monk fed by tube while unconscious breaks no rule, since he is not the one putting the food there.41

    Disputed — whether the Vibhaṅga's three conditions are the rule, or one way of satisfying it.

    The rule says only "not given", and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual observes that whether the Vibhaṅga's factors "were originally the intent behind the rule is unclear".42 In class he went further. Of an airport lounge whose food is provided for exactly the people entitled to be there, he thought a monk who ate it would not fulfil the Vibhaṅga's factors "but I think you're still fulfilling the actual rule", and he would not criticise anyone for eating food "that clearly has been given" without the formal offering, leaving it to each monk to decide.43 Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm entertain no such reading; for both the three conditions are the rule's content.44​ 45 Brahmāli's canonical example, Uppalavaṇṇā taking the meat a bandit hung from a tree at NP 5, is an accepted gift that meets none of the three conditions, though it concerns a nun and does not rule on receiving.46​ 47 I think the formal giving the safer practice, because then the question never has to be decided at the table; but Brahmāli's point is about the rule's own word, which the Vibhaṅga glosses and does not exhaust, and the texts do not settle it.

    The Commentary attaches to this rule a treatise, a long body of practical casuistry, and Thanissaro's framing of it holds: it is "a compilation of the opinions of various teachers", so "the opinions expressed in the treatise are not necessarily normative", and where the texts give no answer a monk should follow his Community within the Canon's framework.48 Ajahn Brahm sets out his own entry in two parts, the rule as the Vinaya has it and as the Commentary enlarged it, so that a monk can see the source of each custom and choose wisely.49 Three of its topics are the ones a monastery argues about. Food already received is not made unreceived merely because a novice or layperson touches it.50​ 51 Lifting unreceived food intending to eat it makes it uggahitaka, on the Commentary's view unusable by anyone even once offered (touching it with that intention bars the toucher alone), a strictness Ajahn Brahm grounds in the Canon; Thanissaro calls the penalty harsher than any nissaggiya pācittiya and reports that many Communities let another monk receive it instead.52​ 53 And something unreceived that falls into received food makes the whole need receiving again unless it is removed, which Thanissaro thinks reasonable for edibles and "taking the concept too far" when the Commentary extends it to dust, rain water or sweat, since the rule covers only what is fit to eat.54​ 55 Ajahn Brahm closes his entry with what the machinery can come to when it is mistaken for the point: a monk in Bangkok who, having eaten food not given, had a strong novice lift him up and formally give his body, food and all, to another monk.56

    An offer, finally, holds for the day it is made. If parents tell a visiting monk to help himself from the refrigerator, Brahmāli said, "that means it is given", but the next day they must offer again, or he would be storing food overnight.57 The mechanics belong to Pc 38. What belongs here is that an offering is something somebody does, not something that stays done.

    Thanissaro answers why so much was written about so small a thing: "the gift of food ranks with sexual temptation as one of the largest issues in a bhikkhu's — or anyone's — life."58 Nothing in picking fruit from a tree is wrong in the world, and the rule does not say it is. What it says is that a mendicant lives by what is given to him, and that the giving has to be an act somebody performs.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — taking food that has not been received, intending to eat it; incurred even if it is never eaten.59
    • Dukkaṭa — where the food has been received but he does not perceive it as such, or is unsure. Where it has not been received, all three perceptions carry the full pācittiya.60

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if it is water or tooth cleaners; if, when there is a reason, but there is no attendant, he himself takes the four foul edibles and eats them; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.61

    The four foul edibles are dung, urine, ash and clay, allowed at Mv.VI.14.6 as an antidote to snakebite; Thanissaro reports the Commentary exempting the monk from the rules on cutting plants and digging earth while he prepares them.62​ 63 Food that falls while being offered may be picked up and eaten.64 The famine allowance to pick up fruit oneself, carry it to an attendant and have it given was withdrawn when the famine ended, among the allowances revoked at Mv.VI.32.2; Thanissaro holds that it can no longer be invoked. Ajahn Brahm still lists it as live; the Concise BMC lists it but records, after Thanissaro, that it cannot be reinstated.65​ 66​ 67​ 68 A monk who finds fruit on a forest path with no one to offer it may carry it only with no thought of eating it himself: picked up to be eaten, it cannot later be made allowable by being received.

    Related Offences

    • Pj 2 — theft, where what "has not been given" is what "belongs to someone else"; here it means what has not been received. The monk of the origin story took nothing from anyone, which is why a second rule was needed.69
    • Pc 37 — the definition of eating, where this rule's mukhadvāraṃ āhareyya does half the work of Brahmāli's position.
    • Pc 38 — storing, which begins where this rule ends: receiving is the act by which food can be stored, and the act that must be repeated when an offer lapses at dawn.
    • Pc 41 — giving food with one's own hand to wanderers, which borrows this rule's definition of giving.
    • NP 5 — the Uppalavaṇṇā story.
    • Sk 41 — not opening the mouth before the mouthful reaches it; Thanissaro's evidence that mukhadvāra is the lips.
    • Pc 4 — the rule whose perception pattern this one follows.
