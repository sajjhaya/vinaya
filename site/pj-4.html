    Pj 4

    Story (Vatthu)

    When the Buddha was staying in the house with the peaked roof in the Great Wood near Vesālī, a number of monks who were friends entered the rains residence on the banks of the river Vaggumudā. The Vajjian country was short of food, its crops blighted, and it was not easy to get by on almsfood. The monks considered how they might have a comfortable rains, live in harmony and get almsfood without trouble. Some proposed working for the householders, and others taking messages for them. Still others proposed that they talk up one another's superhuman qualities to the householders: this monk has the first absorption, that monk the second, this one is a stream-enterer, that one a perfected one, this one has the three true insights, and that one the six direct knowledges. This is what they did.

    The people thought themselves fortunate that such virtuous monks had come to them for the rains. They gave the monks such food and drink that they did not eat themselves, or give to their parents, their wives and children, their servants and workers, their friends or their relatives. Soon the monks had a good colour, bright faces, clear skin and sharp senses.

    When the three months were over, the monks set out for Vesālī to visit the Buddha, as was the custom. The monks who had spent the rains in that region were thin, haggard and pale, with veins protruding all over their bodies; the monks from the Vaggumudā were not. The Buddha asked them how they had got almsfood, and they told him. "But did you really have those superhuman qualities?" "No, sir."

    The Buddha rebuked them: "How could you for the sake of your stomachs talk up one another's superhuman qualities to householders? It would be better for your bellies to be cut open with a sharp butcher's knife than for you to talk up one another's superhuman qualities to householders. Why is that? For although it might cause death or death-like suffering, it would not cause you to be reborn in a bad destination. But this might."

    He then spoke of five notorious gangsters to be found in the world. The first is the bad monk who, like a bandit longing to walk about the villages and towns with a following of a hundred or a thousand men, killing and torturing, longs to walk about them with a following, honoured by lay people and monastics and given robes, almsfood, dwellings and medicines. The second is a bad monk who learns the spiritual path proclaimed by the Buddha and takes it as his own; the third, one who groundlessly charges someone living a pure spiritual life with a sexual offence; the fourth, one who takes the Saṅgha's valuable goods and uses them to bribe householders and gain a following. "But in this world with its gods, lords of death, and supreme beings, in this society with its monastics and brahmins, its gods and humans, this is the most notorious gangster of all: one who claims to have a non-existent superhuman quality. Why is that? Monks, you've eaten the country's almsfood by theft." He added a verse, which ends: "It's better to eat an iron ball, / As hot as a licking flame, / Than for the immoral and uncontrolled / To eat the country's alms." Then he laid down the rule.

    Soon afterwards a number of monks, thinking they had seen and realised what in fact they had not, declared final knowledge because of overestimation. After some time their minds inclined to sensual desire, ill will and confusion, and they became anxious that they had committed an offence entailing expulsion. They told Venerable Ānanda, who told the Buddha. "This is negligible, Ānanda," he said, and amended the rule to except a claim made through overestimation.1

    Synopsis — During a famine a group of monks talk up one another's non-existent attainments to lay supporters, who go hungry to feed them; the Buddha names a false claim to a superhuman quality as the most notorious of five kinds of gangster and lays down the rule, then exempts monks who declare an attainment through honest overestimation.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk falsely claims for himself a superhuman quality, a knowledge and vision worthy of the noble ones, saying, 'This I know, this I see,' but after some time—whether he is questioned or not, but having committed the offense and seeking purification—should say: 'Not knowing I said that I know, not seeing that I see; what I said was empty and false,' then, except if it is due to overestimation, he too is expelled and excluded from the community."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu falsely claims an extraordinary spiritual attainment, except through overestimation, he incurs a pārājika."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu, without direct knowledge, claim a superior human state, a truly noble knowledge and vision, as present in himself, saying, 'Thus do I know; thus do I see,' such that regardless of whether or not he is cross-examined on a later occasion, he—being remorseful and desirous of purification—might say, 'Friends, not knowing, I said I know; not seeing, I said I see—vainly, falsely, idly,' unless it was from over-estimation, he also is defeated and no longer in affiliation."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1, reproducing Thanissaro's rendering in a form that differs from the current BMC1, apparently an earlier printing)

    "Should any monks, without direct knowledge, boast of a superior human state, a truly noble knowledge and vision as present in himself saying, 'Thus I know, thus I see', such that regardless of whether or not he is cross-examined on a later occasion he, being remorseful and desiring purification might say, 'Friends, not knowing I said I knew, not seeing I said I saw, vainly, falsely and idly', unless it was from overestimation, he also is defeated and no longer in communion."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu, [though] not directly knowing [it], should claim a superhuman state pertaining to himself, [a state of] knowing and seeing [that is] suitable for the noble [ones], [saying]: 'Thus I know! Thus I see!,' [and] then, on another occasion, [whether] being interrogated or not being interrogated, having committed [the offence], desiring purification, should say so: 'Friends, [although] not knowing [it,] I spoke thus [saying]: "I know," not seeing [it, I spoke, saying:] "I see." I bluffed vainly [and] falsely,' except [when said] in overestimation, he also is disqualified, not in communion."6

    The four full renderings all give the rule in two halves, a claim and then a later confession that the claim was empty, and a reader might take the offence to wait on the confession. It does not. The word-analysis glosses "after some time" as "the moment, the instant, the second after he has made the claim", and says that the monk who later confesses "has committed an offense entailing expulsion"; the offence is complete in the claim, and a confession neither completes it nor undoes it.7 The recitation line keeps only the first half, and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual explains why: "despite the length of this rule, its essence is simply that a bhikkhu who falsely claims a superhuman state incurs a pārājika."8

    The renderings part on the first word, anabhijānaṁ. Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm render it "without direct knowledge" and Ñāṇatusita "not directly knowing", while Brahmāli writes "falsely". The word-analysis defines it by what the monk does not "see" or "know" in himself, which raises the question whether the offence depends on the fact or on the monk's own view of it, a question discussed below, under Perception. The renderings of uttarimanussadhamma ("superhuman quality", "superior human state") change nothing: Ñāṇatusita reads it as "a state beyond humans", an attainment beyond ordinary human experience rather than membership of a higher kind of being.9

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule protects the holy life's own purpose from being turned into a living, and it gives the lie that does so the heaviest penalty in the Vinaya. Every deliberate lie is already an offence under Pc 1; this rule takes the false claim to a superhuman quality out of that rule, and Thanissaro calls it "one of the most heinous lies a bhikkhu can tell".10 Brahmāli's introduction gives the reason: such claims "subvert the very purpose of the spiritual life for selfish purposes".11 The origin story shows it: the Vaggumudā monks turned the attainments the holy life exists to realise into a means of getting fed, and the people who fed them went hungry. The Buddha's own word for it is theft: "Monks, you've eaten the country's almsfood by theft."12

    The sources do not agree on how to divide the offence. The Manual gives seven factors, following the phrases of the rule, and marks one of them, that "the listener must immediately understand", as the Commentary's.13 Ajahn Brahm arranges his notes under the intention, the action and the result.14 Thanissaro gives four, object, perception, effort and intention, and sets aside the Commentary's fifth, result, as one that "appears to be based on a misreading of the Vibhaṅga".15 I take Thanissaro's four: they leave the question whose understanding counts inside the factor where it arises, and do not settle it in advance with a factor of its own.

    Object

    The Vibhaṅga defines a superhuman quality by a list of ten terms and spells each out: the four absorptions; release, stillness and attainment of emptiness, signlessness and desirelessness; the three true insights; the wings to awakening; the four fruits; the abandoning of desire, ill will and confusion; a mind free of the hindrances; and the delight in solitude that comes of absorption.16 The Commentary sorts these into two families, the enlarged states that rest on absorption and the transcendent states of the paths and fruits, and widens both. The formless attainments count, because they rest on the fourth absorption, and so do the supernormal powers, the divine ear and the reading of minds, which the list does not name.17 The wings to awakening count, on the other hand, only when developed to the level of a transcendent path.18

    The edge of the category is absorption. Divination, protective charms, spells, psychic healing and mediumship do not rest on it, and the discourses class them as tiracchāna-vijjā, "animal knowledge". A monk who lies about his skill at reading palms has told a lie, but not this one.19

    Perception

    The monk must know that his claim is untrue. A monk who honestly but wrongly believes he has an attainment, and declares it, commits no offence under this rule; that is the overestimation of the rule's own exception, and the second story shows how easily it happens.20 If he declares it to a lay person, Pc 8 applies, as it does to a true claim.21

    What counts is the monk's view, not the fact. The word-analysis defines the falsity by what the monk does not see or know in himself, and lists among the marks of the lie that "he misrepresents his view of what is true". Thanissaro concludes that a monk who has reached the first absorption without realising it, and claims it believing the claim to be false, fulfils this factor.22​ 23 Doubt is treated differently. Unlike the Vibhaṅga to Pc 1, the Vibhaṅga here never considers a monk who states an attainment as fact while in doubt about it, and Thanissaro reads its silence as confining the full offence to a monk who knows "without a doubt" that his claim is untrue; the doubtful claim falls back to Pc 1.24

    Effort

    The claim must be made to someone: the word-analysis says he "announces to a woman or a man, to a householder or a monastic".25 Another monk counts, and the Vinītavatthu's monk who told a fellow monk "I've abandoned the fetters" was expelled.26 Words are not needed. A written claim counts, and so does a gesture. In one case monks agreed that whoever set out first from their monastery would be known as a perfected one; one of them, wanting to be taken for one, set out first, and was expelled.27 But there must be someone to hear. Two monks claimed a quality while alone, one rebuked by a monk who could read minds and the other by a god, and each incurred a dukkaṭa, not the pārājika; the Commentary gives the same dukkaṭa for a claim made to a god, a ghost or an animal.28​ 29

    The claim must be about the monk himself. The word-analysis has him either present "those good qualities as in himself" or present "himself as among those good qualities".30 The Vaggumudā monks praised one another, but a false claim about another monk's attainment is not an offence under this rule; Thanissaro places it, as a deliberate lie, under Pc 1, and Ajahn Brahm thinks a pācittiya likely.31 Nor, Thanissaro argues, does a claim about a past life always fall under it. The Commentary confines the rule to the present life, but a stream-entry reached in a past life guarantees stream-entry again in this one, so a false claim to it would fulfil the factor, while a claim to a past-life absorption, which carries no such guarantee, would not.32

    Where the monk names the attainment and only implies himself, the Vibhaṅga does not give the full offence. "The monk living in your dwelling is a perfected one", said by the monk who lived there, and "Those who are pupils of our preceptor are all perfected ones", each said by a monk who "wanted to make a claim", drew a thullaccaya.33 The reverse, where the monk names himself and only implies the attainment, is less settled.

    Disputed — whether a claim that names the monk but only implies the attainment can bring the full offence.

    Thanissaro holds that it can. A statement fulfils the factor if it names the speaker explicitly, while the state may be named or implied; his example is the Vibhaṅga's "delight in an empty dwelling", which he reads as implying the absorption the delight comes from, and he adds that a community's idiom, such as "I have no doubts about the Buddha's teaching" for stream-entry, would count in the same way, provided the implied meaning was intended.34 Ajahn Brahm treats any claim "uncertain as to the achievement" as a hint, a thullaccaya if understood, though he counts an attainment that necessarily implies a superhuman state as a claim to it, so that his line falls at certainty rather than at wording. His case is the Vinītavatthu's sick monk who said that no ordinary person could endure his illness, meaning to be taken for a noble one, and incurred a thullaccaya.35 The Manual requires a claim made "in a direct manner", citing for it the implied-self cases and this same sick monk,36 and teaching the rule he doubted Thanissaro's example on similar grounds, since "other places you only get a thullaccaya for indirect claims", and suggested that the phrase may have been an idiom of the day that amounted to a direct claim.37 His note to the translation says only that the commentaries treat the phrase as an idiom for solitude; the word-analysis, however, defines it as the delight that comes "because of the first absorption", so it may name an attainment rather than hint at one.38 I lean toward the narrower reading, since the only case in which the Vinītavatthu judges an intended hint at an attainment, as Ajahn Brahm counts them, gives the lesser penalty, while the nearest case on the other side, a monk expelled for leaving a rains residence first under an agreement that whoever did so would be known as perfected, depends on a meaning the community had fixed in advance, which is nearer Thanissaro's idiom than a hint; and a monk needs no ruling to see that an idiom his hearers would take as a claim is better left unsaid. But the lean rests on a single case and on the reading of one phrase, and that is too little to settle it.

    We now come to the question that Pc 8 leaves to this rule. In two of its series the Vibhaṅga lets the penalty depend on understanding. One is the implied self just described. The other is the slip of the tongue: a monk who means to claim the first absorption and names the second instead commits the full offence "if the listener understands", in Brahmāli's rendering, and a thullaccaya if not.39

    Disputed — whether the understanding that decides these cases is the listener's or the speaker's.

    The Pāli reads sampajānamusā bhaṇantassa paṭivijānantassa āpatti pārājikassa, and the participle paṭivijānantassa, "understanding", names no one. The Commentary takes it as the listener's and extends it to every claim under the rule. The listener need not know what an absorption is, nor believe a word of it, but must hear the claim clearly enough to know that it is one, and a passer-by who overhears it will do. A claim no one hears is a dukkaṭa, and one half-heard, or worked out only after reflection, a thullaccaya.40​ 41 Thanissaro reads it as the speaker's, on grammar. Where the Vibhaṅga makes a listener's understanding count, as for giving up the training and at Sg 3, it gives the listener a different case, whereas here the participle agrees with the one "speaking", and the listener is "nowhere mentioned in the passage". On his reading a monk who slips without noticing has told no conscious lie and incurs a thullaccaya, and one who is aware of what he says incurs the pārājika.42 The Manual agrees: the two participles more likely refer to one person than to two, and the thullaccaya "for one who does not recognize/understand" must be the claimant's, since the offence is his.43 His translation of the Vibhaṅga, however, renders the participle "if the listener understands", and his note gives the reason. The grammar allows either reading, but "since the speaker knows he is lying, he understands the overall meaning. It follows that the understanding here must refer to the listener." He cites the same two places Thanissaro does, giving up the training and Sg 3, as instances of the same verb used of the listener.44 So the same scholar stands on both sides in print, and the two arguments read the same passages in opposite ways, the one from the difference in grammatical form, the other from the sameness of the verb. Each meets the other only in part: Thanissaro answers the logical objection by observing that a monk who slips may not hear his own words, and Brahmāli's note answers the grammar by allowing the participle a second construction. Brahmāli's own two positions show how evenly the passage is balanced, and I do not choose between them.

    What is settled is narrower, and more useful. Being believed is irrelevant on every reading: the Commentary, which makes the listener decisive, says so, and on the speaker's reading the listener does not enter at all.45 The disagreement decides only the penalty for a slip, a hint and a claim that does not reach its hearer. A monk who states a false attainment outright, to someone who hears him clearly, has committed a pārājika on every reading there is.

    Intention

    The claim must be a deliberate lie, and the Vibhaṅga makes sure of it. Every ruling in its series requires that he knows before he speaks that he is going to lie, knows while speaking that he is lying, and knows afterwards that he has lied, and adds, case by case, that he misrepresents his view, belief, acceptance or sentiment of what is true.46 The Manual's factor is "deliberately lying, aiming to deceive", and the Commentary treats the first knowing as essential: a lie the monk notices only as he speaks, or after, counts as a slip of the tongue.47​ 48 And so a careless phrase does not cost a monk his monkhood. The word-analysis names the motive, "bad desires",49 and the Commentary defines it as the wish to have others believe that skilful states not present in oneself are there. A self-deprecating joke about attainments one does not have, not meant to be taken seriously, lacks it and is not this offence, though as a falsehood it still falls under Pc 1, and Thanissaro warns that such jokes show a careless disrespect for the attainments a monk should hold highest.50 Ajahn Brahm's own list of statements that are no offence includes saying "I am enlightened" as "a mere example, such as in a book explaining the Vinaya".51

    The Vinītavatthu then gives a run of cases in which the words came near a claim and the intention did not. Monks who were ill were asked by their companions whether they had any superhuman qualities, a question put, Thanissaro explains, so that a monk with an attainment could focus his mind on it and one without could strive for it before his illness worsened. One answered that it is not difficult to declare final knowledge, another that such qualities are attained by the energetic; told not to be afraid, one said that he was not afraid of death, and another that one who is remorseful might be. Monks pressed by their relatives to come home said that one like them could not live at home, or that they enjoyed the highest enjoyment. Each became anxious afterwards that he had made a claim, and to each the Buddha said that there is no offence for one who does not intend to make a claim.52 The series closes on a pair. Asked whether he was bearing up, one sick monk said, "It's not possible for just anyone to endure this", and had no offence. Another said, "It's not possible for an ordinary person to endure this", and, asked what he had been thinking, answered, "I intended to make a claim, sir": a thullaccaya.53 The words are almost the same; everything depends on what lay behind them.

    Ajahn Brahm draws the practical lesson. A monk put on the spot by faithful lay people asking about his attainments often answers vaguely, and usually there is no offence, because his aim is to raise a cloud of uncertainty, neither conveying that he has attained anything nor that he has not.54 The same holds when the lay people do the claiming. Brahmins who invited monks with "Perfected sirs, please come" left them anxious about accepting, and the Buddha said that there is no offence when something is spoken in faith.55

    The Vinītavatthu reaches the wish for esteem itself. A monk who lived in the wilderness, walked for alms, or walked, stood, sat or lay down in meditation because he wanted people to esteem him was not expelled, but each such practice undertaken for that wish is a dukkaṭa.56 Lest the ruling discourage the practices themselves, the Commentary lists blameless reasons for forest life: a mind made restless by the village, the wish for seclusion, the aim of arahantship, the Buddha's praise of the wilderness, and a good example to one's fellows.57 The case studies close with the opposite figure. When Mahāmoggallāna's fellow monks complained that he was claiming a superhuman ability, the Buddha confirmed what Moggallāna had seen, and said that he had seen it himself, "but I didn't speak about it. If I had, others wouldn't have believed me, which would have led to their harm and suffering for a long time."58 Brahmāli draws from these cases a lesson for the hearer as well as the speaker: one should be careful "with jumping to the conclusion that such claims are false".59

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Thullaccaya — a false claim that names the attainment and implies the self, where the listener understands; a hint at an attainment meant as a claim; a slip that names another attainment where the listener does not understand (on Thanissaro's reading, where the monk does not notice his slip).60​ 61​ 62
    • Dukkaṭa — the implied-self claim where the listener does not understand (on Thanissaro's reading, where the monk is unaware of the implication); a claim made in private, and on the Commentary's reading one made to a god, a ghost or an animal.63​ 64
    • Dukkaṭa — living in the wilderness, walking for alms, or walking, standing, sitting or lying down in meditation in order to be esteemed.65
    • Pācittiya — a false claim about another monk's attainment; a definite claim to an attainment one is in doubt about; a joke claiming an attainment (all Pc 1).66​ 67​ 68

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he overestimates himself, if he does not intend to make a claim, if he is insane, if he is deranged, if he is overwhelmed by pain, or if he is the first offender.69 The first two are this rule's own, and are treated under Perception and Intention. A true claim made to another monk is no offence at all: the Vinītavatthu's last cases are Mahāmoggallāna's and Sobhita's, each accused by his fellows of claiming a superhuman ability, and each time the Buddha says that he "spoke truthfully".70

    Related Offences

    • Pc 1 — deliberate lying, under which a false claim about another monk's attainment, a doubtful claim stated as fact, and a joke about one's own attainment all fall.
    • Pc 8 — telling an unordained person of an attainment one actually has. There the claim is true and the fault lies in telling it; here the claim is false.
    • Sg 8 — groundlessly charging a monk with an offence entailing expulsion, which reaches the third gangster of this rule's origin story and a groundless charge of a false claim alike.
    • Display of psychic power (Kd 15) — showing a superhuman power to lay people for the sake of support, which the Buddha compares to "a sex worker showing her private parts for the sake of money".71

    Related Suttas

    • MN 105 — taught in the same hall with the peaked roof: of monks who declared final knowledge, some did so rightly and some through overestimation, and for the second the Buddha thinks, "I should teach them the Dhamma."72
    • SN 17.5 — the monk whose "mind is overcome and obsessed by gain, honour, and praise", the motive behind the Vaggumudā monks' scheme.73

    43. Brahmāli, Pātimokkha Manual, Pārājika 4, Commentary on Rule Factors (2): "I think Ajahn Ṭhānissaro is right when he says that understanding on the part of the listener is not an issue. Bhaṇantassa ('for the one who is speaking') and paṭivijānantassa ('for the one who understands'; see III 97,29-30) are more likely to refer to the same person than to different people. Moreover, the natural meaning of na paṭivijānantassa āpatti thullaccayassa (III 97,30-31) is 'there is an offence of thullaccaya for one who does not recognize/understand'. Since the offence mentioned here refers to the person making the claim, this cannot refer to the listener."↩︎

    44. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pj 4, endnote to the permutations: "'If the listener understands' renders paṭivijānantassa. This is in accordance with Sp 1.21[5] … Grammatically paṭivijānantassa could refer to either the speaker or the listener … but logically it seems it must refer to the listener. In accordance with common usage, 'understanding' (paṭivijānantassa) must refer to understanding the overall meaning of what is said, not to knowing the exact words that have been spoken. Since the speaker knows he is lying, he understands the overall meaning. It follows that the understanding here must refer to the listener." He cites the passage on giving up the training in Pārājika 1, where the verb "clearly refers to understanding on the part of the listener", and Saṅghādisesa 3, where the aorist paṭivijāni "again refers to the listener".↩︎
