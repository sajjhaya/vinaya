    Pc 11

    Story (Vatthu)

    At one time the Buddha was staying at Āḷavī, at the Aggāḷava Shrine. The monks of Āḷavī were doing building work, and they cut down trees and had them cut down. When one of them was cutting down a tree, the deity who lived in it said to him, "Venerable, don't cut down our dwelling because you want to build a dwelling for yourself." Taking no heed, he cut it down anyway, and in doing so he injured the arm of that deity's child.

    The deity thought, "Why don't I just kill this monk?" But then it reconsidered: "It wouldn't be right to just kill this monk. Let me instead tell the Buddha about this matter." It approached the Buddha and told him what had happened. "Well done, deity," the Buddha said. "It's good that you didn't kill that monk. If you had killed that monk, you would have made much demerit. The tree over there is empty. Take that as your dwelling."

    The people complained: "How can the Sakyan monastics cut down trees and have them cut down? They are hurting life with one sense." The monks heard the complaint, and the monks of few desires complained in turn to the Buddha. He asked the monks of Āḷavī whether it was true, and they admitted it was. He rebuked them: "People regard trees as conscious. This will affect people's confidence…" He then laid down the rule.

    Synopsis — The building monks of Āḷavī cut down trees, one of them ignoring a tree-deity's plea and injuring its child; the deity chooses to report the matter to the Buddha rather than kill the monk, and after the local people complain that the monks are harming one-sense life, the Buddha lays down a rule against destroying plant life.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk destroys a plant, he commits an offense entailing confession."1

    Condensed recitation translation — "Intentionally destroying vegetation, or having it destroyed, is a pācittiya."2

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "The damaging of a living plant is to be confessed."3

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Destroying or damaging plants is an offence of Pācittya."4

    Ñāṇatusita

    "In the destroying of vegetation, [there is a case] involving expiation."5

    The five renderings agree, and the recitation builds in two things the full rule leaves to its analysis: intentionally, from the non-offence clauses, and or having it destroyed, from the permutations, where cutting, breaking and cooking count whether a monk does them himself or has them done.6​ 7 One word asks for a closer look, bhūtagāma. Thanissaro renders it "living plant" and notes that it literally means "the home of a being", citing the Sub-commentary's explanation: deities may take up residence in standing plants.8 Ñāṇatusita rejects that etymology: gāma here means a kind or collection, as in mātugāma, womankind, at Sg 2, so that the compound means "vegetation", and it "does not mean 'habitation of a being.'"9 The choice leaves the rule's reach untouched; that is set by the Vibhaṅga's definition, discussed below under Object.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A monk leaves living plants unharmed under this rule, so that the people who support him never see him harm what they hold to be alive. The Story gives the reason in the Buddha's rebuke: "People regard trees as conscious. This will affect people's confidence." The deity in the tree shows the same matter from the other side. Asked not to cut down its home, the monk took no heed; it was the deity, wronged and with the power to kill, that held back, and the Buddha praised it for doing so. As Thanissaro reads the origin story, the rule was laid down "to prevent bhikkhus from offending people who held to the animist belief that regarded plants as one-facultied life", and so it binds a monk whatever he himself believes about plants.10

    Thanissaro gives the offence four factors: object, effort, perception and intention.11 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual groups the same ground under two heads, the destroying and the vegetation, with intention and hinting under the first and perception and seeds under the second.12 Thanissaro's four serve better here, since they keep perception and intention apart, as the Vibhaṅga's own permutations and non-offence clauses do.

    Object

    The Vibhaṅga defines a plant as vegetation from any of five kinds of propagation: from roots (turmeric, ginger), from stems (the Bodhi tree, the banyan, the cluster fig), from joints (sugarcane, bamboo, reed), from cuttings (shrubby basil and the like), and from seeds (grains and vegetables).13 Thanissaro reports a second category from the Commentary. A plant or part of one removed from where it grew is no longer bhūtagāma, standing plant life; if it could grow again when put in the ground, it is bījagāma, "home of a seed", and a sown seed stays bījagāma until its first shoot turns green and its first leaf appears.14​ 15

    Disputed — whether destroying a seed, or a cutting still able to grow, is the full offence or only a dukkaṭa.

    One reading gives a dukkaṭa. The Vibhaṅga says nothing of bījagāma, and the Commentary, in Thanissaro's account, takes the lesser penalty from the suttas' praise of a monk who refrains from harming both seed and plant, read through the Cullavagga's rule against bad habits; Ajahn Brahm follows it, and Thanissaro finds it partial support in the Canon.16​ 17 Brahmāli reads the rule as reaching seeds at the full offence. The Vibhaṅga's own permutations, he points out, state the pācittiya with the word bīja just where one would expect bhūtagāma, so that "it is difficult to avoid the feeling that seeds are included in this rule", and six of the ten other schools whose Vinayas survive name bījagāma in the rule itself.18 On the first reading, crushing a viable seed is a dukkaṭa; on the second, it is the same offence as felling a tree. No text settles it; Brahmāli's own exception for eating, discussed below under Intention, is what keeps the stricter reading livable.

    The lowest forms of plant life raise a softer question. Ajahn Brahm sets out two readings and declines to choose. The stricter takes Buddhaghosa's standard, anything green and leafed, and, read by modern biology, it would make "scrubbing moss off a slippery path" a pācittiya; the more lenient leaves the lesser forms out, since the Story and the five kinds both have the larger plants in view. The Vinaya, he says, "does not extend this far", and each community must find its own middle way.19 By Thanissaro's report the Commentary does set aside one narrow band: mold on a wall, algae in a water jar or fungus on a toothbrush is neither kind of plant, and a monk may wipe it away as he would any other dirt.20

    Effort

    The permutations name the act: cutting, breaking or cooking a plant, or having another do it.21 Thanissaro gives the Commentary's reading of the act, and it is very broad: "dealing with a plant as one likes", so that picking a flower or a leaf, uprooting a plant, carving one's initials in a trunk, or even shaking a branch to bring down its dry leaves all count.22 Thanissaro draws the consequence that pruning is included too, since no exception is made for a kindly intention toward the plant, and so is killing plants with herbicide.23 Ajahn Brahm notes that some monks hold pruning allowable because it helps the plant, and leaves the question open.24 A plant's base travels with it: a water plant whose roots do not reach the soil has the water as its base, so lifting it out damages it, and an orchid or mistletoe has its tree, so pulling it off is a pācittiya.25

    A monk who needs plants cleared may still say so, provided he does not give the order. The Vibhaṅga lets him say what is needed, "There's need for this", without saying what is to be done.26 Thanissaro's examples are "This branch is in the way" and "Look at how long the grass is", or asking for flowers without saying which are to be picked; Ajahn Brahm adds that a request indefinite as to which plant, "Fell a tree", "Cut some grass", is likewise no offence.27​ 28 This is the allowable expression, kappiya-vohāra, of Pc 10, and the same discipline governs it here.

    Perception

    The permutations ask whether the plant can still grow. Destroying one that can, while perceiving that it cannot, is no offence; destroying it while unsure is a dukkaṭa; and treating what cannot grow as though it could, or being unsure of it, is a dukkaṭa as well.29​ 30

    Intention

    The act must be meant. There is no offence if it is unintentional, if he is not mindful or if he does not know.31 Thanissaro gives the cases: pulling at grass absent-mindedly while talking, uprooting it while raking leaves, or "grabbing onto a plant for support while climbing a hill and inadvertently uprooting it".32 Brahmāli marks where the line falls with a case of his own. A monk who cuts off a branch because it may damage a building wants to save the building and has no wish to harm the tree, yet he still intends the cut; that "sub-intention directly linked to the action" is enough for the offence.33

    That leaves the most practical question the rule raises: how a monk may eat fruit and vegetables at all, when their seeds are living. Brahmāli answers through intention. A monk eating a fruit with seeds means to eat the fruit, and "there is no separate action apart from the eating that constitutes the destruction of seeds", so there is no offence; nor in eating seeds served as food, which are not able to grow in that state and which he means only to eat.34 The Commentary's answer, which Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm both report, is a ceremony. The monk says kappiyaṃ karohi, "make it allowable", and an unordained person damages the fruit symbolically, a knife drawn across the skin, a fingernail, a touch of fire, saying it is allowable; one piece so treated makes a whole heap allowable, and fruit that is seedless, or whose seeds come away whole, needs no ceremony.35​ 36 Brahmāli observes that the Cullavagga passage giving the five ways sets no penalty for not following them.37 The two answers bring a monk to the same meal by different roads: one finds no offence to begin with, the other lifts one that would otherwise stand.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • A dukkaṭa for destroying bījagāma, a seed or detached plant matter still able to grow, on the Commentary's reading, which Ajahn Brahm follows, Thanissaro finds partly supported, and Brahmāli rejects for the full pācittiya (see the Disputed passage above).38​ 39
    • A dukkaṭa for destroying a plant able to grow while unsure whether it is.40
    • A dukkaṭa for treating what cannot grow as though it could, or being unsure whether it can, and so destroying it.41

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence for destroying a plant unintentionally, unmindfully or unknowingly; for hinting at a need without giving the order; for telling an unordained person to make an item allowable; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.42 Beyond the Vibhaṅga's list, a monk may light a counter-fire against an approaching brush fire without penalty under this rule (Cv V.32.1), though Thanissaro reports the Commentary as having an unordained person light it where one is present, and as letting the allowance lapse where water alone will put the fire out; and Thanissaro cites the Sub-commentary to NP 6 for a monk whose robes have been snatched away and who can find no other covering: he may pick grass and leaves to cover himself.43

    Related Offences

    • Pc 10 — the preceding rule, on digging the earth. Both origin stories voice the same complaint, that the monks were hurting life with one sense, and together, as Thanissaro observes there, the two rules effectively prevent a monk from engaging in agriculture.
    • Sg 13 — Ajahn Brahm sets pruning beside the gardening of the monks of Kīṭāgiri, censured in that rule's origin story, whether or not pruning is an offence under this one.

    Related Suttas

    • DN 1 — the restraint the rule asks of a monk, praised in the Buddha himself: "The ascetic Gotama is a refrainer from damaging seeds and crops", and he refrains from destroying "such seeds as are propagated from roots, from stems, from joints, from cuttings, from seeds", the Vibhaṅga's own five kinds.44

    A note on sourcing

    The Story, the rule, the five kinds of propagation, the permutations and the non-offence clauses are from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation, and the recitation line from his Pātimokkha recitation translation. The Manual gives his reading that seeds fall under the rule, the branch-and-building case and the treatment of eating through intention. Thanissaro's BMC1 supplies the four factors, the Commentary's bījagāma and its dukkaṭa, its broad reading of damaging, the plants based in water and on trees, the lower growths that count as neither, the ceremony for making fruit allowable, and the counter-fire and NP 6 allowances, the commentarial points all cited through him, with the counter-fire's qualification from his BMC2; the commentaries were not opened. Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes confirms the bījagāma distinction and the ceremony, and supplies the question of the lesser plant forms, the pruning question and the indefinite request. The Concise BMC was checked; beyond BMC1 it adds that qualification and a rescue case, which is not carried. The class transcript (session 022, 3 May 2012) was read for its questions, which concern eating seeds and the ceremony; the Manual answers both in writing, and the class is not cited. Ñāṇatusita gives the rule translation and the grammar of bhūtagāma. DN 1 is quoted in Walshe's translation.
