    Pc 42

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. Venerable Upananda the Sakyan said to his brother's student, "Come, let's go to the village for alms." Then, without getting him any food, he sent him away, saying, "Go away! I'm not comfortable talking or sitting with you, but only if I talk and sit by myself."

    But since the right time for eating was coming to an end, the student was unable to walk for alms. As he returned to the monastery, there was nobody offering food, and so he missed his meal.

    He then went to the monastery and told the monks what had happened. The monks of few desires complained and criticised Upananda, "How could Venerable Upananda say to a monk, 'Come, let's go to the village for alms,' and then send him away without getting him any food?" They told the Buddha, who questioned Upananda, established that it was true, rebuked him, and stated the rule.1​ 2

    Synopsis — Upananda takes his brother's pupil on almsround, sends him away before the young monk has been given anything, and tells him why: he would rather talk and sit alone. The young monk gets back too late to eat, and goes without his meal for the day. The rule that follows concerns what Upananda wanted the solitude for.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk says to a monk, 'Come, let's go to the village or town for alms,' and then, whether he has had food given to him or not, sends him away, saying, 'Go away! I'm not comfortable talking or sitting with you, but only if I talk and sit by myself,' and he does so only for this reason and no other, he commits an offense entailing confession."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu takes a bhikkhu on almsround, but then tells him to leave because he wants to misbehave, he incurs a pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu say to a bhikkhu, 'Come, my friend, let's enter the village or town for alms,' and then—whether or not he has had (food) given to him—dismiss him, saying, 'Go away, my friend. I don't like sitting or talking with you. I prefer sitting or talking alone'—doing it for just that reason and no other—it is to be confessed."5

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "While on almsround with another monk, sending him back with the intention to misbehave and not be seen is an offence of Pacittiya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should say so to a bhikkhu, 'Come friend! We shall enter a village or town for alms,' [then after] having had [food] given or not having had [food] given to him, should he dismiss [the bhikkhu saying], 'Go friend! There is no ease for me talking or sitting down together with you; there is ease for me talking or sitting down by myself;' having made just this the reason, [and] not another, [this is a case] involving expiation."7

    The full renderings agree. Ñāṇatusita's "should say so" follows a reading the editions divide over; nothing in the rule depends on it.8 The recitation drops "whether he has had food given to him or not". It parts from the full renderings over the motive, and there the difference matters. Its monk sends his companion away "because he wants to misbehave", which is the word-analysis's motive; the rule's own words give only the quoted speech, a wish to talk and sit alone.9​ 10​ 11 Read by themselves, those words would make this a rule about sociability, and Intention, below, shows why it is not.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A monk who wants his companion out of the way so that he can be alone with a woman has already chosen what he means to hide, and this rule guards his conduct at that first step, before anything has happened that another rule would reach. Upananda's words to the young monk are the rule's own, and on their face they are only unkind: he would rather talk and sit alone. The young monk got back too late to eat and went without his meal, and the Buddha's rebuke was that such conduct "will affect people's confidence".12 But neither the hunger nor the rudeness decides the offence, and the rule does not forbid a monk to want to be alone. The purpose of the solitude decides it, and only the definitions give that.

    Thanissaro gives four factors, object, intention, effort and result, and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual includes among its five factors the setting, which is the rule's one live dispute and is taken last.13​ 14

    Object

    The object is another monk. Dismissing anyone not fully ordained, which here includes a nun, is an offence of wrong conduct, and Ajahn Brahm gives the practical case of a novice or layperson sent away so as not to witness one's misbehaviour.15​ 16 Perception does not mitigate in either direction: a monk is grounds for the pācittiya however the offender perceives him, and an unordained person for the wrong conduct, the pattern Thanissaro calls three pācittiyas and three dukkaṭas.17​ 18

    Intention

    This is the whole rule. The Vibhaṅga's definition of sends away is a list of what the monk wants: to laugh with a woman, to enjoy himself with her, to sit down in private with her, or to misbehave with her.19 Thanissaro reads the last as a catch-all, any misbehaviour of any sort.20 The story does not say what Upananda wanted; that the dismissal was for the sake of a woman the reader learns from the definitions. The clause "only for this reason and no other" then seals it: a dismissal with any proper purpose falls outside the rule.21 The non-offence clauses make no exception for women who are relatives, and need none, for the motive the rule names is the same whoever the woman is.22

    Effort

    The effort is dismissing him, outright or, as Thanissaro adds, by remarks meant to make him leave.23 The Vibhaṅga has its own term for the second way, kalisāsanaṁ āropeti, "puts him down", and gives it its own offence of wrong conduct.24 Brahmāli's endnote translates the Commentary's explanation: anger finding fault with how the other stands, sits and looks about, "like a post … like a dog … like a monkey", in the hope that he will leave in a huff.25 Thanissaro thinks such speech would more likely come under Pc 2, on insulting speech; but the Vibhaṅga makes driving a companion off this way a wrong conduct of this rule too, so a monk who did it in order to be alone with a woman would answer under both.26

    Result

    The offence is complete when the other monk has gone beyond sight or hearing. Before that, the words of dismissal are wrong conduct, and so is his passing beyond.27 Thanissaro gives the Commentary's range: twelve cubits, about six metres, or less where a wall or door stands nearer.28

    Disputed — whether the rule reaches beyond the almsround.

    The rule names one setting, two monks going into a village or town for alms. Thanissaro holds that the setting is only the occasion: the non-offence clauses give no exemption to a monk who dismisses another elsewhere in order to misbehave, and the commentaries, he notes, reasonably leave the setting out of the factors, so the offence can be committed anywhere and at any time of day.29 The Manual makes the setting a factor, "In an inhabited area", and Ajahn Brahm puts the almsround into his rule line.30​ 31 The difference is smaller than it looks. Gāma, Brahmāli argues in his Appendix I, means any inhabited area, even "a single house or a caravan stationary for an extended period".32 What is left between them is chiefly a monastery in the wilderness, where on Thanissaro's reading the rule applies and on Brahmāli's it does not. I find Thanissaro's argument from the non-offence clauses the stronger; but the Manual's factor is read off the rule's own words, and the texts leave the question there.

    Wherever the line falls, the rule asks a monk one question at the moment he sends a companion off: what the solitude is for.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — speaking the words of dismissal, and again while the other is passing beyond sight or hearing.33
    • Dukkaṭa — dismissing one who is not fully ordained, on any perception.34
    • Dukkaṭa — kalisāsanaṁ āropeti, driving him off by disparagement, whether he is fully ordained or not.35​ 36

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he sends him away thinking, "Together we won't get enough"; if he sends him away thinking, "If he sees these valuable goods, he'll become greedy"; if he sends him away thinking, "If he sees this woman, he'll become lustful"; if he sends him away saying, "Take congee or a meal or fresh food or cooked food to the one who is sick or to the one who is left behind or to the one who is guarding the dwellings"; if he does not want to misbehave; if he sends him away when there is something to be done; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.37

    Three of these protect the monk who is sent away rather than the one who sends him, sparing him a lean almsround, the sight of goods he might covet, and the sight of a woman who might unsettle him; a fourth turns the parting into an errand for a monk who could not come. Between them they are a short account of the good reasons for parting on almsround, and Ajahn Brahm states the principle: "When one sends one's companion back for any other reason there is no offence."38

    Related Offences

    • Pc 2 — insulting speech, which applies as well where a companion is driven off by disparagement.
    • Pc 44, Pc 45 — sitting in private with a woman; the dismissal in this rule usually prepares for it.
    • Pc 43 — the next rule, with the same offender.
