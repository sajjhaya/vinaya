    Pc 72

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time the Buddha gave many talks about the Monastic Law, spoke in praise of the Monastic Law and of learning the Monastic Law, and repeatedly praised Venerable Upāli.1

    When the monks heard this, they thought, "Well then, let's learn the Monastic Law from Venerable Upāli." And many monks, both senior and junior, as well as those of middle standing, learned the Monastic Law from Upāli.2

    The monks from the group of six considered this and thought, "If these monks become well-versed in the Monastic Law, they'll boss us around as they like. So let's disparage the Monastic Law."3

    They went to the other monks and said, "What's the point of reciting these minor training rules, when they just lead to anxiety, oppression, and annoyance?"4

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised them: "How can the monks from the group of six disparage the Monastic Law?" They told the Buddha, who questioned them and rebuked them: "Foolish men, how can you do this? This will affect people's confidence."5

    Synopsis — The Buddha's praise of the Monastic Law and of Ven. Upāli sent monks of every seniority to study under him; the group of six, having worked out what a Saṅgha of Vinaya experts would mean for them, set out to talk the subject down instead, and the Buddha made the talking-down an offence. The words they chose have outlasted them: the lesser and minor training rules are the rules the Buddha allowed the Saṅgha to abolish after his death, and the tradition has never settled which ones they are.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "When the Monastic Code is being recited, if a monk says, 'What's the point of reciting these minor training rules, when they just lead to anxiety, oppression, and annoyance?' then in disparaging the training rules, he commits an offense entailing confession."6

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu criticises the pātimokkha rules, he incurs a pācittiya."7

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu, when the Pāṭimokkha is being recited, say, 'Why are these lesser and minor training rules recited when they lead only to anxiety, bother, and confusion?' the criticism of the training rules is to be confessed."8

    Brahm

    "Disparaging the Vinaya by saying, 'Why bother with the lesser and minor rules? They are more trouble than they are worth,' or words similar to this, is an offence of Pācittiya."9

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu, when the Disciplinary Code is being recited, should say so, 'But why these small and minute training precepts that are recited? They just lead to worry, annoyance, [and] discomfort.' In the disparaging of training precepts, [there is a case] involving expiation."10

    The five renderings agree on the offence and part over how much of the rule to print. Brahmāli's recitation reduces it to a single short sentence, dropping both the quoted speech and the clause "when the Monastic Code is being recited".11 Whether the rule needs a recitation at all is taken up under Effort. The other difference is in the phrase the group of six chose. Khuddānukhuddakehi sikkhāpadehi is "these minor training rules" in Brahmāli, "these lesser and minor training rules" in Thanissaro, "the lesser and minor rules" in Ajahn Brahm, and "these small and minute training precepts" in Ñāṇatusita, whose analysis shows a compound of two words, khudda and anukhuddaka, which Brahmāli's single English folds into one.12 What the phrase covers is discussed under Object, and the choice of rendering does not change it.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule guards the study of the Monastic Law: a monk who knows it can keep it, and a Saṅgha whose members know it can hold one another to it. The group of six saw both consequences at once — "they'll boss us around as they like" — and set out to talk the study down before it took hold.13 Study is the means by which a monk comes to know what he has undertaken, and by which the training survives him. The Buddha's rebuke, "This will affect people's confidence", adds what is lost outside the Saṅgha when monks are heard belittling their own discipline. What the rule does not forbid is finding a rule hard, or saying so, or arguing in good faith about one; it is the campaign against the training that the group of six ran, and the motive behind it, that it takes away.

    Thanissaro gives three factors — effort, object and intention — and they are taken in that order.14

    Effort

    The Vibhaṅga does not define disparaging; it gives examples. Beyond the sentence in the rule, it lists the harm study is said to do (those who learn this will be anxious, oppressed, annoyed), the harm not studying is said to spare, and four things "better left" undone — unrecited, unlearned, unstudied, unmastered — and it closes with a wish: "May the Monastic Law disappear, or may these monks remain ignorant."15 Thanissaro notices that the last sentence sounds less like a criticism than a motive for one.16 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual takes the list as examples of a wider act: saying "that one or more rules are not worth keeping, or discouraging their study."17

    Does it have to be said while the Pātimokkha is being recited? The rule says so, but each source that addresses the question reads the rule more widely than the ceremony, though not all equally widely. The Vibhaṅga's own gloss of the clause already includes private study — "when reciting it, when having it recited, or when practicing it" — and Ñāṇatusita, comparing it with the narrower gloss of the same phrase at Pc 73, concludes that this rule "includes unofficial study situations".18​ 19 Thanissaro goes further: the non-offence clauses give no allowance for criticising the discipline at other times, and he gives the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī as not making the recitation a factor, so the effort is complete whenever the criticism is made.20 The Manual holds the clause irrelevant to the offence, and Ajahn Brahm, who reads the Vibhaṅga as restricting it to recitation or study, argues that disparagement deserves the pācittiya at any time anyway.21​ 22 On Thanissaro's reading and the Manual's, then, the rule reaches a monk who runs the group of six's campaign in a corridor; the recitation clause records where the campaign was actually run.

    Object

    The pācittiya falls when the hearer is a fully ordained monk, whatever the speaker thinks about his hearer's ordination. Saying the same things to a novice or a layperson is an offence of wrong conduct.23​ 24 What is disparaged is "the training rules", which the Manual takes to be any rule in the Pātimokkha.25 The group of six's own phrase, "the lesser and minor training rules", has a history of its own: it is the phrase in which the Buddha allowed the Saṅgha, if it wished, to abolish such rules after his death; Ānanda did not ask which they were, the elders of the First Council could not agree, and Mahākassapa's motion that nothing be abolished carried.26 Ajahn Brahm takes the pācittiya to cover the lesser and minor rules and gives wrong conduct for disparaging the others, an offence he finds uncertain too; his conclusion is the practical one: whether the offence is a pācittiya or wrong conduct, a monk should never speak in dispraise of the Vinaya or its study.27

    The permutations add a second offence of wrong conduct, for disparaging aññaṁ dhammaṁ, and the translators mean different things by it. Brahmāli renders it "some other rule", which on the Manual's view that the pācittiya covers the Pātimokkha rules would leave the wrong-conduct offence protecting the Khandhakas.28​ 29 Thanissaro renders it "any other Dhamma" and means the rest of the Canon, noting at Pc 54 that the Vibhaṅga's standing way of naming the suttas and mātikās is to say "another Dhamma".30​ 31 Ajahn Brahm stands partly with each: he gives wrong conduct for disparaging Dhamma, and, as above, for the rules other than the lesser and minor.32 I follow Thanissaro here, for three reasons. The rule's own non-offence clause names that body of teaching, recommending "discourses or verses or philosophy" before the Vinaya. The Vibhaṅga had aññaṁ sikkhāpadaṁ available if it meant another rule. And the phrase stands only twice more in the whole bhikkhus' Vibhaṅga, both times at Pc 21, where Brahmāli himself renders it "another teaching".33​ 34 So a monk who tells another monk the suttas are not worth studying has committed an offence of wrong conduct, and one who says it of the Monastic Law, a pācittiya. I do not call Brahmāli's reading wrong; it is coherent in itself, but it is not the one that makes the Vibhaṅga consistent with itself.

    Intention

    The monk must mean to disparage. Thanissaro gives the Commentary's account of what that aims at: wanting to produce doubt about the discipline in the one who hears.35 This is the factor that separates the rule from honest discussion, and Thanissaro notes that the non-offence clause is the proof that it is needed: words that sound like criticism are not always meant as disparagement.36 Brahmāli draws the line in practice. A monk who has good grounds for thinking a rule was not laid down by the Buddha may say so, and may even argue on those grounds that it should not be kept; what the rule forbids is disparagement "just because you don't like the rule".37

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The one exemption is a piece of pastoral advice with a rule wrapped round it: "if, not desiring to disparage, he says, 'Listen, learn discourses or verses or philosophy, and later you can learn the Monastic Law.'"38 It is the group of six's own recommendation with the intention taken out, and the Vibhaṅga's willingness to print it shows how narrow the rule is. A teacher may put the Vinaya last in a curriculum, and say so, and commit nothing. Ajahn Brahm's example is a teacher who wants his disciples grounded in meditation before they study the finer points of the Vinaya.39

    A monk who keeps it up is not left to the confession. Thanissaro notes that a concerted campaign against the Dhamma or discipline may meet a formal act of censure or banishment; the Khandhaka's grounds name disparaging the Buddha, the Teaching and the Saṅgha, and "or discipline" is his reasonable extension.40​ 41

    The Buddha himself spoke of these rules. In a sutta Thanissaro quotes, even an arahant "falls into offenses and rehabilitates himself" with reference to the lesser and minor training rules, and that is no disqualification, while in the rules basic to the holy life his virtue is steadfast.42 The group of six were not wrong that the minor rules can produce anxiety. They were wrong about what follows from it.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • An offence of wrong conduct for disparaging another Dhamma — the suttas and mātikās, on the reading adopted above — in the presence of one fully ordained.43
    • An offence of wrong conduct for disparaging the Monastic Law, or another Dhamma, in the presence of one not fully ordained, whatever his perception.44
    • An offence of wrong conduct for the effort, while the disparagement is being made — the Parivāra's rung, not the Vibhaṅga's.45
    • Censure or banishment for a monk who keeps it up — not an offence, but the Saṅgha's answer to one.46

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if, not desiring to disparage, he says, "Listen, learn discourses or verses or philosophy, and later you can learn the Monastic Law"; if he is insane; if he is the first offender.47 The clause exempts a curriculum decision, not a verdict on the rules themselves.

    Related Offences

    • Pc 71 — refusing to train in a rule when corrected. The chapter's first rule: there a monk evades one rule, here he talks the whole collection down.
    • Pc 73 — feigning ignorance during the recitation. The next rule, whose narrower gloss of "being recited" is the evidence that this one reaches private study.
    • Pc 54 — disrespect toward a rule, where Thanissaro gives the reading of "another Dhamma" followed here.48
    • Pc 21 — the instruction of the bhikkhunīs, where aññaṁ dhammaṁ stands twice and means another teaching.49
    • Kd 11 — censure and banishment, whose grounds include disparaging the Buddha, the Teaching and the Saṅgha.50
    • Kd 21 — the First Council, where the elders gave six opinions on what the lesser and minor training rules are.51

    Related Suttas

    • DN 16 — the Buddha's permission, given before his death, for the Saṅgha to abolish the lesser and minor training rules if it wished.52
    • AN 3.88 — the Buddha on the "lesser and minor training rules", the group of six's own words: even one who fulfils virtue, concentration and wisdom falls into offences against them and rehabilitates himself, while in the rules "fundamental to the spiritual life" his behaviour is steadfast. The anxiety the six claimed the rules caused has an answer the training itself provides; Thanissaro quotes the sutta in his introduction.53​ 54
