    NP 14

    Story (Vatthu)

    First sub-story. While the Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery, monks were having blankets made every year. They kept on begging and asking, "Please give wool! We need wool!" People complained and criticised them, "How can the Sakyan monastics have blankets made every year, begging and asking, 'Please give wool! We need wool!'? We only make blankets for ourselves every five or six years, even though our children defecate and urinate on them and they're eaten by rats."

    The monks heard the complaints of those people, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised those monks, "How can those monks do this?" After rebuking those monks in many ways, they told the Buddha. He had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned the monks: "Is it true, monks, that there are monks who do this?" "It's true, sir." The Buddha rebuked them, "How can those foolish men do this? This will affect people's confidence …" And he laid down a rule that a monk who has had a new blanket made should keep it for six years.1

    Second sub-story. At one time a certain monk at Kosambī was sick. His relatives sent him a message, saying, "Come, venerable, we'll nurse you." The monks urged him to go, but he said, "The Buddha has laid down a training rule that a monk who has had a new blanket made must keep it for six years. Now because I'm sick, I'm unable to travel with my blanket. And because I'm not comfortable without it, I can't go." They told the Buddha, who gave a teaching and addressed the monks: "Monks, I allow you to give permission to a sick monk to make a blanket." He set out how it is to be given, and amended the rule to except the monks' agreement.2​ 3

    Synopsis — monks have blankets made every year and keep asking the laity for wool, who make their own last five or six years; the Buddha requires a new blanket to be kept for six years, then allows the Saṅgha to give a sick monk permission to make another sooner.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk has had a new blanket made, he should keep it for six years. Whether that blanket has been given away or not, if he has another new blanket made in less than six years, except if the monks have agreed, he commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession."4

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu makes a blanket, or has one made, he must wait six years before making another. If he does not, except with the Saṅgha's agreement, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."5

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "When a bhikkhu has had a new felt (blanket/rug) made, he is to keep it for (at least) six years. If after less than six years he should have another new felt (blanket/rug) made, regardless of whether or not he has disposed of the first, then—unless he has been authorized by the bhikkhus—it is to be forfeited and confessed."6

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Having a new santhata made more than once in six years, except when one has been given the Saṅgha's permission, is a nissaggiya pācittiya."7

    Ñāṇatusita

    "By a bhikkhu who has had a new rug made, it is to be kept for six years [at least]. If within less than six years, having given up or not having given up that rug, he should have another new rug made, except with the authorisation of bhikkhus, [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture."8

    The renderings agree on the rule, and santhata divides them as it does at NP 11. Two differences are deliberate. Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita add "at least" to the six years, which is the Vibhaṅga's own gloss, discussed below, under Effort. And Brahmāli's recitation, like Ajahn Brahm's line, leaves out "whether that blanket has been given away or not", a clause that says only that the old blanket's fate does not alter the offence.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule holds a monk to the people's own measure: a blanket kept as long as they kept theirs, and no more asking than that. The people's complaint is the whole of the case: monks were begging every year, "Please give wool! We need wool!", while the people made their own blankets last five or six years "even though our children defecate and urinate on them and they're eaten by rats."9 The Buddha's rebuke, "This will affect people's confidence", is what he said at the three rules before, and the six years he set are close to the people's own figure.10 The second story shows the rule's other edge. A rule meant to spare the laity was keeping a sick monk from the relatives who would nurse him, and the Buddha did not leave it there: he allowed the Saṅgha to set the rule aside for him.11​ 12

    Unlike the three rules before it, this one governs how often a santhata may be made, where they governed what it is made of, and the santhata itself is an allowable thing. The offence has three factors: object, effort, and result.13 Its one exception is a Saṅgha act, set out after them under Procedure.

    Object

    A new santhata, "newly made", for one's own use; the santhata and what counts as one today are NP 11's.14​ 15

    Effort

    Making it, or having it made, through any of the four ways of finishing, less than six years after one's last, and without the monks' agreement.16​ 17 The six years are a minimum: the Vibhaṅga glosses "he should keep it for six years" as "for six years at a minimum", using paramatā, a word that normally means "at a maximum", which Brahmāli explains by its other sense, "in the extreme".18 In the Vinaya, Ñāṇatusita notes, years are counted by rains-retreats.19 What became of the last blanket makes no difference. The rule says "whether that blanket has been given away or not", and Ajahn Brahm spells it out: it does not matter whether he is still using it, whether it has long since worn out, or whether he has none at all.20 Nor does an honest miscount excuse him. The texts are silent on perception, and Thanissaro takes it that a monk who makes a new blanket thinking six years have passed, when they have not, fulfils the factor all the same.21 The safe course is to count.

    Result

    As at NP 11: wrong conduct for the effort, and the full offence when he gets the blanket, or finishes it if he makes it himself.22 It is relinquished by the procedure of NP 1 and given back to him, and here, unlike NP 11–13, he may then use it as he pleases, since, as Ajahn Brahm puts it, the thing itself is not unallowable.23

    Procedure

    The sick monk approaches the Saṅgha, arranges his upper robe over one shoulder, pays respect at the feet of the senior monks, squats on his heels with joined palms, and asks three times: "Venerables, I'm sick. I'm unable to travel with my blanket. I ask the Sangha for permission to make a blanket." A competent and capable monk then puts it to the Saṅgha with a motion and one announcement, and the Saṅgha gives its permission by silence.24 The monk's part is to ask, in person and in those words; the Saṅgha's is to judge the need and give or withhold.

    Disputed — whether the Saṅgha may give the permission for anything but illness.

    Ajahn Brahm holds that it may not: "The Saṅgha's Permission is only given to a monk who is ill and who has need of a new santhata."25 Thanissaro adds one case. The rule has no exemption for a monk whose blanket is snatched away, lost or destroyed, which he explains by the abuse such an exemption would invite; instead, if the Community is satisfied that the monk did not lose it on purpose or leave it where it might easily be stolen, it can authorise a new one.26 Brahmāli reads the clause more widely still, and from the text: the story names illness, but the rule says only "except if the monks have agreed", so at NP 2, whose clause his Pātimokkha Manual says this one follows, he concludes "that the saṅgha has the discretion to give approval when it sees fit."27​ 28 Ajahn Brahm's reading keeps to the story that produced the allowance, Brahmāli's to the rule's own words, and Brahmāli's seems to me the closer to the text; yet it is a reading of what the rule leaves unsaid, and the question stays open. What all three agree on is where the decision sits: with the Saṅgha, not with the monk.

    Special Cases

    Two questions about what starts the six years have no answer in the texts. Ajahn Brahm argues at NP 13 that a santhata made against those proportions, which a monk may not use, still starts them.29 And he holds that a monk who, within his six years, has one made for someone else, though he commits no offence, must then wait a further six years before he may have one made for himself.30 The rule's own words, "he should keep it for six years", read most naturally as speaking of a blanket that is his to keep, and the Vibhaṅga frees the making for another without saying whether it starts the count. A monk who wants to be safe will count from any santhata he has had made.

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    From the exemption for using what another has made, Ajahn Brahm draws the practical consequence: a santhata someone else has made, received as a gift, may be used freely within the six years.31

    The people's blankets lasted five or six years through children and rats. That is the measure set for a monk, and it binds him only for what he has had made himself.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — for the effort of making a new santhata, or having it made, within six years and without the monks' agreement, before it is got.32

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he makes one after six years, or after more than six years; if he makes one, or has one made, for the sake of another; if he gets what was made by another and then uses it; if he makes a canopy, a floor cover, a cloth screen, a mattress, or a pillow; if he has the permission of the monks; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.33

    Related Offences

    • NP 11 — the anchor of NP 11–15, where the santhata and the shared factors are set out.
    • NP 13 — whose santhata, made against its proportions, may on Ajahn Brahm's argument start this rule's six years.
    • NP 15 — the sitting santhata, made with a piece of an old one.
    • NP 2 — the Saṅgha's permission for a sick monk to be apart from his robes, the clause this one follows.

    32. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, NP 14, definitions: "That blanket has been given away: it has been given to others. Not: it has not been given to anyone. Except if the monks have agreed: if he makes another new blanket, or has one made, unless the monks have agreed, then for the effort there is an act of wrong conduct. When he gets the blanket, it becomes subject to relinquishment"; relinquishment as in NP 1.↩︎

    33. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, NP 14, non-offenses: "There is no offense: if he makes one after six years; if he makes one after more than six years; if he makes one, or has one made, for the sake of another; if he gets what was made by another and then uses it; if he makes a canopy, a floor cover, a cloth screen, a mattress, or a pillow; if he has the permission of the monks; if he is insane; if he is the first offender."↩︎
