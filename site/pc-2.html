    Pc 2

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī, in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time the monks from the group of six were arguing with the good monks and speaking abusively to them. They reviled and insulted them about their caste, name, family, occupation, profession, illnesses, physical traits, defilements, and offences, and by calling them names. The monks of few desires complained and criticised them: "How can the monks from the group of six argue with and abuse good monks? How can they revile and insult them about these things?" After rebuking them in many ways, they told the Buddha. Soon afterwards he had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned the monks: "Is it true, monks, that you're doing this?"

    "It's true, sir."

    The Buddha rebuked them: "Foolish men, how can you do this? This will affect people's confidence." Then he gave them a teaching, telling a story of the past.

    Once there was a brahmin in Takkasilā who had an ox named Nandivisāla. One day the ox said to him, "Go, brahmin, and bet a thousand coins with the wealthy merchant that your ox will pull one hundred carts tied together." The brahmin made the bet. But when he had tied the hundred carts together and yoked Nandivisāla to them, he said, "Go, you fraud! Pull, you liar!" And Nandivisāla did not budge.

    The brahmin, having lost his thousand coins, became depressed. Nandivisāla asked him why. "Because I lost a thousand coins because of you." "But why did you disgrace me by calling me a fraud when I'm not? Go and make the bet again, for two thousand coins, and this time do not disgrace me." The brahmin bet again; and when he had yoked the ox, he said, "Go, good ox! Pull, good ox!" And Nandivisāla pulled the hundred carts.

    One should say what's pleasant, Never what's unpleasant. Because of his pleasant speech, Heavy loads were pulled, And he obtained wealth; And he was delighted with that.

    "Even at that time, monks, reviling and insulting was unpleasant to me. How then could reviling and insulting be pleasant now?" Then he laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — The group of six abuse the good monks about every ground for insult they can find, and the Buddha, recalling an ox that would not pull for a master who called it a fraud, lays down the rule against abusive speech.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk speaks abusively, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "Abusive speech to a bhikkhu is a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "An insult is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Reviling another monk is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "In abusive speech, [there is a case] involving expiation."6

    The five renderings agree on the act, and Ñāṇatusita gives the image beneath it: omasa is from a verb meaning "touches", so that abuse is a way of laying hands on a person with words. They differ on the person abused. The full rule and Thanissaro leave the object to the definitions; the recitation, "to a bhikkhu", and Ajahn Brahm, "another monk", write it into the rule. The recitation thus reads narrower than its translator's own view, and Ajahn Brahm takes a position on the point the sources dispute, discussed below under Object.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule restrains a monk's tongue among his fellows, for the harmony of the Saṅgha and the confidence of those who watch it. The group of six chose every ground they could find to wound the good monks with, and the Buddha's rebuke names the cost to the confidence of others; then he answers with a story rather than a definition. The ox that will not budge for a master who calls it a fraud pulls a hundred carts for one who calls it good, and the verse draws the moral, "One should say what's pleasant, never what's unpleasant." The Buddha adds that reviling and insulting were unpleasant to him even then.7 The rule does not ask whether the words are true. A monk who has in fact committed an offence, or is in fact short or dark, is insulted no less for having it thrown at him; the rule looks only at the aim to hurt and the ground chosen to hurt with.

    Thanissaro gives the offence three factors: effort, insulting a person directly, on one of ten topics for abuse; object, that the person is a bhikkhu; and intention, the motive to humiliate him.8 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual gives two, speaking to a fully ordained person and doing so intending to insult, treat with contempt or humiliate.9 Thanissaro's three serve here, since lifting the object out as its own factor gives the rule's dispute a place of its own.

    Effort

    The Vibhaṅga fixes the grounds of abuse as ten: caste, name, family, occupation, profession, illnesses, physical traits, defilements, offences, and name-calling. For each it gives low instances and high, and it makes every combination an offence "for every statement": the low said to the low or the high, and the high said to either, so that sarcastic praise, "You're learned", meant to jeer, counts as much as open abuse.10 Thanissaro adds that an insult may be a gesture or a statement, written or spoken, and that the remark must be made to the listener directly, "You are X."11 By his report the Commentary and Sub-commentary would make any insult outside the Vibhaṅga's listed terms a dukkaṭa, but the topics are defined so generally that any term related to them fulfils the factor.12 Modern abuse more often takes the form of a command, and both Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm count it here by its intent; Thanissaro adds that such commands would in most cases fall under Pc 54.13​ 14

    The insult must be direct. Abuse phrased indirectly, "There are outcasts right here", or as a guess, "Perhaps these are outcasts", or by folding oneself in, "We're not outcasts", is a dukkaṭa, whether the person is ordained or not.15

    Object

    To insult a bhikkhu is the full offence; to insult someone who is not fully ordained is a dukkaṭa.16 Where the line falls is disputed.

    Disputed — whether insulting a bhikkhunī incurs the full pācittiya or only a dukkaṭa.

    In Thanissaro's report the Commentary counts as unordained everyone "from bhikkhunīs to all other living beings", so that insulting a nun is a dukkaṭa, and Ajahn Brahm states the same in his own voice: "reviling a bhikkhunī, novice, sāmanerī or layperson is an offence of Dukkata."17​ 18 The Manual rejects the restriction: the Pāli upasampanna "refers to any ordained person, irrespective of gender", and treating the abuse of nuns differently "does not seem reasonable."19 The Vibhaṅga's permutations do not name the nun either way. The weight stays open. What is not in question is that abusing her is an offence, and a monk who speaks to a nun as the rule asks him to speak to a monk is safe on every reading.

    Intention

    The aim is the whole hinge of the rule. The Vibhaṅga's speaker is "wishing to revile, wishing to insult, wishing to humiliate", and a hard word spoken to correct is not that: a teacher who uses strong language to bring a stubborn student back to the Dhamma commits no offence, though Thanissaro warns that one should be "very sure of the purity of one's motives" first.20​ 21 Does it matter whether the other feels insulted? It does not, for the offence lies in the aim: an insult taken as a joke is still the full offence. The Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī would add a fourth factor, that the target knows he is being insulted, but Thanissaro finds no basis for it in the Vibhaṅga or the Commentary; an insult muttered under one's breath is letting off steam, not the intention the rule covers.22

    The same words said to amuse, with no wish to wound, are neither the full offence nor a dukkaṭa but a dubbhāsita, an offence of wrong speech, a class of offence that, Thanissaro notes, no other rule imposes.23​ 24 Brahmāli's example is the Australian habit of greeting an old friend with a word that would elsewhere be a plain insult; to his mind dubbhāsita means little more than speech badly spoken, and in a community where friends banter freely he doubts it is the serious matter it may once have been.25 Ajahn Brahm marks the other edge: teasing is often done with an edge, knowing it will probably hurt, and then it is the full offence. He doubts how often a monk can be sure of a compassionate heart when he admonishes another, and points to the Cullavagga's standard for doing it, to "speak with gentleness, not with harshness; … with a mind of loving-kindness not with inner hatred", which is the ox's lesson put as a discipline.26

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • A dukkaṭa for an insult made indirectly, as a guess, or by folding oneself in with the target, whether or not the person is ordained.27
    • A dukkaṭa for insulting one who is not fully ordained.28
    • A dubbhāsita for saying any of these things meaning it in fun, not to wound.29

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence for one aiming at something beneficial, at giving a teaching, or at giving an instruction; nor for one who is insane, deranged, or overwhelmed by pain, nor for the first offender.30 In Thanissaro's report the Commentary illustrates the teaching exemption with a monk who tells a man of the untouchable caste, "You are an untouchable. Don't do any evil. Don't be a person born into misfortune and going on to misfortune": words that name the hard fact only to turn the hearer from harm.31

    Related Offences

    • Pc 1 — the previous rule: Pc 1 forbids the false word, Pc 2 the wounding one, each turning on the speaker's intent.
    • Pc 3 — tale-bearing, which reuses this rule's ten topics of abuse as the matter carried between the parties.
    • Pc 13 — where Thanissaro places an insult made behind its target's back, not to his face.32
    • Pc 54 — disrespect; in most cases where a command-form of abuse falls, when it answers a correction and does not fit this rule's "You are X" shape.33
    • Sg 3 — lewd speech to a woman from lust, not malice, which Ajahn Brahm places there, not here.34

    Related Suttas

    • MN 21 — the aim to hurt is what the rule penalises, and the simile of the saw rules it out even under the worst provocation: "we shall utter no evil words; we shall abide compassionate for their welfare, with a mind of loving-kindness, without inner hate."35
    • Dhp 133 — "Say no harsh words to anyone; they might respond in kind": the harsh word invites its answer, the cost to harmony that the rule guards.36
    • AN 3.28 — the speech like honey, "such words as are gentle, pleasing to the ear, and lovable", that the Story's verse asks for in place of the insult.37
