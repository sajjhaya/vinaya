    Pc 79

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. The monks from the group of six were misbehaving, but when a legal procedure was being done against any one of them, they would object.1

    On one occasion the Saṅgha had gathered on some business. The monks from the group of six were busy making robes and so they gave their consent to one among them. When the monks saw that only one monk from the group of six had come, they did a legal procedure against him.2

    When he returned to the monks from the group of six, they asked him, "What did the Saṅgha do?"3

    "It did a legal procedure against me."4

    "We did not give our consent for that. If we had known that a procedure would be done against you, we would not have given our consent."5

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised them, "How could the monks from the group of six give their consent to legitimate legal procedures and then criticise them afterwards?" The Buddha questioned them, established that it was true, rebuked them, and stated the rule.6

    Synopsis — The group of six had found a way of making themselves untouchable: as long as one of them was in the room to object, no act could be carried out against any of them. The day they were all at their sewing, the Saṅgha took its chance; and what the rule penalises is not the objection, which they had lost their right to make, but the complaint afterwards.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk gives his consent to legitimate legal procedures, and then criticizes them afterwards, he commits an offense entailing confession."7

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu has given his consent for the Saṅgha to perform a valid official act, but then criticises the act, he incurs a pācittiya."8

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu, having given consent (by proxy) to a transaction carried out in accordance with the rule, later complain (about the transaction), it is to be confessed."9

    Brahm

    "Having given one's consent (Chanda) for a Sanghakamma to proceed in one's absence, afterwards engaging in criticizing the Sanghakamma is an offence of Pācittiya."10

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu, having given consent to legitimate [legal] actions, should afterwards engage in the act of criticising, [this is a case] involving expiation."11

    The renderings agree on the offence, and two of them make its mechanism visible. Thanissaro writes "having given consent (by proxy)" and Ajahn Brahm "for a Sanghakamma to proceed in one's absence"; neither addition is in the Pāli, and both are right, since the consent here is the kind an absent monk sends.12​ 13 "Legitimate legal procedures", the Vibhaṅga's dhammika kamma, is given as "valid official act" and "a transaction carried out in accordance with the rule", and the word-analysis defines it by a list that Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita both carry; that list is taken up below, under Object. The verb, "criticizes" or "complain", is the one word that needs settling, for the Vibhaṅga does not define it, and it is taken up under Effort.14​ 15

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule guards a monk's good faith with his community: a consent once given makes the Saṅgha's decision his own, and the decision is to be let stand. The Story shows the abuse it answers. The group of six had made themselves untouchable by objecting whenever any of them was dealt with, and when the Saṅgha finally acted against one of them in their absence, they disowned the consent they had sent. A Saṅgha whose decisions can be reopened by every member who later dislikes them cannot settle anything, and the confidence the rebuke names rests in part on a Saṅgha able to govern itself.

    The rule and the next presuppose the machinery of a Saṅgha act, and the origin story makes no sense without it. For an act to stand, Thanissaro explains, it must follow the right form and be carried out by a complete assembly: every monk within the territory is either present or has sent his consent, and no one present makes a valid protest.16 One monk's protest stops a decision, as Ajahn Brahm puts it, except that the monk about to receive a penalty may not protest his own.17 Thanissaro adds the two facts that explain the group of six. A protest need not be justified to count, and a Saṅgha may not act against more than three monks at once; so as long as some of the six were present, they could block any act against the others: the monks being acted against had no right to protest, but "their friends did, and they took advantage of their right."18 The day they all stayed at their sewing and sent their consent with one of their number, the Saṅgha acted against him, and there was no one to object.

    The allowance to send consent is given in the Khandhaka for a sick monk, and where he cannot be moved, the Saṅgha is to go to him and do the procedure there.19 Thanissaro notes that the group of six were not ill and the act still stood, and suggests that "ill" here covers any serious inconvenience. That widening is his: the Khandhaka names only the sick monk.20

    In Thanissaro's analysis the offence has three factors: object, perception and effort.21

    Object

    The object is a legitimate act to which the monk has given his consent. The Vibhaṅga defines a legitimate act by its four forms — by getting permission, by one motion, by one motion and one announcement, by one motion and three announcements — each done according to the Teaching, the Monastic Law and the Teacher's instruction.22 What the consent covers is fixed by the story. The group of six complained that they had not consented to that, and the rule was laid down on that very complaint: a monk who sends his consent is bound by what the Saṅgha then legitimately does, and has no say afterwards in which act it chose.23

    Perception

    The rule grades by perception, and the grading runs all the way down to no offence. A legitimate act perceived as legitimate is the pācittiya; an act about which the monk is unsure, or an illegitimate act he takes for legitimate, an offence of wrong conduct; an act he perceives as illegitimate, whether it was or not, no offence at all.24​ 25 Ajahn Brahm states the effect in one sentence, and it is the sentence to teach, though the Vibhaṅga asks only that he perceive the act so, not that he have reasonable grounds: "When one has reasonable grounds for thinking that the Sanghakamma was improperly carried out (i.e. not according to Vinaya procedures) then, whether one is correct or not, criticizing the Sanghakamma is not an offence."26 Not every such rule grades this way; Pc 81, on criticising a gift of robe-cloth, gives the full offence for a legitimate act however it is perceived.

    Effort

    "Any expression of displeasure with the transaction would fulfill this factor," Thanissaro says. But, he adds, a monk who says the act was not carried out according to the rule has asserted its invalidity, and that falls under Pc 63, the rule on reopening a settled issue, whether or not he had given his consent.27 The same words can land in two rules according to what they claim.

    How much must be said to count as criticism? The Vibhaṅga does not define the verb, and Brahmāli, unsure, thought it "would have to be reasonably public", not a remark to "one of your mates".28 The same verb stands in the final ruling of Pc 13, and its story leans his way: the two troublemakers, forbidden to complain about Dabba, criticised him within earshot of other monks so that the monks would hear. But Pc 13's word-analysis is satisfied by criticism to a single fully ordained listener.29 The texts, then, will not carry a threshold, and a monk who wants to be safe does not grumble to his friend either.

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The non-offence clause covers knowledge and the perception grid covers belief, so between them a monk who complains in good faith is not caught.30​ 31

    The rule is not only a restraint. Brahmāli stated the principle behind it: a monk raises his objections during the act and votes against it if he disagrees, for "you have your chance, and that chance really is during the saṅghakamma itself." Brahmāli also held that to vote for an act one privately dislikes, for harmony's sake, is "perfectly okay", provided it comes from the heart, "and then you should not criticise it afterwards"; for a major issue, "you should probably be careful."32 A monk who votes for the sake of the peace, then, keeps the peace afterwards as well, whether or not this rule reaches him.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — complaining after consenting, where the act was legitimate but he was unsure of it, or was illegitimate but he took it for legitimate or was unsure.33
    • Pācittiya (Pc 63) — where what is said is a claim that the act was invalid, not displeasure.34

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he criticises it because he knows that the legal procedure was illegitimate, done by an incomplete assembly, or done against one who did not deserve it; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.35 The perception grid adds a monk who merely perceives the act as illegitimate, whether it was or not.36

    Related Offences

    • Pc 80 — leaving a meeting without giving consent: the second half of the same episode, with the same three-ground exemption. The two are best read together.37
    • Pc 81 — complaining after a unanimous Saṅgha has given robe-cloth to an official: the same act of criticism, without the grading for a legitimate act.
    • Pc 63 — reopening a settled legal issue: where a complaint becomes an assertion that the act was invalid.38
    • Pc 13 — complaining about or criticising a Saṅgha-appointed official: the rule whose two substories show what this rule's verb means.39
    • Pc 72–78 — with this rule and Pc 80, the chapter's run of rules laid down at Sāvatthī on account of the group of six.40
