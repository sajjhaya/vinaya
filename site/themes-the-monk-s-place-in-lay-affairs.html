    The Monk's Place in Lay Affairs

    Venerable Udāyī used his visits to families in Sāvatthī to arrange marriages, and when a former courtesan's daughter, married on his word, was being treated like a slave and he went to her husband's family, they told him, "We deal with the courtesan, not with you. A monastic shouldn't get involved. You should behave like a proper monastic."1 Sg 5 followed, and Udāyī's story is told in Characters of the Vinaya. What belongs here is that last sentence and who said it: the family that had used his services told him that a monk had no business offering them.

    The same voice is heard across the classes. At Pc 48, when the group of six went out to see King Pasenadi's army on the march, the king himself asked them, "What's the use of seeing me finding pleasure in battle? Shouldn't you see the Buddha?", and people complained, "It's our misfortune that we must go out with the army for the sake of our livelihoods and because of our wives and children."2 At Pc 50 a monk who had gone to a battle was struck by an arrow, and people teased him: "We hope you had a good battle, venerable. How many targets did you hit?"3 The wanderer who regretted trading robes with Upananda at NP 20 (his story too is in Characters of the Vinaya) asked, "Even householders give back to each other when they regret a trade. How, then, can a monastic not do the same?"4 And the people who found the group of six in the village hall at the wrong time, talking of kings, armies and food, said of them, "They're just like householders who indulge in worldly pleasures!"5 In each of these stories those outside the Saṅgha knew what a monk is before a rule said it, and the rule that followed held the monk to their measure.

    The rules mark out what a monk does not do among lay people. He does not carry proposals between families (Sg 5) or buy and sell (NP 20). He does not go to see an army, stay with it beyond three nights or watch its drills (Pc 48, Pc 49, Pc 50), enter a royal compound unannounced (Pc 83), or enter a village at the wrong time without informing an available monk (Pc 85). The Brahmajāla Sutta counts the same restraints among the things for which a worldling would praise the Buddha: he "refrains from running errands, from buying and selling", and from such shows as "sham-fights, parades, manoeuvres and military reviews".6 At Pc 83 the Buddha gave ten dangers of entering a royal compound, and nine of them are suspicions. A gem disappears, or secret discussions are spread outside, and the king thinks, "Only the monk enters here. Is he responsible for this?"7 A monk who has a place in a household's business will be held to account for it.

    None of this closes the door between the monk and the household. Where keeping a rule left a real need unmet, the rule was eased. A monk may go to the army "when there's a suitable reason", as a monk with a sick uncle in the camp needed to, and he may enter a village at the wrong time "if there is some appropriate urgent business". Even at Pc 83, whose story of Ānanda is told in Characters of the Vinaya, the compound was not closed: Ānanda had been sent there by the Buddha's own word, "Well then, Ānanda, teach the king's harem", and the rule asks only that a monk be announced.8 The rules take away the monk's part in the household's affairs. They leave him his visits, his almsround and his teaching.

    Sg 13 shows why the line cannot be left to the families alone. At Kīṭāgiri the monks Assaji and Punabbasuka made garlands for the women of the families, danced when they danced and played their games, and won the town. When a monk who "was perfect in deportment" walked for alms there, people said, "Who's this, acting like a moron and always frowning? Who's gonna give almsfood to him?", and kept their food for "our Venerables Assaji and Punabbasuka, for they're gentle, congenial, pleasant to speak with".9 The Vibhaṅga lists the means by which a monk corrupts families, "flowers, fruit, bath powder, soap, tooth cleaners, bamboo, medical treatment, or by taking messages on foot", and calls the families corrupted when "they have lost their faith because of him".10 Thanissaro names the harm: such a monk ingratiates himself with lay people until "they withdraw their support from bhikkhus who are earnest in the practice and give it to those who are more ingratiating instead."11 The families of Kīṭāgiri had stopped asking what a monk is, and Sg 13 asks it for them.

    One rule at a time, these forbid a list of occupations. Between them they give the monk one place among lay people: near enough to receive and to teach, with no part in their bargains, their marriages or their wars. It seems reasonable to say that the complaints from outside the Saṅgha draw that line from one side, and that Sg 13 holds it from the other where the families' own preference has gone astray. The Buddha gave the place an image: "Bhikkhus, you should approach families like the moon—drawing back the body and mind, always acting like newcomers, without impudence towards families." Then he waved his hand in space, and said that a monk worthy to approach families is like the hand, which "does not get caught in space".12 A newcomer is welcomed in a house and does not run it. Udāyī had stopped being a newcomer in the houses of Sāvatthī.
