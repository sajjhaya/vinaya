    Pc 18

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time two monks were staying in a dwelling with an upper storey belonging to the Saṅgha, one below and one above. The monk above sat down hastily on a bed with detachable legs. A leg fell off and hit the monk below on the head. He cried out. Monks rushed up and asked him why, and he told them what had happened.

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised him: "How could a monk sit down hastily on a bed with detachable legs on an upper storey in a dwelling belonging to the Saṅgha?" They told the Buddha, who questioned the monk, confirmed that it was so, and laid down the training rule.1

    Synopsis — A monk sits down hastily on a bed whose legs simply drop out when the frame is lifted, on the upper storey of a Saṅgha dwelling; a leg falls onto the head of the monk below, and the Buddha forbids sitting or lying down on such furniture in such a place.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk sits down or lies down on a bed or a bench with detachable legs on an upper story in a dwelling belonging to the Sangha, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu sits or lies down on a bed or bench with unfastened legs on an unplanked upper storey of a kuṭi belonging to the Saṅgha, he incurs a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu sit or lie down on a bed or bench with detachable legs on an (unplanked) loft in a dwelling belonging to the Community, it is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Sitting or lying down suddenly on a bed or chair which has removable legs perched on the upper story of a 'Vehāsa-kutī' is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should [brusquely] sit down or lie down on a bed or seat with detachable legs in a hut with an upper-floor in a dwelling belonging to the community, [this is a case] involving expiation."6

    The renderings differ on how much of the Vibhaṅga to build into the rule. Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga says only "on an upper story" and leaves the state of the floor to the non-offence clause, where planking removes the offence; his recitation and Thanissaro carry it into the rule itself, "unplanked", Thanissaro marking the addition with parentheses.7​ 8​ 9 That changes nothing a monk must do. The renderings that do differ in substance are Ajahn Brahm's "suddenly" and Ñāṇatusita's bracketed "[brusquely]", which answer to a word some editions carry and others do not. Whether the rule forbids sitting on such a bed at all, or only sitting on it heavily, is taken up under Effort.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A monk sitting down on an upper floor is asked to think of whoever lives beneath him, for the safety and ease of the monks he shares a dwelling with. The monk of the Story did nothing worse than sit down hastily, and a loose bed-leg fell through the floor onto the head of the monk below. The non-offence clauses follow the same thought: where nobody can be underneath, or the floor is boarded, or the legs are bolted on, there is no offence.10

    Four conditions must come together, in Ajahn Brahm's account: an upper storey incompletely floored, a bed or chair on it with detachable legs, somebody below, and a monk who sits or lies down without care.11 Thanissaro treats the same ground under object and effort, and his division is followed here, since the fourth of Ajahn Brahm's conditions is the very point on which the sources divide.12

    Object

    The object is a bed or a bench with detachable legs, on an upper storey of a dwelling belonging to the Saṅgha. The bed and bench are those of Pc 14, where Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual takes the bench to include a chair.13 Of the four kinds the Vibhaṅga distinguishes there by how the legs are attached, this rule takes the one that "stands after inserting the limbs": in the Manual's words, legs "that fall off when the bed or bench is lifted".14​ 15​ 16

    The upper storey is one under which a man of average height does not hit his head, and its floor makes it dangerous.17 Thanissaro takes it as a loft whose joists have not been covered with floorboards; the Manual describes a floor that is incomplete, so that "objects may fall down to the lower storey".18​ 19 The rule says kuṭi, but the Manual reads it as any dwelling of the Saṅgha, since "the limits of what constitutes a kuṭi are not clearly specified".20 Perception of ownership does not mitigate, as in the preceding rules: in a Saṅgha dwelling the offence is the same whatever the monk takes it to be; in another individual's it is wrong conduct, and in his own, nothing.21​ 22

    Effort

    The effort is to sit down or to lie down on such a bed or bench. The Vibhaṅga's word-analysis adds no qualification of manner: "if he sits down on it, he commits an offense entailing confession."23

    Disputed — whether the rule forbids sitting on such a bed at all, or only sitting on it brusquely.

    The monk of the Story sat down hastily, and Ajahn Brahm builds the manner into the offence: to sit or lie down "suddenly", "without care".24​ 25 Ñāṇatusita sets out the textual case. Several editions and manuscripts, all of Sinhalese origin, carry sahasā, "brusquely", in the rule, and so do some other schools' recensions; the Mahāsāṅghika does not, and neither does the word-analysis. He suggests that the reciters disagreed very early over whether the sitting itself was the offence, and he prints "[brusquely]" in his translation, holding that the Vibhaṅga and the commentaries support that reading.26 Thanissaro reads the rule as it stands: beds with detachable legs "do not sound like wise things to have on an unplanked loft", and perhaps the Buddha meant to discourage their being there at all.27 The texts leave both readings standing; the word-analysis, as Ñāṇatusita himself notes, gives the offence for sitting down with no word of how, and a monk who does not sit or lie on such a bed at all is safe on either.

    The non-offence clause also lets a monk stand "on it to get hold of something or to put something up". Brahmāli's endnote gives the Commentary's reading, standing on the bed itself to take down a robe from a wall peg above; Thanissaro finds that hard to credit, since standing on such a bed seems more dangerous than sitting on it, and thinks "there" more probably means the loft.28​ 29

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The exemptions follow the rule's purpose. There is no offence where nobody lives below, or the space below is used only for storage, as the Commentary says in Thanissaro's report; nor where the ceiling below is too low for a man of average height to stand under.30​ 31 Ajahn Brahm thinks the rule no longer relevant, since all four of his conditions must meet before there is an offence.32 What does carry over is the lesson under it. In the Vinītavatthu of Pj 3 a monk sat down on a bench and crushed a boy hidden under an old cloth, and the Buddha, finding no pārājika, added: "you shouldn't sit down on a seat without checking it."33 Look before you sit, and think of whoever is beneath you.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • A dukkaṭa where the dwelling belongs to another individual, whatever the monk takes it to be.34

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if there is no upper storey; if the upper storey is so low that one hits the head; if the lower storey is not in use; if the upper storey has floorboards; if the legs are fastened by bolts; if he stands on it to get hold of something or to put something up; if he is insane; if he is the first offender.35 Thanissaro adds the case where the bed or bench stands on the ground.36

    Related Offences

    • Pc 14 — the anchor for the bed and bench; its Vibhaṅga lists the four kinds of each, of which this rule takes one.
    • Pj 3 — the Vinītavatthu's dukkaṭa for sitting down on a seat without checking it, which binds wherever the seat and whatever its legs.
    • Pc 19 — the next building rule: there the danger is a dwelling overloaded until it collapses.

    A note on sourcing

    The Story, the rule, the definitions, the permutations and the non-offence clauses are from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation, with his endnotes on kuṭi and on standing on the bed, and the recitation line from his Pātimokkha recitation translation; the bench case is from his Vinītavatthu to Pj 3, the connection Ajahn Brahm's, in a note to this rule. The Manual gives the bed-leg, the incomplete floor and the reading of kuṭi as any Saṅgha dwelling. Thanissaro's BMC1 supplies the object and its exemptions, the Commentary's storage case, cited through him, the reading of standing, and his answer on sahasā. Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes gives the four conditions and his judgment that the rule is no longer relevant. The Concise BMC was checked and condenses BMC1 without adding to it. Ñāṇatusita gives the rule translation and the textual case on sahasā, reported as his. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 18, non-offences: "There is no offense: if there is no upper story; if the upper story is so low that one hits the head; if the lower story is not in use; if the upper story has floorboards; if the legs are fastened by bolts; if he stands on it to get hold of something or to put something up; if he is insane; if he is the first offender."↩︎

    36. Thanissaro, BMC1, Pc 18, "Object": "an unplanked loft is one whose joists have not been covered with floorboards… Perception of the ownership of the dwelling, as in the preceding rules, is not an issue here. The purpose of this rule, as indicated by the origin story, is to guard against injury to a bhikkhu living under the loft." No offence "if the area under the loft cannot be used as a dwelling (e.g., it is used solely for storage space, says the Commentary); if the bed or bench with detachable legs is on the ground".↩︎
