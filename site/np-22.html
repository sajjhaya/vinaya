    NP 22

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying in the Sakyan country, in the Banyan Tree Monastery at Kapilavatthu. At that time a potter had invited the monks, saying, "If any of you needs an almsbowl, I'll provide it." But the monks had no sense of moderation, and they asked for many bowls. Those who had small bowls asked for large ones, and those who had large bowls asked for small ones. The potter was so busy making bowls for the monks that he was unable to make goods for sale. He could not make a living for himself, and his wives and children suffered.

    People complained and criticised them, "How can the Sakyan monastics have no sense of moderation and ask for many bowls? This potter is so busy making bowls for them that he is unable to make goods for sale. He is unable to make a living for himself, and his wives and children are suffering." The monks of few desires heard the complaints, and after rebuking those monks in many ways, they told the Buddha. He had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned the monks, and he rebuked them: "How can those foolish men do this? This will affect people's confidence …" Then he addressed the monks: "Monks, a monk should not ask for an almsbowl. If he does, he commits an offense of wrong conduct."

    Soon afterwards the bowl of a certain monk broke. Knowing that the Buddha had prohibited asking for a bowl and being afraid of wrongdoing, he did not ask for a new one. As a consequence, he collected almsfood with his hands. People complained and criticised him, "How can the Sakyan monastics collect almsfood with their hands, just like the monastics of other religions?" The monks told the Buddha, who addressed them: "Monks, I allow you to ask for a new almsbowl if your bowl has been lost or is broken."

    When they heard about the allowance, the monks from the group of six asked for many bowls even when their existing bowls only had a minor chip or scratch. Once again the potter was so busy making bowls for the monks that he was unable to make goods for sale, and his wives and children suffered, and people complained as before. The monks of few desires told the Buddha, who questioned and rebuked the group of six, "Foolish men, how could you do this? This will affect people's confidence …", and laid down the final rule.1

    Synopsis — a potter's invitation to supply bowls is worn down twice, first by monks who ask for many bowls, then, after the allowance for a lost or broken bowl, by the group of six asking whenever a bowl has a chip or a scratch; the Buddha sets the line at five mends, and a bowl got in breach of it goes down the line of the Saṅgha in exchange for the last bowl.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk exchanges an almsbowl with fewer than five mends for a new almsbowl, he commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession. That monk should relinquish that almsbowl to a gathering of monks. He should then be given the last almsbowl belonging to that gathering: 'Monk, this bowl is yours. Keep it until it breaks.' This is the proper procedure."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu asks for and receives a new bowl when his existing bowl has fewer than five mends, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu with an alms bowl having fewer than five mends ask for another new bowl, it is to be forfeited and confessed. The bowl is to be forfeited by the bhikkhu to the company of bhikkhus. That company of bhikkhus' final bowl should be presented to the bhikkhu, (saying,) 'This, bhikkhu, is your bowl. It is to be kept until broken.' This is the proper course here."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "When one's alms bowl is either still usable or is damaged but with less than five cracks or holes, then receiving another alms bowl, having asked for it, without being invited to do so, from a layperson who is not a relation is a nissaggiya pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should exchange a bowl with less than five mends for another new bowl, [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture. That bowl is to be relinquished by that bhikkhu to the assembly of bhikkhus, and whichever [bowl] is the last bowl of that assembly of bhikkhus, that [bowl] is to be bestowed on that bhikkhu [thus]: 'Bhikkhu, this bowl is for you, it is to be kept until breaking.' This is the proper procedure here."6

    The renderings divide over one verb. Cetāpeyya is "exchanges" for Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation and Ñāṇatusita, the trading verb of NP 8; his recitation has "asks for and receives", and Thanissaro "ask for". The Vibhaṅga's own definition joins them: "Exchanges: if he asks, then for the effort there is an act of wrong conduct. When he gets the bowl, it becomes subject to relinquishment."7 The exchange is made by asking and completed by receiving, and no old bowl is traded. Ajahn Brahm's rendering builds the exemptions for relatives and those who have invited into the rule itself. "The last almsbowl", pattapariyanto, is the bowl left at the end of the line, an idiom the Canon uses too for the last seat, the last bed and the last dwelling.8 What counts as a mend is the rule's real question, and Effort answers it.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    Even an invited monk may ask too much, and the rule sets the measure, for his own sake and for the trust of those who offer to supply him. The potter had invited the monks, and the fault lay in having "no sense of moderation", not in asking: bowls asked for many at a time, small exchanged for large and large for small, until a craftsman who had offered gladly "could not make a living for himself."9 The story's middle episode shows the other limit. A monk whose bowl broke, afraid to ask, went for alms with his hands "just like the monastics of other religions", and the Buddha allowed him to ask; the rule is drawn against the group of six's chips and scratches, not against a monk's need.10 And the procedure that follows the offence makes the lesson plain to every monk in the line: the bowl he asked for goes to whoever wants it, and he keeps the one nobody wanted.

    The rule concerns when a monk may ask for a bowl, and what the Saṅgha then does with one asked for too soon. In Thanissaro's analysis the offence has three factors, effort, object and result; Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual covers the same ground as definitions, of the new bowl, the mends and the receipt, and adds the Saṅgha's last bowl. I take Thanissaro's three, with the Saṅgha's part under its own label.11​ 12

    Effort

    The effort is asking for a bowl while one's own still has fewer than five mends; "asks" carries its NP 6 sense, and the asking is wrong conduct before any bowl arrives.13​ 14 What makes a mend count is a fracture, not a repair. The Vibhaṅga sets the line at a fracture of two fingerbreadths, which Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga puts at 3.5 cm and Thanissaro as "two inches": a crack shorter than that does not merit mending and is not counted, which is exactly the minor chip or scratch the group of six were asking on.15​ 16

    For a ceramic bowl Thanissaro reports the Commentary's count as a measure of total damage: the bowl is past repair when its countable cracks, none shorter than two fingerbreadths, add up to ten fingerbreadths in length, and whether they have actually been mended does not matter. Another may then be asked for.17 For an iron bowl the sources part.

    Disputed — whether an iron or steel bowl with five plugged holes entitles a monk to ask for a new one.

    Holes alone do not unmake an iron bowl in the Commentary's reading, which Thanissaro reports. Plugged and polished smooth, "no matter how many such holes there are in the bowl they do not make it a non-bowl"; only a hole so large that its patch cannot be made smooth, so that it collects food, entitles the monk to ask for another.18 Ajahn Brahm reads the rule's five mends as covering both kinds of bowl: when the fifth crack or hole appears the monk may mend it or ask, and an iron bowl with five or more holes, "plugged or not", is no offence.19 The Manual defines mends as "Repairs to cracks or holes".20 The Vibhaṅga's five mends draw no line between materials, which is the strength of the second reading; the Commentary's test is whether the bowl still does its work, which is the strength of the first, and fits the group of six, who asked while their bowls served. The text does not choose between them. The cautious monk does not ask for another while his iron bowl still serves, which keeps him clear on both.

    Whom one may ask is set by the exemptions: relatives, those who have invited, and a monk asking on another's behalf on the terms of NP 6. Asking a fellow monastic is no offence.21 A bowl from the monastery's own store Brahmāli judged in class to fall outside the rule, as his own view and not a ruling of the text: the procedure plainly has a bowl from lay donors in view, and a store given to the Saṅgha stands to its monks like a standing invitation.22

    Object

    A "new" bowl is any bowl got by asking, used before or not, and it must be fit to be determined by the standards of NP 21.23​ 24

    Result

    The full offence falls when he receives the bowl.25​ 26

    Procedure

    What follows is the Saṅgha's act, and the offender's part in it is small. He relinquishes the bowl in the midst of the Saṅgha, with the bowl's own words, "Venerables, this almsbowl, which I got in exchange for a bowl that had fewer than five mends, is to be relinquished. I relinquish it to the Sangha", and confesses.27 The monks come with the bowls they have determined, and a monk with a poorer spare bowl may not determine it for the meeting in the hope of a valuable one.28 By a motion and one announcement the Saṅgha appoints a distributor of almsbowls, a monk free of favouritism, ill will, confusion and fear, who knows what has and has not been distributed.29 He offers the bowl to the most senior monk; if the senior monk takes it, his old bowl goes to the next, and so down to the most junior. A monk who prefers the offered bowl may not refuse it out of sympathy for the offender; in Thanissaro's report the commentaries excuse one who does not prefer it, or who declines it from contentment.30​ 31 The bowl left at the end is given to the offender, "Monk, this bowl is yours. Keep it until it breaks."32

    He must then look after it. Keeping it in an unsuitable place, using it in an unsuitable way or giving it away, thinking, "How may this bowl be lost, destroyed, or broken?", is wrong conduct; he is to care for it as best he can until it is no longer usable, as Thanissaro puts it.33​ 34 Ajahn Brahm reads "until it breaks" as until the fifth crack or hole, and draws the consequence that his old bowl must meanwhile be given away or assigned, since he must use the Saṅgha's last bowl.35 The monk who reached for a better bowl ends with the plainest in the community, and carries it with care.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — asking for a bowl, before receiving it, when one's own has fewer than five mends.36
    • Dukkaṭa — for a monk coming to the exchange: determining a poorer bowl in the hope of getting a valuable one.37
    • Dukkaṭa — for a monk in the line: not taking the offered bowl out of sympathy, when he prefers it.38
    • Dukkaṭa — for the offender: keeping the Saṅgha's last bowl in an unsuitable place, using it in an unsuitable way, or giving it away, in the hope that it will be lost, destroyed, or broken.39

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if his almsbowl is lost or broken; if it is from relatives; if it is from those who have given an invitation; if it is for the benefit of someone else; if it is by means of his own property; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.40 Asking for someone else is read, as at NP 6, as asking only from one's own relatives or inviters or from the other monk's.41

    Related Offences

    • NP 21 — what makes a bowl fit to be determined, and the hole that ends a determination.
    • NP 6 — the anchor for "asks", for asking on another's behalf, and for an allowance when a requisite is lost.
    • NP 8 — cetāpeti, "exchanges", the verb this rule shares.
    • Pc 47 — the terms of an invitation to ask, on which the invitation exemption depends.

    Related Suttas

    • AN 8.30 — "This Dhamma is for one with few desires, not for one with strong desires" (see NP 1); the potter's invitation was worn down by monks who asked without moderation, and few desires keep the measure an invitation leaves to the monk.42
    • SN 16.1 — Mahākassapa praised for contentment with whatever he has (see NP 21); a chipped or scratched bowl still serves the monk who is content, and the rule draws its line at five mends.43
