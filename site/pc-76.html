    Pc 76

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. The monks from the group of six groundlessly charged a monk with an offence entailing suspension.1

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised them, "How could the monks from the group of six do this?" The Buddha questioned them, established that it was true, rebuked them, and stated the rule.2

    Synopsis — The monks of the group of six charged a monk, without grounds, with an offence entailing suspension. The rule that follows asks for no motive, and that, with the lighter charge, is what separates it from Sg 8.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk groundlessly charges a monk with an offense entailing suspension, he commits an offense entailing confession."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu falsely accuses a bhikkhu of a saṅghādisesa, or has him accused, he incurs a pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu charge a bhikkhu with an unfounded saṅghādisesa (offense), it is to be confessed."5

    Brahm

    "Falsely accusing a monk of an offence of Sanghādisesa is an offence of Pācittiya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should accuse a bhikkhu with a groundless [case] involving the community in the beginning and in the rest, [this is a case] involving expiation."7

    The renderings agree on what the rule forbids and differ in two small places. The Pāli rule has one verb, anuddhaṁseyya; "accuses or has accused" is the word-analysis's gloss of it, and the condensed recitation alone lifts the gloss into the rule line with "or has him accused".8​ 9 Nothing is added thereby, since every factor list covers the charge made through another. The factor in Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual gives "falsely" as "having no reason to think or suspect", which drops the seeing and the hearing from the Vibhaṅga's three limbs — "not seen, not heard, not suspected" — though a monk who has seen or heard has a reason to think, and the difference changes nothing.10​ 11 The verb is the one word that carries a question, for Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga has "charges" where his recitation has "accuses", and Ñāṇatusita refers the phrase to Sg 8. Whether a charge must be understood by the man it is made to depends on how that verb is read,12 and that question is taken up below, under Effort.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule keeps a monk's honesty intact at the point where a lapse in it can do a brother most harm: in a charge of a serious offence. A charge is how a Saṅgha corrects itself, and a monk who brings one against a man he has no reason to think guilty turns the means of correction into a weapon. The Story gives one sentence and no motive, and the rule asks for none; what it censures is the act itself. And the people's confidence, which the rebuke names, cannot rest on a community whose members accuse one another at will.

    The rule is Sg 8 with the charge lowered. A groundless charge that a monk has committed a pārājika is a saṅghādisesa under that rule; a groundless charge of a saṅghādisesa is a pācittiya under this one. Thanissaro sends the reader back to Sg 8 for the machinery, and what counts as unfounded, who may accuse whom and after what procedure, and what qualifies a monk to accuse are therefore at Sg 8. He names the two differences: intention is not a factor here, and the lighter charge carries the lighter penalty.13 Ajahn Brahm's Story gives the group of six a motive, that they accused first so as not to be accused themselves; the Vibhaṅga gives none, and none has to be shown.14

    Thanissaro gives the offence three factors: object, perception and effort.15

    Object

    The monk charged must be fully ordained, and perception of his ordination makes no difference. The Vibhaṅga gives the full offence whether the accuser takes him to be fully ordained, is unsure of it, or takes him to be unordained, and Thanissaro refers the pattern to Pc 42.16​ 17 A groundless charge against anyone not fully ordained — a nun or a novice in Thanissaro's examples, a layperson in Ajahn Brahm's — is an offence of wrong conduct, again whatever the accuser takes him to be.18​ 19​ 20

    Perception

    "Groundless" is defined as "not seen, not heard, not suspected".21 The groundlessness is the accuser's and not the accused's. Thanissaro states the factor so at Sg 8: to perceive the accused as innocent is enough, whether or not he actually is.22 A monk who invents a charge, and happens to be right, has committed the offence. Ajahn Brahm adds the case between, by analogy with Sg 8 and saying that he does so: a monk who only suspects, but charges the other as though he knew, also commits the full offence.23 And so a suspicion may be voiced as a suspicion; it may not be dressed as knowledge.

    Effort

    The charge is made to the monk in his presence, by the accuser himself or through someone else, and the penalty is the same either way.24​ 25​ 26 What is charged sets the penalty. A charge of a saṅghādisesa is this rule's pācittiya; a charge of "failure in conduct or failure in view" is an offence of wrong conduct, failure in conduct meaning, in Thanissaro's summary of the Khandhaka's definition, "any offense of a thullaccaya or less".27​ 28 A charge of a pārājika belongs to Sg 8.

    Is a false charge of a lesser offence, then, only an offence of wrong conduct, when a plain lie is a pācittiya under Pc 1? Ajahn Brahm holds that it is not: following the Vinayamukha, he treats such a charge as a deliberate lie and so a pācittiya under Pc 1.29 Thanissaro, listing the lies that fall under a more specific rule, gives the lesser charges this rule's dukkaṭa, and he meets the Vinaya-mukha's argument directly: it "contradicts the Vibhaṅga".30 I follow Thanissaro: the Vibhaṅga grades the lesser charges under this rule, and on his precedence principle the specific rule displaces the general one.31 The result is uncomfortable. A monk who invents a dukkaṭa against a brother is penalised more lightly than one who tells a pointless lie about the weather, and the answer is that the two penalties measure different things: Pc 1 measures the lie, and this rule measures the accusation by the gravity of what it alleges. A teacher meeting the case should say both — the dukkaṭa is what the rules give, and it is not the measure of the conduct.

    The Manual adds two further factors that it also gives at Sg 8: the charge is made face to face, and the accused understands it at once.32 Thanissaro accepts the first and rejects the second, finding nothing in the Vibhaṅga to support it.33​ 34 Both factors are disputed at Sg 8 (presence disputed by Ajahn Brahm there, though at this rule he states it), where the sides are set out. On the Manual's reading, a charge muttered to let off steam and never taken in falls short of this rule's full offence; on Thanissaro's, it does not.

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence "if he accuses someone, or has someone accused, according to what he has perceived".35 The clause carries the whole of the rule's mercy, and it is a clause about perception, not about truth. A monk who honestly believes, on what he has seen or heard or suspects, that another has committed a saṅghādisesa, and charges him, commits no offence even if the other is innocent.36

    The clause has limits. An honest charge made without first asking the other monk's leave is an offence of wrong conduct, and so is asking leave with no grounds at all; a charge framed to insult brings in Pc 2.37 And a true charge, spread among those not fully ordained, is Pc 9's business. A monk who believes he has seen a brother commit a serious offence is protected by this rule when he speaks; he is still to ask the man's leave first, and to say it to him and not about him.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — a groundless charge of failure in conduct, which is any offence of a thullaccaya or less, or of failure in view.38​ 39
    • Dukkaṭa — a groundless charge against anyone not fully ordained, however he is perceived.40
    • Dukkaṭa (Parivāra) — for the effort, while the charge is being made; the pācittiya follows when it has been made.41
    • Dukkaṭa (Khandhaka) — accusing a monk without first asking his leave, and asking leave with no grounds; the first applies to an honest charge as much as to a false one.42
    • Saṅghādisesa — a groundless charge of a pārājika: Sg 8, not this rule.

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he accuses someone, or has someone accused, according to what he has perceived; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.43

    Related Offences

    • Sg 8 — a groundless charge of a pārājika, made in anger and with the aim of expulsion. The anchor rule: this one's account of what "unfounded" means, its procedural setting and the dispute over presence and understanding are there, and Thanissaro refers this rule to it.
    • Sg 9 — the same charge made by seizing on some genuine but unrelated detail.
    • Pc 1 — deliberate lying: the rule a false charge would otherwise fall under, displaced by this one as the more specific.
    • Pc 2 — reviling: where the aim is to insult rather than to charge, or is both.
    • Pc 9 — telling someone not fully ordained of a monk's serious offence.
    • Pj 4, Sg 8, 9, Pc 13, 24 — with this rule, the rules Thanissaro lists as taking precedence over Pc 1 where a lie is of a specified kind.44
    • Pc 42 — where Thanissaro sets out the principle that perception of the other's ordination does not mitigate.
    • Pc 77, 78 — the two rules that follow, on manufactured anxiety and eavesdropping: with this rule's false charge, each laid down at Sāvatthī on account of the group of six, and none of them requiring the victim to have committed any offence.45

    Related Suttas

    • AN 5.167 — the second of the five things a monk establishes before reproving another, "I will speak truthfully, not falsely", and the remorse to be induced in one who reproves falsely, "so that another bhikkhu would not think of reproving falsely".46
    • Snp 3.10 — Kokālika, who would not stop charging Sāriputta and Moggallāna with evil desires, and the axe "born inside his mouth / with which the fool cuts himself": where a baseless charge leads the one who makes it.47

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register, with the complaint, interrogation and rebuke given in the standing summary form; the rule carries no endnotes. Ajahn Brahm's Story gives the group of six a motive of pre-emption that the Vibhaṅga does not; it is noted in the Explanation and not merged into the Story. The Manual, the recitation, the class (session 031, 23 July 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1 and BMC2, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were all checked; the Concise BMC condenses BMC1 here. The Manual's second note on its factors refers the reader to "Sg.3"; its only explanation of the face-to-face factor is at Sg 8, and I read the note so. The Vinaya-mukha, which is not used in this book, is known here only through Ajahn Brahm's and Thanissaro's reports of it. AN 5.167 is quoted from Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation, and Snp 3.10 from his translation of the Suttanipāta.
