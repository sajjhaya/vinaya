    Pc 62

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time the monks from the group of six were using water that they knew contained living beings.1

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised them: "How can the monks from the group of six use water that they know contains living beings?" They told the Buddha, who questioned the monks and rebuked them: "Foolish men, how can you do this? This will affect people's confidence." A monk who uses water that he knows contains living beings commits an offence entailing confession.2

    Synopsis — The group of six used water they knew had creatures in it. The rule that follows is the second of the pair on such water, and the one that does not ask what the monk meant by it.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk uses water that he knows contains living beings, he commits an offense entailing confession."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu uses water, when he knows it contains living beings that will die, he incurs a pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu knowingly make use of water containing living beings, it is to be confessed."5

    Brahm

    "Using water, knowing it contains living being and knowing that it will cause their death, is an offence of Pācittiya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should knowingly use water containing living beings, [this is a case] involving expiation."7

    The renderings agree, and two of them write one more clause into the rule. The Pāli is jānaṁ sappāṇakaṁ udakaṁ paribhuñjeyya, "should knowingly use water containing living beings", and says nothing of death; the death comes in the word-analysis, where the monk uses the water "knowing that it contains living beings and knowing that they will die if the water is used".8​ 9 Brahmāli's recitation line and Ajahn Brahm move that clause up into the rule, and the recitation does the same at Pc 20, so it is his standing treatment of the pair rather than a choice made here.10​ 11 It changes nothing the rule requires. The verb is the rule's real difference from Pc 20: paribhuñjati is simply "uses", where Pc 20's verb is to pour, and so this rule takes every use of such water the other leaves.12​ 13

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule keeps a monk's harmlessness alive in the most ordinary act of his day, the use of water, where a creature can die not because he wants it dead but because he did not care. The group of six used water they knew had living things in it, and that is the whole of the origin story.14

    Pc 20 is the anchor of this pair. Its object is this rule's object, its perception analysis is this rule's, and the checking of water before use is set out there; what follows is only what differs. Thanissaro gives three factors: object, water containing living beings; perception, knowing they are there and that the use will kill them; and effort, using it. Neither intention nor result is a factor, for "whether the living beings actually die is of no consequence".15

    Object

    The living beings are those visible to the eye, as at Pc 20, and mosquito larvae are the standing example.16 Brahmāli limited the rule to creatures that live in water: an ant that has fallen into a bucket is not what it means, and is better lifted out.17

    Perception

    This is what separates the rule from Pc 61. There the monk aims at the death; here he only knows of it, and uses the water anyway. Since the non-offence clauses excuse only what the monk does not know, never what he does carelessly, Thanissaro draws a consequence: a monk who knows the water holds creatures that would die and spills it by accident incurs the offence all the same.18​ 19 Thanissaro records the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī's claim that a non-murderous intention is a factor here as well; his argument against it belongs to Pc 20.20

    Effort

    Any use counts. Thanissaro gives the Commentary's examples: drinking the water, washing one's bowl with it, cooling hot porridge in it, dipping it out of a tank or pond to bathe, and making waves in a pool so that the water splashes over its banks; the Commentary to Pj 3 brings even putting out an approaching wildfire with it under this rule. The Commentary also gives the way to clean out a dirty pool without killing what lives in it: eight to ten potfuls of clean water are put in another place that will hold them, and the pool's water is dipped into that.21

    Disputed — whether getting someone else to use such water falls under the rule.

    The Vibhaṅga does not include it, as it does at Pc 20. Thanissaro notes that the Commentary and the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī include it.22 Ajahn Brahm thinks telling a novice to wash out a footbath full of mosquito larvae "seems to deserve an offence of Pācittiya".23 Thanissaro thinks the Vibhaṅga's silence probably right, though he grants that the position he attributes to the commentaries seems reasonable on the surface. An order to pour water on the ground dooms the creatures, while an order to use water leaves the other person to decide whether to strain it first, and if he does not, "the offense under this rule would be his".24 The texts leave the case open, and I cannot close it; the safe course is to give the instruction together with the strainer.

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The exemption in daily use is the last, use knowing they will not die. Bathing in a river is no offence, Ajahn Brahm notes, though the river is full of life, because bathing will not kill it.25 Brahmāli's examples were the rainwater tanks at a monastery's huts: draining a tank one knows has larvae in it is the offence, and fishing the larvae out first is "exactly what you are supposed to be doing". Where he was unsure, his test was to fill a bucket from the tap and look.26 A filter serves the rule only if what it catches lives, and Ajahn Brahm adds the instruction to return the creatures "to some other water in good time".27

    The strainer that makes the rule keepable came with a story. Two monks were travelling; one had reproved the other, who became resentful, and when the first grew very thirsty and asked to borrow his water filter, he refused, and the monk died of thirst. The Buddha's two rulings pointed both ways: a monk must lend his filter to a travelling companion who asks, and no monk may travel without one.28

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — using water that contains living beings while unsure whether it does.29
    • Dukkaṭa — using water that does not contain living beings while perceiving that it does, or being unsure.30
    • Dukkaṭa — refusing to lend a water filter to a travelling companion who asks, and travelling without one.31

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he does not know that it contains living beings; if he knows that it does not contain living beings; if he uses it knowing that they will not die; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.32

    Related Offences

    • Pc 20 — pouring water one knows contains living beings: the anchor of the pair, with the same object and perception and a narrower verb.33
    • Pc 61 — intentionally killing an animal: the rule this one is defined against, where the monk aims at the death.34
    • Kd 15:13 — the allowance of the water filter and the strainer, the duty to lend and to carry one, and the corner of the robe determined as a filter when there is none.35

    Related Suttas

    • Snp 1.8 — care toward all beings like a mother's for her only son, reaching even the smallest creatures, such as the mosquito larvae this rule asks a monk to take care over in the water he uses.36

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; the complaint is verbatim and the rebuke is given in the standing form. I treat this rule as the lean mirror of Pc 20, where the shared material is treated. Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual, the recitation, the class transcript (session 029, 19 June 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1 and BMC2, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were checked, with the Khandhaka passage on water filters and Snp 1.8 in Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation. The commentaries are known here only as Thanissaro reports them. The class is cited for its modern case, the rainwater tanks, and for its limit to creatures that live in water.

Thus the Vibhaṅga seems correct". And: "Whether the living beings actually die is of no consequence in determining the offense."↩︎

    35. Kd 15:13 (Cv.V.13), Brahmāli's translation: two monks travelling in the Kosalan country; one reproved the other, who became resentful, refused the thirsty monk his filter, and the monk "died from thirst". The rulings: "If you're traveling with a monk and he asks to borrow your water filter, you should lend it. If you don't, you commit an offense of wrong conduct. On the other hand, you shouldn't travel without a water filter. If you do, you commit an offense of wrong conduct." Where there is neither filter nor strainer, a corner of the robe may be determined as one.↩︎

    36. Snp 1.8, Metta Sutta, Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation, v. 149 (PTS p. 26): "Just as a mother would protect her son, / her only son, with her own life, / so one should develop toward all beings / a state of mind without boundaries."↩︎
