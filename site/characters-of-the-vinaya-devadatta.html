    Devadatta

    Devadatta occasioned four rules, and in the Vibhaṅga they tell the middle of his story. The beginning and the end are in the Khandhakas, in the chapter on schism, and the beginning is a question he put to himself at Kosambī: "Who might I inspire to have confidence in me, so that I'd get much material support and honor?"1 He found Prince Ajātasattu, won him with a display of psychic power, and was attended on morning and evening "with five hundred carriages and a meal offering of five hundred dishes of food". When the monks told the Buddha, his answer was a warning: "Just as a plantain banana tree produces fruit to its own destruction and ruin, so too will Devadatta's material support, honor, and praise cause his own destruction and ruin."2

    Then, in front of a gathering that included the king, he asked for the Saṅgha: "Sir, you're now old and close to the end of life. … Hand the Sangha of monks over to me. Let me lead the Sangha." He asked three times, and the third time the Buddha called him "a wretched devourer of junk", a word Brahmāli explains as "an eater of spittle", the spittle being "the material support, honor, and praise he had acquired in an inappropriate manner".3 The chapter marks the moment: "This was the first time Devadatta had ill will toward the Buddha." What followed, the hired men, the stone and the elephant Nāḷāgiri, lies outside the Pātimokkha, but its result does not. People said, "How evil and indiscriminate he is, this Devadatta", and "Devadatta's material support and honor declined".4

    That decline is where his rules begin. "Because of his loss of material support and honor, Devadatta and his followers had to ask families repeatedly to get invited to meals," and people asked, "Who doesn't like nice food? Who doesn't prefer tasty food?" The rule against eating in a group, Pc 32, answers them.5 The Khandhaka tells the same story and gives the rule three reasons, the second of them "for the ease of good monks, stopping those with bad desires from creating a faction and then a schism in the Sangha".6 A rule about meals was, from the start, also a rule about harmony. At Pc 29, which the Vibhaṅga does not place in the sequence, the nun Thullanandā tried to turn a family's meal away from Sāriputta, Moggallāna and nine other senior monks, "such inferior monks", to "the great ones", Devadatta and his four companions. The householder threw her out, and Devadatta was rebuked for eating almsfood knowing that a nun had it prepared.7 Seen from outside, his following had become a party with great monks of its own.

    Then he went to the four: "Let's cause a schism in the Sangha of the ascetic Gotama. Let's break its authority." When Kokālika asked how, Devadatta's answer was a programme. They would ask the Buddha to make five practices binding for life, the wilderness, almsfood only, rag robes, the foot of a tree, and no fish or meat, knowing he would refuse, and then win people over with the five. Kokālika saw why it would work: "for people have confidence in austerity."8 The Buddha's refusal is the centre of the story. "No, Devadatta. Those who wish may stay in the wilderness, and those who wish may live near inhabited areas," and so for the first three; for the other two he pointed to what he had already allowed.9 Devadatta left "glad and elated" and told Rājagaha, "The ascetic Gotama doesn't agree to them, but we practice in accordance with them," and some people concluded that the Buddha "is extravagant and has chosen a life of indulgence".10 That is Sg 10. Sg 11 was laid down against the four who told the monks that Devadatta "speaks with our consent and approval. He knows about us and speaks for us, and we approve of this."11 The harmony of the Saṅgha, and how it guards itself against schism, is the subject of a theme of its own, in Themes.

    The Khandhaka finishes it. Five hundred Vajjian monks, "newly ordained and ignorant", voted for the five points, "Thinking, 'This is the Teaching, this is the Monastic Law, this is the Teacher's instruction'", and Devadatta left for Gayāsīsa with them. The Buddha let Sāriputta and Moggallāna go after them, "before they're afflicted with misfortune and disaster". Devadatta took their coming for approval, taught far into the night, asked Sāriputta to go on while he stretched his aching back, and fell asleep. While he slept the two of them taught the monks, who saw the Truth and went back with them to the Bamboo Grove. When Kokālika woke him with the news, "Devadatta vomited hot blood right there."12

    Taken as one story, the four rules show that the schism came dressed as strictness. Devadatta did not ask for an easier training. He asked for a harder one, and for the Buddha to make it everyone's, and the people who believed him believed it because the hard practices were real ones. The Buddha's answer kept the practices open to those who wished, within what he had already allowed, and refused only the compulsion. The same care can reasonably be read in Pc 32, which closes the smallest door to a faction, a shared table among supporters. A monk who takes up an austere practice has the Buddha's words to keep it in its place. It is his to choose, and it is not a measure of the monk who chooses otherwise: "Those who wish may eat only almsfood, and those who wish may accept invitational meals."13

    9. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Sg 10, origin story: "No, Devadatta. Those who wish may stay in the wilderness, and those who wish may live near inhabited areas. Those who wish may eat only almsfood, and those who wish may accept invitational meals. Those who wish may wear rag robes, and those who wish may accept robe-cloth from householders. I have allowed the foot of a tree as a resting place for eight months of the year, as well as fish and meat that are pure in three respects …"↩︎

    10. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Sg 10, origin story: "Glad and elated, he got up from his seat"; "The ascetic Gotama doesn't agree to them, but we practice in accordance with them."; "These Sakyan monastics are practicing asceticism and living with the aim of self-effacement.     11. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Sg 11, origin story.↩︎
