    Pj 3

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying in the house with the peaked roof in the Great Wood near Vesālī. He spoke to the monks in many ways about unattractiveness, praising unattractiveness, the development of the mind in unattractiveness, and its attainment. Then he told them he wished to go into solitary retreat for half a month, and that no one should visit him except the monk who brought his almsfood.

    The monks reflected that the Buddha had praised unattractiveness in many ways, and devoted themselves to developing it in its many facets. As a consequence they became troubled by their own bodies, ashamed of them and disgusted with them. "Just as a young woman or man — someone fond of adornments, with freshly washed hair — would be ashamed, humiliated, and disgusted if the carcass of a snake, dog, or man was hung around her neck, just so those monks were troubled by their own bodies." They took their own lives and took the lives of one another. Some went to Migalaṇḍika, the monastic lookalike, and said, "Please kill us. You will get our bowl and robes."

    Hired for a bowl and robes, Migalaṇḍika killed a number of monks. Then he took his bloodstained knife to the river Vaggumudā, and while washing it he became anxious and remorseful: "What the heck have I done? I've made so much demerit by killing good monks." A god from the realm of the Lord of Death came across the water and said to him, "Well done, superior man, you're truly fortunate. You've made much merit by helping across those who hadn't yet crossed." Migalaṇḍika concluded that he must be fortunate after all. He went from dwelling to dwelling and from yard to yard, asking, "Who hasn't crossed yet? Who can I help across?" The monks who still had worldly attachments became fearful and terrified; only those who were free from them were unaffected. On a single day Migalaṇḍika killed one monk, two monks, three, four, five, ten, twenty, thirty, forty, fifty, even sixty monks.

    When the Buddha came out of seclusion at the end of the half-month, he asked Ānanda why the Saṅgha of monks was so reduced. Ānanda told him what had happened and asked him to give another instruction, so that the Saṅgha might become established in perfect insight. The Buddha had all the monks living near Vesālī gathered in the assembly hall and taught them stillness by mindfulness of breathing: "Just as a great, unseasonal storm in the last month of summer removes the dust and dirt from the air, just so, when stillness by mindfulness of breathing is developed and cultivated, it is peaceful and sublime, and it removes bad and unwholesome qualities on the spot, whenever they arise." He set out the sixteen steps of the practice. Then he questioned the monks, rebuked those who had taken their own lives, killed one another and asked Migalaṇḍika to kill them, and laid down a rule against intentionally killing a human being or seeking an instrument of death for one.

    Later a lay follower fell sick. He had a beautiful and pleasant wife, and the monks of the group of six had fallen in love with her. "If this lay follower recovers, we won't get her," they said to each other. "Come, let's praise death to him." They went to him and said, "You've done what's good and wholesome; you've made a shelter against fear. You haven't done anything bad; you haven't been greedy or immoral. So why carry on with this miserable and difficult life? Death is better for you. When you've passed away, you'll be reborn in a happy place, in heaven." The man believed them. From then on he ate detrimental food and drank detrimental drinks, became very ill and died. His wife complained that the monks had praised death to her husband and that he was dead because of it, and other people complained in the same way. The Buddha rebuked the monks and extended the rule to praising death and inciting someone to die.1

    Synopsis — While the Buddha is in retreat, monks who have taken up the meditation on unattractiveness become disgusted with their own bodies; some take their own lives or one another's, and many are killed at their request by Migalaṇḍika, whom a god encourages to go on. The Buddha teaches mindfulness of breathing and lays down the rule against killing a human being. It is extended after monks who want a sick layman's wife praise death to him and he dies.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk intentionally kills a human being or seeks an instrument of death for them or praises death or incites someone to die, saying, 'My friend, what's the point of this miserable and difficult life? Death is better for you than life!' — thinking and intending thus, if he praises death in many ways or incites someone to die — he too is expelled and excluded from the community."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu intentionally causes the death of a human being, he incurs a pārājika."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu intentionally deprive a human being of life, or search for an assassin for him, or praise the advantages of death, or incite him to die (saying): 'My good man, what use is this evil, miserable life to you? Death would be better for you than life,' or with such an idea in mind, such a purpose in mind, should in various ways praise the advantages of death or incite him to die, he also is defeated and no longer in affiliation."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1, reproducing Thanissaro's rendering in a form that differs from the current BMC1, apparently an earlier printing)

    "Should any monk deliberately deprive a human being of life, or search for an assassin for him, or praise the advantages of death or incite him to die (thus): 'My good man, what use is this miserable, difficult life to you? Death would be better for you than life.' Or with such thoughts in mind, such intentions in mind, should he in various ways praise the advantages of death or incite him to die, he is also defeated and no longer in communion."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should deliberately deprive a human being of life, or should seek an assassin for him, or should praise the attractiveness of death, or should incite [him] to death [saying]: 'Dear man, what [use] is this bad, wretched life for you? Death is better than life for you!' should he, [having] such-thought-and-mind, [having such-] thought-and-intention, praise in manifold ways the beauty of death or incite [him] to death, he also is disqualified, not in communion."6

    The four full renderings name the same four routes to the offence, and their differences of wording ("advantages", "attractiveness", "beauty" of death) change nothing a monk must do. The recitation line gathers all four into "causes the death of", a single phrase chosen, Brahmāli explains in his Pātimokkha Manual, to catch them all.7 It is accurate, but a monk who knows only it will not know how many roads lead into this offence.

    On satthahārakaṃ the full renderings part. Thanissaro, Ajahn Brahm and Ñāṇatusita read a person, "an assassin"; Brahmāli reads a thing, "an instrument of death", and says in his note that he follows Richard Gombrich's interpretation.8 This does change what the rule covers, since on the first reading the phrase reaches a monk who arranges his own death at another's hands, a question discussed below, under Object.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule keeps a monk's hand and voice from any part in taking a human life, and its two stories show how far that reaches. The first came out of a catastrophe, and the Buddha answered it twice, with a rule and with a practice. The monks who died were not wicked: they had taken up a meditation without their teacher and turned it against themselves, and Migalaṇḍika killed them at their own request. The second story is the opposite case, monks with a base motive who never touched their victim and talked him into his grave. Between them they show that the rule covers the hand that kills and the voice that arranges, praises or urges a death, and, as we shall see in the case law, it makes no allowance for a kind motive.

    Each source counts the factors its own way. Ajahn Brahm uses four parts, "the intention, the action, the object and the result".9 The Manual follows the phrases of Brahmāli's rendering, folding perception into "intentionally" and speech, commanding and inciting into "causes".10 Thanissaro gives five, all of which must be present: object, intention, perception, effort and result.11 I follow Thanissaro's five, because perception is where one of this rule's live disagreements sits, and it is clearer with a factor of its own.

    Object

    A human being, in the Vibhaṅga's definition, exists "from the mind's first appearance in the mother's womb, from the first manifestation of consciousness, until the time of death".12 Thanissaro reasons from DN 15 that consciousness is present from conception, and Ajahn Brahm adds the Mahāvagga's ruling that a candidate for ordination may count his twenty years "from his conception".13​ 14 And so both conclude that a monk who intentionally brings about an abortion, by arranging the operation, supplying medicine or advising a woman who then follows the advice, incurs a pārājika, as does one who recommends a contraceptive that works after conception and is followed.15 The Vinītavatthu bears them out: a monk who helps a woman abort, or tells her "crush it" or "heat yourself" and she does, is expelled.16

    Two neighbouring cases in the Vinītavatthu show where the medical edge lies. A monk gives medicine to a barren woman who wants to conceive, and another to a fertile woman who does not; in each case the woman dies, and the monk incurs a dukkaṭa. The Commentary infers that monks are not to act as doctors to lay people, though it allows exceptions.17

    Killing a non-human being, such as a spirit, a ghost or a nāga, is a thullaccaya, the penalty the Vinītavatthu gives an exorcist monk who killed a spirit; commanding a possessing spirit to leave, the Commentary adds, does it no harm and is no offence.18 An animal is the object of Pc 61, not of this rule.

    One's own life is not this rule's object. The Vinītavatthu tells of a monk "plagued by lust" who jumped from the Vulture Peak and killed a basket-maker below; there was no pārājika, and the Buddha laid down a dukkaṭa for jumping off anything.19 Thanissaro argues from the size of that penalty: the monk probably felt pain when he landed, so under this rule he would have incurred a thullaccaya.20 The Commentary extends the dukkaṭa to any attempt on one's own life, refusing food included, but allows a sick monk to refuse food and medicine where a long illness has worn out those caring for him and his body will not last, and where his illness is harsh and some attainment seems within reach.21 Thanissaro questions both, since they add motive and perception to a rule that has neither and draw no line for how ill or how near is enough. He would apply the subsidiary rule only where an attempt endangers another person, as at the cliff, and treat the rest as a matter of Dhamma, remembering the Buddha's word that the only blameless death is an arahant's.22

    Disputed — whether the rule reaches a monk who arranges for another person to take his own life.

    The rule names a monk who seeks a satthahāraka, and Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita read the word as an agent noun, "knife-carrier", that is, an assassin: Thanissaro because it "clearly means 'assassin'" elsewhere in the Canon; Ñāṇatusita because hāraka is unambiguously "carrier" at NP 16, and because in the origin story it is a person, Migalaṇḍika, who kills at the monks' request.23​ 24 Since the Canon uses the word mostly of someone who kills a person at that person's request, Thanissaro concludes that a monk intent on dying who arranges for another to do it falls under the rule, which "would rule out trying to get a doctor to arrange an assisted suicide for oneself", for he asks another to take on the kamma of killing a human being.25 The Commentary reads the other way on both counts. It takes satthahāraka, Thanissaro reports, as a general term for a murderous weapon and the whole phrase as setting up a stationary device;26 and it "categorically states", in Ajahn Brahm's report, "that there is no pārājika for the bhikkhu who kills himself or has some obliging fellow kill on request".27 Brahmāli's "instrument of death" sits with the Commentary on the word, though he does not say where he stands on the consequence. Thanissaro answers that the device reading makes the phrase redundant, since stationary devices are already covered as ways of taking life. Each reading has something the other lacks, the one the word's grammar and its use elsewhere in the Canon, the other the Vibhaṅga's own gloss as the Commentary reads it, and nothing in the texts weighs the one against the other, so the question is left open here; a monk weighing an assisted death for himself should know that on Thanissaro's reading the rule forbids it, and arranging it incurs the rule's derived offences.

    Intention

    The word-analysis defines intentionally as "knowing, perceiving, having intended, having decided, he transgresses", and the rule's "intending" as "perceiving death, intending death, aiming at death".28 Thanissaro reads these terms through the non-offence clause, whose three exemptions the Vinītavatthu illustrates. Unintentionally is an accident, such as a brick dropped on a monk below while building. Not knowing is a deliberate act done without knowing it could kill, such as sharing almsfood one does not know is poisoned. Not aiming at death is a deliberate act not meant to end a life, such as striking a choking monk on the neck to dislodge the meat.29 The Vinītavatthu repeats these as pairs. The same blow on the neck, the same push of a father told that the Saṅgha is waiting, the same instruction to stand on a building platform, is no offence when death was not the aim and expulsion when it was; where the aim was death and the victim lived, it is a thullaccaya.30

    Two refinements narrow the factor. Thanissaro notes that the word-analysis requires a decision: a monk who strikes "unthinkingly in a sudden fit of rage, without being clear about what his intention was" has not acted intentionally under this rule, and the Commentary agrees.31 And the Commentary explains the Vinītavatthu's monk who gave another poison "with the purpose of investigating it" and incurred only a thullaccaya: testing whether a poison can kill, or whether a man can survive it, is not aiming at death, but a monk who believes the poison lethal and gives it to kill commits the full offence if the man dies.32

    Motive, by contrast, does not narrow it at all. In the Vinītavatthu's first case, monks praise death to a sick fellow monk "out of compassion", and he dies; they are expelled.33 A monk at a place of execution asks the executioner not to torture the condemned man but to kill him with a single blow; the executioner does so, and the monk is expelled.34 Thanissaro concludes that "impulse and motive are irrelevant in defining the factor of intention here", and Brahmāli's introduction draws the same conclusion from the first case: the monks spoke "out of compassion" and were expelled, so "a monastic may never suggest dying for any reason whatsoever, no matter how positively motivated".35​ 36

    What counts is the intention present in the act itself. A monk who kills a man, mistaking him for the one he meant to kill, is expelled: the Vinītavatthu gives the pārājika for killing "another person, thinking it was them", and the Commentary explains that his intention was to kill the person he was aiming at. If he aims at his target, misses, and kills a bystander, he never intended the bystander's death and incurs only the dukkaṭas of the attempt.37 Thanissaro finds an apparent contradiction here, in the Vinītavatthu's case of a monk who gives an abortifacient, after which the mother dies and the child survives, and who incurs only a thullaccaya; Brahmāli's note resolves it in a line, since the monk "was aiming to kill the child, not the mother".38​ 39 It is the missed shot, not mistaken identity.

    Perception

    The monk must at least perceive that someone is there who could die. The Vinītavatthu's monks of the group of six set fire to a forest grove and people burned to death; with no aim at death, there was no offence, and Thanissaro concludes that aiming at death includes the perception of a being who could die.40 The Commentary sets the standard there: one must be aware that "this is a living being", and need not know that it is human.41

    Disputed — whether a monk who kills a human being he takes for an animal has committed the offence.

    The Commentary's example is a monk who sees a goat lying in a certain spot by day and returns at night to kill it, not knowing that the goat has gone and a man has lain down in its place; he kills the man, still taking him for the goat, and the verdict is pārājika. Thanissaro grants that "this judgment may seem strange" but finds "nothing in the Canon to contradict it", and gives the Vibhaṅga's non-specific pit as the closest support: a monk digs a pit meaning that whatever falls into it should die, and if a human being falls in and dies he is expelled, though he never thought of a human being.42 Brahmāli replies from the rule's own words, sañcicca manussaviggahaṃ jīvitā voropeyya, "if one should intentionally deprive a human being of life": the intention the rule requires is the intention to kill a human, and a monk who perceives an animal does not have it, even if he is wrong.43 The pit case may not decide it, since there the monk's intention took in any being at all, humans included, which the goat-killer's did not. I lean toward Brahmāli's reading, because the rule's wording supports it; but no case under this rule has a monk mistake a man for an animal, and the Commentary's verdict, which the Canon does not contradict, is the only ruling on killing there is. The lean is a judgment against it, not a finding.

    Effort

    Effort is anything done to bring the death about, and the rule names four kinds: taking life, seeking an assassin, praising death, and inciting someone to die.44 The second has been treated under Object.

    Taking life. The Vibhaṅga defines kills as "cuts off the life faculty, brings it to an end, interrupts its continuation", whether by the body, by "something connected to the body" or by "something released".45​ 46 Its permutations then work through a pit, rigged furniture, a weapon or poison "placed near" someone in the hope he will use it on himself, a tonic given to kill, and a sight, sound, smell, taste, touch or mental quality arranged to frighten someone to death.47 Thanissaro brings the list up to date with the gun and the land mine.48 The pit shows how intention shapes a device. Dug for a particular person, according to the Commentary, it makes the monk liable only if that person falls in; dug for "whatever falls into it", it brings expulsion if a human dies in it, a thullaccaya for a spirit and a pācittiya for an animal.49

    A monk who sets such a thing up and then thinks better of it is not freed by changing his mind. According to the Commentary he must fill in the pit so that no one can so much as stumble in it, and destroy a trap, or a writing praising death, past reassembling; if a half-filled pit causes an injury that later kills, he incurs the full offence.50 Ajahn Brahm's trap, thrown away and found by a bandit twenty years later, must be burned to ashes.51

    Commanding. Telling another to kill is a dukkaṭa at once, and if the other kills the person named, both are expelled. The Vibhaṅga lets the instigator out only where the act departs from what he set in motion: the killer kills someone else, a messenger in a chain passes the order to the wrong man, or the killing comes before or after the time arranged or the sign agreed. Regret frees him only if it reaches the killer. If he regrets the order but does not say "Don't kill them", both are expelled; if he says it and the other kills anyway, only the killer is; if the other says "Fine" and desists, neither has an offence.52 The Commentary extends the instigator's escape to six particulars he may specify, namely the person, the time, the place, the weapon, its use and the victim's posture, so that a student told to kill a man sitting in meditation at midnight who kills him asleep in bed leaves his teacher with a thullaccaya.53

    The Vibhaṅga's commands are all imperatives, and its only indirect speech is a monk saying aloud, "I wish so-and-so was killed", a dukkaṭa.54 The Commentary holds that a monk who announces that whoever beheads a certain bandit will be honoured by the king is expelled if a listener does it, and Ajahn Brahm reproduces the case; Thanissaro infers from the Vibhaṅga's silence that such statements are not effort under this rule.55​ 56 The safe course needs no ruling: a monk who finds himself hoping aloud for a death has already said too much.

    Praising death and inciting to die. Praise shows "the drawbacks of living" and speaks "in praise of death"; incitement says "Kill yourself with a knife", "Eat poison", "Jump off a cliff".57 Either may be given by gesture, speech, writing or messenger, and each runs the same scale: a dukkaṭa for the praise, a thullaccaya if the listener resolves to die and does something painful, expulsion if he dies.58 It covers telling someone how to die, asked or unasked, and arranging something lovely as well as something dreadful, "thinking, 'Seeing this and then being unable to get hold of it, they'll wither and die'".59 Talking of heaven is not in itself the offence: the Vinītavatthu absolves a monk who did so without aiming at death, and Thanissaro notes that in SN 55.54 the Buddha advises turning a dying lay follower's mind toward heaven.60

    We now come to a question a monk may well meet in practice, at a sickbed. Doing nothing does not cut off a life; it lets one end. Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm agree that a monk who stands by while a man drowns, or otherwise does nothing while a life ends, has not fulfilled this factor, whatever he feels about the death.61​ 62 Where they part is over a monk who tells someone else to stand by.

    Disputed — whether advising against an intervention, or advising that treatment be withdrawn, is an act under this rule when a death follows.

    Ajahn Brahm gives two monks who each wish a brain-damaged disciple dead. One makes no effort to prolong his life and incurs no offence; the other advises the doctor to make none, the doctor complies, and he is expelled, because "in the latter the bhikkhu performed the action of giving advice".63 Thanissaro reasons the other way: the category of command covers only inciting someone to one of the four actions the rule names, and withholding or discontinuing a treatment does not cut off the life faculty but "at most" allows it "to end on its own". His canonical evidence is Mv.VIII.26.3–4, which imposes only a dukkaṭa on refusing all treatment to a sick monk or abandoning his care, so that the compilers cannot have regarded withdrawing care as killing; and he adds that a mandated standard of care would expose the sick to abandonment.64 Both sides argue from the Canon. I find Ajahn Brahm's reading the safer for a monk to live by, since a monk who follows it has no offence under this rule on Thanissaro's reading either; but safety is not proof, and between two canonical arguments the choice is mine.

    Brahmāli's introduction puts the general point: "Monastic Law and kamma are two different things. The Monastic Law was laid down for a number of reasons, whereas kamma is governed strictly by intention."65 It follows, on our reading, that a monk who coldly lets a man die has no offence here and has still done something unwholesome.

    What, then, may a monk say at a bedside? Thanissaro holds that telling a dying patient not to fight death, in the hope that he will let the life faculty end, is not aiming at death; but since intentions are hard to read in such moments, he advises avoiding it and helping the patient toward a skilful state of mind.66 Where lay people ask about death, suicide or euthanasia, Brahmāli held that a monk who feels uncomfortable may say so and send them to a senior teacher, but that in the end a monk owes them the best account he can give of what the Buddha taught.67 The same test of intention covers public speech. Ajahn Brahm gives a monk who lectures that euthanasia is the compassionate choice and is followed by a listener: no offence, since he "spoke hypothetically, not meaning to be the cause of death".68

    Result

    The death must follow from what the monk did. If it does not, the attempt draws a thullaccaya where the victim suffers pain or injury and a dukkaṭa for each step where he does not.69 Since every life ends eventually, the Commentary works out how far responsibility reaches: a victim who climbs out of the pit but dies years later of a disease caught there counts, unless complications were the predominant cause; so does one who falls in while fleeing thieves and is killed by them, since the pit made the killing possible.70 A monk who means to kill any of a group is expelled when any one of them dies.71

    The rule's origin story belongs beside its case law. When the Buddha came out of retreat and found the Saṅgha diminished, he answered first with a practice, and only then with the rule: mindfulness of breathing, which removes bad and unwholesome qualities as a storm at the end of summer clears the dust from the air. The rule fixes the edge a monk must never cross. The practice was what the monks had lacked when they fell.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — each step toward a killing, and each character written in praise of death; telling another to kill; saying aloud that one wishes someone killed; digging a pit to kill; a monk who asks an executioner to kill with a single blow and is refused; the attempt that misses and kills someone else.72​ 73
    • Dukkaṭa — giving medicine to a lay woman to help her conceive, or not to, where she dies.74
    • Thullaccaya — the intended victim suffers pain or injury but does not die; the agreement of the killer in a chain of messengers; killing a non-human being; testing a poison on a person, whether he dies or not; an abortifacient that kills the mother while the child survives.75​ 76
    • Pācittiya — an animal killed in a pit dug for whatever falls in (Pc 61).77

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if it is unintentional, if he does not know, if he is not aiming at death, if he is insane, or if he is the first offender.78 Not knowing means not knowing that the act could cause death (see Intention). Thanissaro notes that the Thai edition also exempts a monk possessed by spirits or delirious with pain, which other editions omit, and that the Commentary's "insane, etc." suggests it knew them.79 Among the Vinītavatthu's cases with no pārājika are monks who tickled a fellow monk until he could not catch his breath and died, and monks who overpowered one, intending a legal procedure against him, and he died.80

    Related Offences

    • Pc 61 — intentionally killing an animal.
    • Pc 74 — striking a monk; where the aim was injury and not death and the victim dies, Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm refer the case there.
    • Pc 52 — tickling; its origin story is the same death this rule's Vinītavatthu clears of expulsion.
    • Pc 20, Pc 62 — pouring out, or using, water known to contain living beings.
    • Three subsidiary rules (this rule's own dukkaṭas) — sitting down on a seat without checking it, after a monk sat on a bench and crushed a boy hidden under a cloth; throwing stones for fun; jumping from a height.
    • Pc 18 — where the bench case's lesson, to look before one sits, applies to a bed or bench on an upper storey.
    • Sg 13 — whose Vibhaṅga supports the Commentary's inference, drawn from this rule's medicine cases, that monks are not to act as doctors to lay people.
    • Pj 2 — the instigator and the messenger, where the same machinery governs a theft.

    Related Suttas

    • Dhp 129–130 — "everyone fears death … everyone likes to live; / recognizing that you are similar, / do not harm or cause others to harm"; the verse's last line is the rule's reach, the hand that kills and the voice that has another kill.81
    • MN 8 — among the first resolves of effacement, the wearing away of the defilements: "'Others will kill living beings; we shall abstain from killing living beings here'"; the rule holds the floor of that resolve where the life is human.82
    • SN 55.7 — the noble disciple who, wishing to live himself, "abstains from the destruction of life, exhorts others to abstain from the destruction of life, and speaks in praise of abstinence from the destruction of life", the rule's own routes turned the other way.83
    • SN 55.54 — the Buddha's counsel to Mahānāma on how one wise lay follower should console another who is gravely ill: turn his mind from human pleasures to the heavens, and beyond them to "the cessation of identity". Talk of heaven aimed at a dying man's peace, not at his death, is the edge the rule's second story crossed.84
