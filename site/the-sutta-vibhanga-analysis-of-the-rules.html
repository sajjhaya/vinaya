    The Sutta Vibhaṅga — Analysis of the Rules

    The Vinaya Piṭaka falls into two main parts: the Sutta Vibhaṅga, the Analysis of the Pātimokkha rules, and the Khandhakas, the Chapters. Some traditions add further material: the Theravāda's own Parivāra, the Compendium, or the Mūlasarvāstivādins' Uttaragrantha.1 The Theravāda Sutta Vibhaṅga contains two divisions: the Bhikkhu-vibhaṅga, analysing the 227 rules for monks, and the Bhikkhunī-vibhaṅga, analysing the 311 rules for nuns. The majority of rules are shared between the two codes, but 130 are unique to the nuns and 46 unique to the monks, a discrepancy taken up in "A Note on the Bhikkhunī-vibhaṅga" below.2

    Rules are organised by the penalty incurred for breaching them, in roughly descending order of severity. Pārājika offences entail expulsion — four for monks, eight for nuns — reserved for conduct incompatible with monastic life. Saṅghādisesa offences entail a period of penance, with probation before it if the offence was concealed: thirteen for monks, seventeen for nuns. Together, these two classes are known as the "heavy offences", garukāpatti; everything else is "light", lahukāpatti, clearable by confession alone, subdivided by the severity of the breach, the form of confession required, and whether relinquishment of wrongly acquired requisites is also needed. The last seven rules of the Sutta Vibhaṅga, the adhikaraṇasamathadhammas, are not offences at all but principles for resolving legal issues. Their fuller explanation now lies mostly in the Khandhakas; in the pātimokkha itself the seven principles were reduced, over time, to little more than a bare list of key words.3

    The Vibhaṅga embeds the pātimokkha rules within a detailed analytical commentary, in effect the Vinaya's own "ancient commentary", structured around the pātimokkha as its organising kernel.4 Each rule is presented with the following structure:

    • An origin story (or several), relating the events that led the Buddha to lay the rule down. Some are no more than a single stock sentence about a stereotyped offending monk; a few are elaborate narratives that incorporate sub-rules, procedures, or even sutta-type or Jātaka-type material; most fall somewhere between these extremes.5
    • The rule itself, often amended one or more times before reaching its final form. Each amendment is prompted by a further origin story, and each new formulation supersedes the one before it. More than 40 of the monks' rules were amended at least once, including every one of the four pārājikas.6
    • A word analysis (padabhājanīya), defining the rule's significant terms: sometimes no more than a synonym, sometimes a lengthy technical exposition.
    • A permutation series, working out the rule's application across every combination of its relevant factors. For the heaviest offences, these series are exceptionally long, sometimes more than half the rule's total word count. A few of these series add real interpretive nuance. Too often, in Brahmāli's judgement, a series seems to be little more than a mechanical listing of combinations, of limited value beyond showing that no relevant case has been left out.7
    • Non-offence clauses, listing the circumstances under which no offence is incurred. Four such circumstances recur as a kind of standing background exemption across nearly the whole pātimokkha: insanity, derangement or possession, being overwhelmed by pain, and being the first offender (i.e., the rules are not retroactive).8
    • Case studies, found only for the four pārājikas and the first five saṅghādisesas, in which the Buddha adjudicates a specific, often ambiguous real-world scenario and rules on whether or not it constitutes an offence.9

    The Vibhaṅga covers most of what is needed to understand a rule; where it is silent, the entries turn to the Khandhakas, or to the later commentaries as the modern sources report them.

    One disagreement runs through the whole book and is best met here. Thanissaro and Brahmāli, the two scholars this book relies on most, do not agree on how much authority the Vibhaṅga itself should carry relative to the bare pātimokkha rule when the two appear to be in tension. Thanissaro, citing a Cullavagga passage in which the Buddha tells Mahāpajāpatī Gotamī that in the rules they share with the bhikkhus the bhikkhunīs should train "as the bhikkhus train themselves", and in the rest as the rules stand, argues that the Vibhaṅga generally reflects the Buddha's own intent and should be given real interpretive weight, since it was the bhikkhus themselves, under his apparent sanction, who first worked out how the bare rules applied in practice.10 Brahmāli, by contrast, holds, as his treatment of the self-intercourse question at Pj 1 shows, that where a clear conflict exists between a rule's own wording and the Vibhaṅga's later gloss on it, the rule itself should generally be preferred.11 He also dates the two layers apart. In his account the pātimokkha rules, with a few of the most important legal procedures, are the parts of the Vinaya likely to go back to the Buddha's lifetime, and the origin stories, word analyses, permutation series, non-offence clauses and case studies gathered around them over time as a canonical commentary, a later addition for which, he writes, "there is significant evidence".12 Both reason from canonical evidence. The disagreement is not resolved here, and the reader should expect it to return, case by case, throughout the rules ahead.
