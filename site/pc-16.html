    Pc 16

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī, in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time the monks of the group of six had taken the best sleeping places, and the senior monks evicted them. Wondering how they might secure a place to stay for the rains, they arranged their sleeping places so as to encroach on the senior monks, thinking, "Whoever feels crowded will leave."

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised them: "How could the monks from the group of six arrange their sleeping places so as to encroach on the senior monks?" They told the Buddha, who questioned the monks, confirmed that it was so, and laid down the training rule.

    Synopsis — Evicted from the best sleeping places they had seized, the group-of-six monks crowd in on the senior monks by laying their bedding right up against them, meaning to make them leave; the Buddha forbids knowingly encroaching, in a Saṅgha dwelling, on a monk who arrived first, for the sole purpose of driving him out.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If, in a dwelling belonging to the Sangha, a monk arranges his sleeping place in a way that encroaches on a monk that he knows arrived there before him, with the intention that anyone who feels crowded will leave, and he does so only for this reason and no other, he commits an offense entailing confession."1

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu lies down in a dwelling belonging to the Saṅgha, knowingly encroaching on a bhikkhu and aiming to make him depart, he incurs a pācittiya."2

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu knowingly lie down in a dwelling belonging to the Community so as to intrude on a bhikkhu who arrived there first, (thinking), 'Whoever finds it confining will go away'—doing it for just that reason and no other—it is to be confessed."3

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Encroaching on another monks sleeping place to make him uncomfortable and drive him away is an offence of Pācittiya."4

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu, having encroached upon a bhikkhu who has arrived before, should knowingly use a sleeping place in a dwelling belonging to the community [saying]: "He for whom it is [too] cramped, will leave," having done [it] for just this reason, [and] not another, [this is a case] involving expiation."5

    The five renderings agree. The recitation and Ajahn Brahm compress: Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual leaves out "previously arrived" as "implied in 'encroaching'", and gives the monk's thought, "for whom there will be confinement, he will depart", as "aiming to make him depart".6 What the shorter renderings drop is the rule's last clause, "only for this reason and no other", which Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga, Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita all keep. Everything hangs on that clause, and it is taken up under Intention.7​ 8

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    What the Saṅgha has given to one monk, another may not take from him by pressure, and this rule holds that line, for the concord of the Saṅgha and the ease of the monks who live in it. The monks of the group of six had been turned out of the best places by their seniors. They did not argue the allotment; they lay down close against the senior monks and waited, "Whoever feels crowded will leave." The rule names that tactic exactly, and it names only that: crowding a monk with no other aim than to make him go.

    Thanissaro gives the offence four factors: object, perception, effort and intention.9 The Manual has three, knowing, encroaching and the aim, with perception folded into the first.10 Thanissaro's four are kept here, though object and perception travel together, as the Vibhaṅga's own definition of "knowing" joins them.

    Object and perception

    The object is a monk who should not be forced to move, and the encroacher must know it. The Vibhaṅga spells out the knowing: he knows the occupant is senior, or sick, or that the dwelling was given to him by the Saṅgha.11 A dwelling given by the Saṅgha's lodging official counts as given by the Saṅgha.12 Thanissaro reports the Commentary reading the three as examples and generalising to any case where one knows, "This bhikkhu shouldn't be forced to move."13 Perception of whether the dwelling belongs to the Saṅgha does not soften the offence: if it is the Saṅgha's, the factor is met whatever the encroacher takes it to be, as at Pc 4.14

    Effort

    To encroach is to put one's sleeping place at the access to the occupant's bed or bench, or at the entrance or the way out: putting it there is a dukkaṭa, and sitting or lying down on it is the pācittiya.15 By Thanissaro's report the Commentary measures the zone at seventy-five centimetres around the occupant's place, and a path of the same width to the door. Thanissaro counts a fresh pācittiya each time the encroacher sits or lies down there. Bedding placed elsewhere in the dwelling with the same aim is a dukkaṭa, and sitting or lying on it another.16 The same crowding outside a Saṅgha dwelling, in an assembly hall, under a roof cover, at the foot of a tree or out in the open, is a dukkaṭa.17 So a monk who crowds another out of the corner of a meditation hall where he has chosen to sleep has done wrong, though not the full offence.

    Intention

    Intention carries the rule's weight. The offence falls only where the sole purpose is to make the occupant leave: he does so "only for this reason and no other".18 The Vibhaṅga names the reasons that break the factor: there is no offence if he enters because he is sick, because he is cold or hot, or because there is an emergency.19 Ajahn Brahm adds a case a crowded modern monastery knows well: encroaching "for some other reason, for instance there are many monks and few lodgings, is no offence."20

    Could a monk plead his cold as cover, while in truth he wants the other out? Thanissaro answers from the Vibhaṅga's wording. Only a sole motive brings the offence, so where the motive is mixed, real discomfort together with a wish to see the other go, there is none. What he may not do is stay on once the reason has passed: after the illness is gone, each further time he sits or lies down in the occupant's space is an offence.21 The allowance looks strange at first, and Thanissaro shows why it is not. The occupant would hardly feel pressed by a sick monk sheltering beside him; he would feel it keenly once that monk had recovered and still showed no sign of moving.22

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • A dukkaṭa for putting one's sleeping place at the access to the occupant's bed or bench, the entrance or the way out, without yet sitting or lying down on it.23
    • A dukkaṭa for placing one's bedding elsewhere in the Saṅgha dwelling with the aim of forcing the occupant out, and another for sitting or lying down on it.24
    • A dukkaṭa for the same encroachment around a dwelling, in an assembly hall, under a roof cover, at the foot of a tree, in the open, or in a dwelling belonging to another individual; in one's own dwelling there is no offence.25

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he enters because he is sick; because he is cold or hot; if there is an emergency; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.26

    Related Offences

    • Pc 15 — the preceding rule, whose place-graded dukkaṭas and Pc 4 perception ruling this rule reuses; there the concern is bedding abandoned, here a monk deliberately crowded.
    • Pc 17 — the companion rule: where this one meets the monk who squeezes another out, Pc 17 meets the monk who throws him out in anger. Both grew from the group of six's attempts to take over a Saṅgha dwelling.
    • Pc 43 — the other rule built on anupakhajja, "encroaching": there a monk intrudes by sitting down in a house where a couple would be private.

    Related Suttas

    • MN 31 — Anuruddha and his companions living "in concord… blending like milk and water", each asking himself, "Why should I not set aside what I wish to do and do what these venerable ones wish to do?", the opposite of the encroacher's thought.27

    A note on sourcing

    The Story, the rule, the definitions of knowing and of arranging a sleeping place, the permutations and the non-offence clauses are from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation, and the recitation line from his Pātimokkha recitation translation. The Manual gives his factors and his notes on the rule's wording. Thanissaro's BMC1 supplies the four factors, the Commentary's generalisation of knowing and its seventy-five-centimetre zone, as he reports them, the graded penalties, the perception ruling and the reading of the sole-motive clause. Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes adds the many-monks-and-few-lodgings case. The Concise BMC was checked and condenses BMC1 without adding to it. Ñāṇatusita gives the rule translation. The class transcript (session 023, 10 May 2012) was read for its questions, on why the senior and the sick are named and whether the rule reaches a shared hall; the Vibhaṅga and Thanissaro answer both in writing, and the class is not cited. MN 31 is quoted in Bodhi's translation.
