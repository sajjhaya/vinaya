    Pc 30

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time Venerable Udāyī's ex-wife became a nun. She often went to see Udāyī, and he often went to see her. And Udāyī would sit down in private alone with that nun. The monks of few desires complained and criticised him: "How can Venerable Udāyī sit down in private alone with a nun?" They told the Buddha, who questioned him, established that it was true, and rebuked him — "Foolish man, how can you do this? This will affect people's confidence" — and laid down the training rule.1

    Synopsis — Udāyī and his former wife, now a nun, visit one another often and sit alone together in private; the Buddha forbids it, in a rule laid down once and never amended.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk sits down in private alone with a nun, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu, aiming at privacy, sits alone in private with a bhikkhunī, he incurs a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu sit in private, alone with a bhikkhunī, it is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Sitting in private with a bhikkhunī is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should take seat with a bhikkhunī, privately, one [man] with one [woman], [this is a case] involving expiation."6

    The renderings agree, and the recitation is fuller than any of them. Its "aiming at privacy" is in no rule text; it comes from the non-offence clause "if he is not seeking privacy", so the recitation states as part of the rule what the Vibhaṅga leaves to its exemptions, as it does at Pc 44 and Pc 45.7​ 8 Eko ekāya, "one [man] with one [woman]", has the most work to do. The Vibhaṅga defines the two separately, "alone" as "just the monk and the nun" and "in private" by what others can see and hear, and the difference is what decides the rule's exemption, discussed below under Effort.9

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    Alone with a nun, out of everyone's sight and hearing, a monk's restraint is most exposed and his good name least defended, and this rule closes that door, for his own practice and for the good name of the Saṅgha. The Story is brief and needs no more: Udāyī's former wife became a nun, the two often went to see each other, and he would sit down with her alone in private; the rebuke is the chapter's usual one, that it "will affect people's confidence".10

    The sources that place this rule treat it as a satellite. Thanissaro writes that except for one rare case it is "completely subsumed under Pc 45", and Ajahn Brahm calls it "essentially identical" with that rule.11​ 12 So the machinery it shares with Pc 44 and Pc 45, and the privacy vocabulary of Aniyata 1 and Aniyata 2, are set out there. The three factors those rules carry, object, effort and intention, serve here too, and each needs only what is particular to a nun.

    Object

    A nun fully ordained by both Saṅghas is grounds for the pācittiya, and the word-analysis says nothing more about her.13 That silence is Thanissaro's "one rare case". At Pc 45 the woman must know what is lewd and what is not; this rule carries no such requirement, so a nun who did not know would still fall inside it.14​ 15 The understanding this rule does ask for belongs to someone else, the companion whose presence exempts. Nor does it grade the object: there is no lesser offence for a nun ordained on one side only, for a trainee nun or a novice nun.16

    Effort

    "Sits" covers every order in which two people come to be seated or lying down: he beside her, she beside him, or both together.17 Lying down is included throughout, and standing is not: a monk who stands and does not sit commits no offence.18

    "Alone" means just the two of them, so any third person who understands removes the offence.19​ 20 That person may be a woman as well as a man: the non-offence clause here has only "a companion who understands", where Pc 44 and Pc 45 have "a male companion", and Thanissaro, at Pc 45, reads the eko ekāya wording the same way and reports that the Commentary says so explicitly.21​ 22 Here the rule parts from Pc 44, whose exemption needs a man. Thanissaro further cites the Commentary: the companion must be awake, not deaf or blind, and within sight, which he puts at a radius of six metres; whether he is distracted or drowsy does not matter.23

    "In private" is the aniyata pair of tests: no one else can see the two of them wink, raise an eyebrow or nod, and no one can hear ordinary speech.24 Both must hold. The place need not be hidden. That is what separates this rule from Pc 44, whose seat is concealed: Ajahn Brahm's example is sitting out in the open with a nun where nobody can see or hear, and Thanissaro's at Pc 45 include a bench in a deserted park and an open-air pavilion.25​ 26 Perception of the privacy does not mitigate: if the place is private, the offence falls whether he perceives it, is unsure, or does not; if it is not, perceiving it as private or being unsure is wrong conduct.27

    Intention

    A monk who is not seeking privacy commits no offence, nor one who sits down preoccupied with something else.28 What "seeking privacy" means the Vibhaṅga never says, and the readings of it divide: not wanting anyone near enough to see or hear, any sexual motive, or simply having meant to be alone with her. They are set out in the Disputed passage at Pc 44, the rule where the question does most work. Ajahn Brahm's example shows the ordinary case: a monk is sitting in a group when everyone else suddenly departs, leaving him alone with a nun, and he has not aimed at privacy.29

    But the exemption is not one to lean on. Thanissaro warns, at Pc 44, that sitting alone with a woman is dangerous even when one's intention is innocent: one's own defilements may tempt, and "even when his motives are pure, he is inviting the suspicions of others."30 A factor resting on one's own intention is a poor thing to rely on, and a monk who keeps a companion near when he speaks with a nun has no need to.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • An offence of wrong conduct where the place is not in fact private, but the monk perceives that it is or is unsure.31

    The Vibhaṅga gives no lesser offence for a nun ordained on one side only, a trainee nun or a novice nun.32

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he has a companion who understands; if he stands and does not sit down; if he is not seeking privacy; if he sits down preoccupied with something else; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.33

    Related Offences

    • Pc 45 — sitting alone in private with a woman; the same rule with the object widened, and the place where Thanissaro sets out this rule's analysis. The two rules share a title in the Pāli.
    • Pc 44 — sitting in private on a concealed seat with a woman or women; unlike this rule, it needs a man to exempt, and it carries the Disputed passage on what "seeking privacy" means.
    • Aniyata 1, Aniyata 2 — where the vocabulary of privacy is defined, and where the same conduct, reported by a trustworthy laywoman, produces an undetermined charge. Aniyata 2's seat is this rule's seat: private, and not concealed.
    • Pc 27, Pc 28 — the travel rules, where the objection survives the presence of other people; here it does not.

    Related Suttas

    • AN 5.55 — a monk and a nun, son and mother, who "often saw one another" until a bond, then intimacy, then lust followed: the course this rule closes where a monk is most exposed.34
