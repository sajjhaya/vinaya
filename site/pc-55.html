    Pc 55

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. The monks from the group of six were scaring the monks from the group of seventeen. They cried. Other monks asked them why, and they told them.

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised them, "How can the monks from the group of six scare other monks?" The Buddha questioned them, established that it was true, rebuked them, and stated the rule.1

    Synopsis — Four sentences before the complaint, and the only detail is in the third: they cried. Nothing is said about what was done to them, and the rule that follows does not require that it work.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk scares a monk, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "Trying to frighten a bhikkhu is a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu try to frighten another bhikkhu, it is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Attempting to frighten another monk is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should scare [another] bhikkhu, [this is a case] involving expiation."6

    The renderings agree on what is forbidden and differ on one word. Three of them, Brahmāli's recitation line, Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm, make the verb an attempt: "trying", "try", "attempting". The Pāli has no such word. It is the word-analysis's repeated clause, "whether the other monk is scared or not", written back into the rule, while Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation and Ñāṇatusita keep the bare verb.7​ 8 Nothing changes in what the rule requires, since the analysis says it in as many words. The verb itself, bhiṁsāpeyya, is a causative, "should make afraid", and Ñāṇatusita suggests the form was chosen "probably because the other bhikkhu is frightened through a sound, sight etc.", that is, through the sights, sounds and other things the word-analysis lists.9

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A monk who sets out to frighten a fellow monk has made the other's fear his aim, and this rule forbids the aim itself, for the sake of his own conduct and the ease of the monks he lives among. The origin story gives no method and no detail. The group of six were scaring the group of seventeen, and they cried.10 The rule forbids the attempt, and the Vibhaṅga is explicit that it makes no difference whether the attempt succeeds.

    Thanissaro gives three factors: intention, effort and object.11 They are taken here in reverse, so that the intention, where the rule's one exemption lies, comes last.

    Object

    The other must be a fully ordained monk. Anyone else is grounds for an offence of wrong conduct, through both ways of frightening, and what one perceives about his status makes no difference, the pattern Thanissaro refers to Pc 42.12​ 13

    Effort

    The Vibhaṅga gives two ways of trying.14 The first is arranging something for the senses: a sight, a sound, a smell, a taste or a physical contact. Thanissaro's examples are a sheet hung in a dark room to look like a ghost and a ghostly wail outside the window; Ajahn Brahm's are a hair-raising ghost story and jumping out from behind a tree to shout "Boo!"15​ 16 The second is speech, telling him "about a wilderness inhabited by criminals, predatory animals, or demons."17 Neither the word-analysis nor Thanissaro asks whether the danger described is real. The offence lies in the wish to frighten, and so a true report made in order to frighten seems to fit the word-analysis as well as a false one; it is the non-offence clause, below, that protects a warning given in good faith.

    In both cases the result is expressly irrelevant: "whether the other monk is scared or not, he commits an offense entailing confession."18 The same five arrangements stand in the word-analysis of Pj 3, its dreadful arrangements made there in the hope that the man will die of terror, and there the result is graded: wrong conduct for the arranging, a serious offence if he is terrified, expulsion if he dies.19 The two rules treat the same effort in opposite ways. At Pj 3 terror is a step toward a death. Here it is the whole of what was wanted, and a monk who has set out to frighten another has done what the rule forbids, whether or not the other is frightened.

    Intention

    Intention carries the rule, and the one exemption is its absence: there is no offence in arranging any of these things, or telling of any such wilderness, "but not because he wishes to scare anyone."20 A real warning is no offence, and neither is a story told for its own sake; Ajahn Brahm's ghost story is an offence only "if one does so in order to scare the monk".21​ 22

    A mixed intention is harder, and Brahmāli worked one modern case: a monk startled on purpose to cure his hiccups. He thought it turned on the main intention, which had been to get rid of the hiccup, with the wish to scare beneath it, and so "it's probably okay".23 He hedged the answer twice, and the texts do not reach the case: the exemption speaks of the wish being absent, not of its being outranked. What is not in doubt is that a monk who startles another in order to startle him is inside the rule, whatever else he also wanted.

    The monks frightened in the origin story were the boys of the group of seventeen, and the Vibhaṅga records nothing of what was done to them, only that they cried.24 A rule that does not ask whether the fright worked is asking something simpler of a monk: that he never set out to make a fellow monk afraid.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — arranging a frightening sight, sound, smell, taste or physical contact for anyone not fully ordained, or telling him of a dangerous wilderness, in order to frighten him, whether or not he is frightened.25
    • Dukkaṭa — where the other is not fully ordained, under all three perceptions; where he is fully ordained, all three perceptions carry the full pācittiya.26

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he arranges a sight, a sound, a smell, a taste, or a physical contact, or he tells about a wilderness inhabited by criminals, predatory animals, or demons, but not because he wishes to scare anyone; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.27

    Related Offences

    • Pj 3 — killing a human being: its word-analysis arranges the same five objects with the intention that the man die of them.28
    • Pc 77 — provoking anxiety in another monk: Thanissaro calls this rule "in some ways parallel" to it, and argues from this rule's explicit statement that result is irrelevant that the silence of Pc 77's analysis means the opposite there. The Concise BMC gives it as this rule's one related offence.29​ 30
    • Pc 52, 53 — tickling and playing in water: rules against horseplay between monks, which sit beside this one in the chapter.
    • Pc 74 — striking a monk in anger: the rule on contact distinguished by its motive, as this one is by the wish to frighten.

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; the complaint is verbatim and the interrogation, rebuke and ruling are given in the standing abbreviated form. The rule carries no endnote. Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual, the recitation, the class transcript (session 028, 12 June 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were checked, with the word-analysis of Pj 3. One class use is kept, Brahmāli's judgment on the hiccup case, with only his answer quoted; his point on ghost stories is carried by the written sources. Transcript quotations are in the house spelling, with disfluencies removed.
