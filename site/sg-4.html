    Sg 4

    Story (Vatthu)

    At one time the Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove. Venerable Udāyī was associating with and visiting a number of families in Sāvatthī. One morning he robed up, took his bowl and robe, and went to the house of a beautiful widow, where he sat down on the prepared seat. She bowed and sat down, and Udāyī instructed, inspired, and gladdened her with a teaching. She then said, "Venerable, please tell me what you need. I can give robe-cloth, almsfood, a dwelling, and medicinal supplies."

    "It's not hard for us to get those requisites. Give instead what's hard for us to get."

    "What's that, venerable?"

    "Sexual intercourse."

    "Is it wanted now?"

    "Yes."

    Saying, "Come," she entered her bedroom, took off her wrap, and lay back on the bed. Udāyī went up to her and spat out, "Who would touch this stinking wretch!" and he left.

    The woman complained and criticized him: "… How could the ascetic Udāyī ask for sexual intercourse from me, but then spit out, 'Who would touch this stinking wretch!' and leave? What's bad about me? How am I stinking? How am I inferior to anyone?" Other women criticized him in the same way. The monks of few desires complained, "How could Venerable Udāyī encourage a woman to satisfy his own desires?" They told the Buddha, who had the Saṅgha gathered, questioned Udāyī, rebuked him, and laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — Venerable Udāyī, offered the requisites by a widow he has taught, asks her instead for sexual intercourse and then spurns her when she agrees; the Buddha lays down the rule against encouraging a woman to satisfy one's own desires.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk, overcome by lust and with a distorted mind, encourages a woman to satisfy his own desires, saying, 'Sister, she provides the highest service who in this way satisfies one like me, who is virtuous, celibate, and of good character,' and if it is a reference to sexual intercourse, he commits an offense entailing suspension."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu, motivated by lust, recommends to a woman that they have sexual intercourse, he incurs a saṅghādisesa."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu, overcome by lust, with altered mind, speak in the presence of a woman in praise of ministering to his own sensuality thus: 'This, sister, is the foremost ministration, that of ministering to a virtuous, fine-natured follower of the celibate life such as myself with this act'—alluding to sexual intercourse—it entails initial and subsequent meetings of the Community."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "With lustful intentions, recommending to a woman sexual intercourse with oneself is a saṅghādisesa."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu, under the influence of an altered mind, [and] in the presence of a woman, should speak praise about the ministering to himself with sex: 'Sister, this is the best of ministerings: she who would minister to a virtuous, good natured celibate like me with this act!' [which is something] connected with sexual intercourse: [this is a case] involving the community in the beginning and in the rest."6

    The rule carries a formula in the monk's own voice, and the five renderings divide over keeping it. The full translations keep it, as "the highest service", "the foremost ministration" and "the best of ministerings"; the recitation and Ajahn Brahm keep only what it recommends, intercourse with the monk. The formula is the point of the rule, since it offers the monk's virtue and celibacy as the reason a woman should give herself to him, and Ñāṇatusita notes that its key word for ministering often carries a sexual sense.7 But it is an example, not a required form of words: the word-analysis glosses "the highest" with four other superlatives, and none of the case studies uses the formula at all, as discussed under Effort.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule guards a monk's celibacy against his own lust, and closes what could be mistaken for a loophole in Sg 3: a proposal of intercourse presented as the highest gift a woman can give him. It can look redundant beside Sg 3, which already covers lustful speech about intercourse. The Sub-commentary distinguishes them by the kind of lust, the wish to say something lewd for Sg 3 and the wish for intercourse here; Thanissaro rejects this, since the Commentary's system of classifying lust contradicts the Vibhaṅga.8 His own explanation starts from the language of the time. "Giving" was a common euphemism for a woman's consenting to sex; gifts to contemplatives were widely believed to bring great reward; and somewhere the conclusion was drawn that sex, as the highest gift, would bring the highest. Several case studies under Pj 1 tell of women who approached monks with this belief. Sg 3 exempts speech aimed at welfare, attha, which is also the widow's word for what Udāyī "needs", and this rule makes clear that the exemption does not cover such a proposal.9 The belief was not confined to Buddhists: Ñāṇatusita quotes the Jain Ācārāṅgasūtra, in which women say of ascetics who have given up sex that a woman who sleeps with one will bear a strong, victorious son of heavenly beauty.10

    Thanissaro notes that the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī lists five factors, of which four have a basis in the Vibhaṅga: object, perception, intention and effort.11 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual makes the rule Sg 3 limited to intercourse, with the lust and the woman as there.12 Object and perception are taken together below, and the fifth factor at the end.

    Object and Perception

    The woman is defined as at Sg 3, one who "understands and is capable of discerning good and bad speech", and the speech is made in her presence.13 The other beings and the misperceptions are graded as there: a paṇḍaka is a serious offence and a man or an animal wrong conduct, a woman taken for something else is a serious offence, and anything else taken for something else is wrong conduct, with the same disagreement among the editions over the elided cases.14

    Intention

    The Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī takes the lust to be the wish that the woman satisfy his desire for intercourse. Thanissaro observes that the Vibhaṅga defines the lust here in the same broad terms as at Sg 2 and Sg 3, so that the wish to enjoy making such remarks in a woman's presence is enough, such as the pleasure of testing her reaction, which he takes to have been Udāyī's impulse.15 The Story fits this, since Udāyī walked away from the intercourse he had asked for. Ajahn Brahm gives the same range, from the wish for intercourse to the wish merely to talk about sex.16

    Effort

    The monk speaks in praise of her satisfying his desires, with reference to intercourse. The word-analysis defines his own desires as those aimed at satisfying himself, gives "the highest" four other superlatives, and glosses "in this way" as with sexual intercourse.17 The Commentary requires a direct mention of intercourse, and Thanissaro answers it as at Sg 3, since the rule's own superlatives do not name it.18 The case studies show how far from the formula the offence reaches. Seven women asked a monk who associated with their families how to get pregnant, how to have a child, how to be loved by a husband, how to be more fortunate, what to give him, how to support him and how to reach a good rebirth; each time he told her to give "the highest gift" and, asked what it was, said "Sexual intercourse", and each time the Buddha gave the full offence.19 None of them names the monk as the one to receive it, so the reference to himself need not be explicit either.20 Whether a roundabout proposal brings the full offence is Sg 3's question. A gesture or a letter serves as well as speech.21 Ajahn Brahm cites a case from the Samantapāsādikā of a monk who argues from their shared warrior caste; the Samantapāsādikā itself, which uses the case to illustrate its demand for a direct mention, makes the caste talk alone no saṅghādisesa, and the offence arises when he adds that she is fit "to give me sexual intercourse".22

    Disputed — whether the full offence requires, as at Sg 3, that the woman immediately understand.

    The Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī adds this as a fifth factor, and grades a woman's not understanding a serious offence; Ajahn Brahm follows the commentaries: the words must be spoken to a woman old enough to understand them, and she must understand; and if the monk had put the caste proposal roundabout, her understanding at once would make it a serious offence and her not understanding wrong conduct.23 Thanissaro answers that the Canon gives it no basis.24 The Manual lists no understanding factor here, carrying over from Sg 3 only the lust and the woman, though in its Sg 3 her understanding sat under the effort heading this rule replaces, so the silence may not be a ruling.25 The case studies cannot decide it, since in each of them the woman asks what the gift is and is told. At Sg 3 the case studies depend on understanding and here the Vibhaṅga says nothing of it, so I lean to Thanissaro, and would treat the full offence as complete when the proposal is made; but the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī's reading keeps the two neighbouring rules alike, and the Vibhaṅga's silence, which is all my lean rests on, cannot rule it out.

    The non-offence clause names the very things Udāyī turned down. A monk may say, "Support us with robe-cloth, almsfood, dwellings, and medicinal supplies";26 what he may not do is offer his own virtue and celibacy as the reason a woman should give him what the rule forbids. Brahmāli's verdict in class on the belief behind the rule is blunt: from a Buddhist point of view sexual intercourse is not the highest gift, and to present it so is "a complete absolute misrepresentation of Buddhism".27

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Thullaccaya — the proposal made to a paṇḍaka, or to a woman he takes for a paṇḍaka, a man or an animal.28
    • Dukkaṭa — the proposal made to a man or an animal, or to a paṇḍaka, man or animal he takes for something else.29
    • Saṅghādisesa — one for each woman to whom the proposal is made: two women, two offences.30

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he says, "Support us with robe-cloth, almsfood, dwellings, and medicinal supplies"; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.31 The Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī reads the first as allowing a monk who wants the requisites to speak in praise of giving them, and Thanissaro infers that a monk without lust may repeat the remarks this rule forbids, as when explaining it.32 The Concise BMC narrows this, against the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī: in its compiler's own note the exemption holds only when the monk speaks for her benefit and not for material gain, and praising such gifts out of a desire for material gain is, it says, wrong livelihood, an offence of wrong conduct under the Khandhaka's general rule on misbehaviour, a penalty BMC1 does not state for this rule.33

    Related Offences

    • Sg 3 — lustful speech about a woman's private parts or intercourse, of which this rule is the narrower case, and whose exemption for speech aimed at welfare this rule closes to such proposals.
    • Pj 1 — the case studies of women who approached monks believing that sex with a contemplative was meritorious.34

    Related Suttas

    • AN 7.51 — union and disengagement: "Beings who are delighted with their masculinity enter upon union with women"; the desire Udāyī presented as a gift begins there.35
    • AN 7.50 — the seven bonds of sexuality by which an ascetic "claiming to be perfectly celibate" who never has intercourse still breaches the celibate life, as a proposal does though nothing follows it.36
