    Pc 59

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. Venerable Upananda the Sakyan assigned the ownership of a robe to a monk who was his brother's student. He then used that robe without that monk having relinquished it.

    That monk told the monks, "Venerable Upananda is using a robe that he had assigned to me, even though I haven't relinquished it." The monks of few desires complained and criticised Upananda: "How could Venerable Upananda use a robe he had assigned to a monk, without that monk first relinquishing it?" They told the Buddha, who questioned Upananda and rebuked him: "Foolish man, how could you do this? This will affect people's confidence." A monk who assigns a robe to a monk, a nun, a trainee nun, a novice monk or a novice nun, and then uses it without its being relinquished, commits an offence entailing confession.1

    Synopsis — Upananda put a robe into an arrangement the Vinaya provides for storing cloth one may not keep, and then went on wearing it. The rule closes the loophole by making the arrangement mean what it says: once a robe has been assigned away, it cannot be used until the other party gives it back.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk himself assigns the ownership of a robe to a monk, a nun, a trainee nun, a novice monk, or a novice nun, and he then uses it without the other first relinquishing it, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu uses robe-cloth that is being stored through vikappana with a fellow monastic, he incurs a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu, having himself placed robe-cloth under shared ownership (vikappana) with a bhikkhu, a bhikkhunī, a female trainee, a male novice, or a female novice, then make use of the cloth without the shared ownership's being rescinded, it is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Using cloth which is stored under vikappa is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu, having himself assigned a robe to a bhikkhu or a bhikkhunī or a female trainee or a male novice or a female novice, should use [it] without withdrawing [the assignment], [this is a case] involving expiation."6

    The renderings agree on what is forbidden and differ on the name of the arrangement. Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation and Ñāṇatusita say the monk "assigns" the robe; Thanissaro says he places it "under shared ownership"; the recitation leaves vikappanā untranslated, and Ajahn Brahm calls it "vikappa". Brahmāli's Appendix I rejects "shared ownership" by name, since the Vibhaṅga's formula speaks of giving the cloth to others as their property, which is the language of a change of ownership, and he proposes "assignment".7 I follow him and say "assign". The difference does not change what the rule requires, for on every rendering the cloth so placed may be stored and may not be used.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A monk who stores cloth by assigning it away is trusted to mean what he said, and the rule keeps him to his word, for his own straightness with his fellows and for the confidence the Buddha named in rebuking Upananda, who assigned a robe to a junior monk and went on wearing it: "This will affect people's confidence."8

    Thanissaro gives it two factors: object, robe-cloth of the six kinds and at least four fingerbreadths by eight, that one has oneself assigned; and effort, using it before the assignment has been withdrawn.9

    Object

    Assignment exists to let a monk store cloth he may not keep. It was allowed in the same Khandhaka passage as the ten-day limit on an extra robe, as its relief, so that assigned cloth is not counted under NP 1.10​ 11 A later passage sorts the requisites: the three robes, the sitting mat and the rest are to be determined and not assigned, while the rains-bathing cloth outside the rains and the itch-covering cloth when one is well are to be assigned.12 From these Thanissaro draws the principle that assignment is for cloth being stored, not cloth in use.13 The smallest cloth that can be assigned is eight standard fingerbreadths by four, and anything smaller falls outside the rule altogether.14 The cloth may be assigned to any of the five co-religionists the rule names, and Thanissaro reports the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī extending the rule to a bowl, which he thinks the Great Standards could be cited to support, given NP 21.15​ 16

    Effort

    While the assignment stands, the cloth may be stored and nothing else. Using it is the pācittiya; determining it or giving it away is an offence of wrong conduct; and, in Ajahn Brahm's words, "any monk is prohibited from making use of it".17​ 18

    The Vibhaṅga gives two ways of making the assignment. In one, made "in the presence of", one assigns the cloth to "you" or to a named person. Thanissaro takes the presence to be the recipient's, while calling the point controversial, and reads the two forms as a courtesy, "you" for a senior monk and the name for a junior.19​ 20 The cloth need not be in hand: his Appendix V gives the words for an article beyond reach.21 In the other, made "in the absence of", one gives the cloth to a middleman for the purpose of assigning it; the middleman asks who one's friends are, and assigns it to the two one names. In Thanissaro's account the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī reworks these into three methods, and he judges the Vibhaṅga's two preferable: its second and third methods differ in nothing practical, and its absent method names one friend where the Vibhaṅga names two.22 The two methods of the Vibhaṅga are followed here, and BMC1's Appendix V sets out their Pāli formulae for use.23

    Perception

    Where the assignment has not been withdrawn, the pācittiya falls under all three perceptions; where it has, a monk who thinks it has not, or is unsure, commits an offence of wrong conduct. Thanissaro refers the pattern to Pc 4.24​ 25

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The cloth becomes usable again in two ways: the other gives it back, or the monk takes it on trust.26 For the present method the Vibhaṅga gives no form of words for giving it back, and Thanissaro supplies the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī's, in which the other tells him to use what is his, give it away, or do as he likes with it.27 Taking on trust has five canonical conditions: the owner is a friend, a close companion, has spoken about it, is alive, and will be pleased that one takes it.28​ 29 Either way the cloth is the monk's own again, and that has a consequence. In Thanissaro's report, the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī returns cloth taken on trust to the status of extra cloth, and Ajahn Brahm adds that in either case it must then be determined, assigned again or given away within ten days, under NP 1.30​ 31 An assignment also lapses, in his account, if the cloth is destroyed or stolen, or if one of the owners dies or disrobes.32

    Disputed — what the middleman's closing words mean in the absent method: "I give it to them. Please use their property, give it away, or do as you like with it."

    On one reading the second sentence is the withdrawal: Brahmāli reports that the Commentary seems to take it as the new owners relinquishing the cloth so that the old owner may use it, Thanissaro gives the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī saying the same, and Thanissaro's own Appendix V gives the sentence as the formula of withdrawal.33​ 34​ 35 Brahmāli objects that both sentences are spoken by the middleman, who cannot withdraw what he has just given, and that the Pāli shows no period of storage between them, and offers as one possibility that the second announces that the monk may now take the cloth on trust from the two friends he named; if so, that is why the middleman asked about friends at all.36 Thanissaro's chapter also rests the monk's use on trust, but holds that the middleman has no standing to grant it, since trust runs from whoever owns the thing, though he allows that the sentence may be there "to show that all sides involved … are agreeable to one's making use of the cloth"; and so, if the two new owners have not already given the monk leave to use their things, he may not use the cloth until they expressly allow it.37​ 38 Ajahn Brahm reads the words as part of the giving, "they can do as they wish with it", and has the monk ask both new owners to relinquish their share, or take the cloth on trust, before he uses it.39 Each reading fits a different part of the text, and the texts do not settle which is right. Thanissaro's and Ajahn Brahm's are the strictest, and my counsel is to follow them, since a monk who does will not go wrong. Brahmāli's own counsel was to leave the absent method alone, as "rather strange".40

    The present method needs none of this. Brahmāli once described his own practice: he asked a fellow monk whether he might assign his outer robe to him, the monk agreed, "and that's it", and from that moment the robe was one he could "only store".41 The step he counselled next keeps the arrangement easy: to ask the other monk whether he minds one's taking the cloth on trust when it is needed.42 Then, on the day it is needed, the monk takes it, it is his own again, and he determines it before the ten days run out.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — determining the assigned cloth, or giving it away, while the assignment stands.43
    • Dukkaṭa — using cloth whose assignment has in fact been withdrawn, while perceiving that it has not, or being unsure.44

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if the other person gives it, or he uses it after taking the other person's property on trust; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.45

    Related Offences

    • NP 1 — keeping extra robe-cloth beyond ten days: the rule assignment exists to relieve, and the rule a monk returns to when an assignment is undone.46​ 47
    • NP 21 — keeping an extra bowl beyond ten days: the warrant for assigning bowls as well as cloth.48
    • Pc 33 — eating a meal before another: a rule outside the cloth rules where the same verb is used, of an expected meal assigned to another before eating elsewhere.49
    • Pj 2 — taking what is not given: where the conditions for taking on trust are discussed in full.50

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; the aggrieved monk's report and the complaint are verbatim and the rebuke is given in the standing form. Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual, the recitation, his endnotes and Appendix I, the class transcript (session 029, 19 June 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1 with its Appendix V, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were checked, with the Khandhaka passages on assignment, determination, the smallest cloth, trust and couriers. Ajahn Brahm cites the Vinaya-mukha, which this book does not use; his positions are carried as his own. The class is cited for Brahmāli's counsel on the absent method, his own practice and the request for trust; its points on storage and on the cloth reverting to extra cloth are carried by Ajahn Brahm and Thanissaro. The commentaries are known here only as Thanissaro and Brahmāli report them.
