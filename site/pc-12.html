    Pc 12

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Kosambī, in Ghosita's Monastery. At that time Venerable Channa was misbehaving. When he was examined about an offence in the midst of the Saṅgha, he spoke evasively: "Who has committed an offence? What offence was committed? In regard to what was it committed? How was it committed? Who are you talking about? What are you talking about?"

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised him: "How can Venerable Channa speak evasively when examined about an offence in the midst of the Saṅgha?" They told the Buddha, who asked Channa whether it was true, and he admitted it was. The Buddha rebuked him, and directed the Saṅgha to charge him formally with evasive speech: a competent monk was to inform the Saṅgha of the charge in a formal motion, carried in silence.1 He then laid down a preliminary rule: "If a monk speaks evasively, he commits an offence entailing confession."

    Later, when Channa was again being examined about an offence in the midst of the Saṅgha, he thought, "By speaking evasively I'll commit an offence," and instead harassed the Saṅgha by remaining silent. Again the monks of few desires complained, again the Buddha rebuked him, and this time he directed the Saṅgha to charge Channa formally with harassment, by the same kind of motion. He revised the rule to its final form.

    Synopsis — Venerable Channa, examined about an offence in the Saṅgha, first dodges the questioning with a barrage of counter-questions and then, once that is forbidden, stonewalls in silence; after the Saṅgha formally charges him each time, the Buddha lays down a rule against evasive speech and against obstructive silence under questioning.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk speaks evasively or harasses, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "Evasive speech or remaining silent, when questioned in the Saṅgha about an offense, is a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Evasive speech and causing frustration are to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Persistently replying evasively or keeping silent (being uncooperative) when being questioned in a meeting of the Sangha is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "In evading, in vexing, [there is a case] involving expiation."6

    The renderings agree on the conduct and differ only on how hard to make the second word. The Vibhaṅga defines vihesaka as harassing the Saṅgha by remaining silent, and Brahmāli's "harasses", Thanissaro's "causing frustration" and Ñāṇatusita's "vexing" keep the silence aimed at the Saṅgha, where the recitation's "remaining silent" and Ajahn Brahm's "keeping silent (being uncooperative)" name only what the monk does. Nothing turns on the difference, since the definition supplies the purpose: he is silent "because he does not want to talk about it or reveal it".7 The recitation adds the setting the rule line leaves to the definitions, "when questioned in the Saṅgha about an offense". Neither carries the formal charge on which the full offence depends, which is taken up under Object.8

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The Saṅgha can set a fault right only in a monk who will speak of it, and this rule keeps him answerable when it asks, for his own sake and for the Saṅgha's. Channa's two tactics, a barrage of counter-questions and then, once that was forbidden, silence, each left the Saṅgha nothing to work with, since it can deal with an offence only if the monk accused of it will say what he has done. The Buddha met each tactic first with a formal charge, a warning given by the whole assembly, and only then with the rule.

    This is a procedural rule. It governs how a monk answers when the Saṅgha formally questions him about an offence, and its whole weight sits on that warning: the full offence falls only on a monk who goes on as before once the Saṅgha has charged him.

    Thanissaro gives the offence three factors: intention, the motive to hide one's offences; effort, continuing in evasive speech or in silence; and object, being questioned in the Community after it has formally charged one with either.9 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual sets the same elements under its own three heads, and its last line fixes the moment: the offence falls only if the monk continues "after the completion of the saṅghakamma".10

    Object

    The setting is a formal inquiry: a monk is being questioned in the midst of the Saṅgha about an action that was the basis for an offence, or about the class of an offence.11 The first evasion is not the full offence. A monk who answers evasively or falls silent to keep his offence hidden incurs, at that stage, wrong conduct. The Saṅgha may then, if it sees fit, restrain him with a formal charge, for evasive speech or for harassment, enacted by a motion put to the assembly and carried in its silence, as the Story shows for Channa. Only if he continues after that charge does he incur the pācittiya.12​ 13​ 14 The rule has a warning built into it, and the Saṅgha gives the warning before the offence becomes a confessable one.

    Perception plays no part once the charge is rightly brought: if he continues, the offence is full whether he takes the charge to be legitimate, illegitimate, or is unsure. Where the procedure was not legitimate, the Vibhaṅga gives wrong conduct in every case.15 Thanissaro reads this as allotting the offences as though the charge had never been brought. A monk who deserved the charge but was charged by a defective procedure, and goes on hiding his offence, incurs a further wrong conduct; a monk who did not deserve it, who was silent or asked questions for an allowable reason and was charged by a Saṅgha abusing its powers, incurs nothing for continuing as before.16

    Effort

    The Vibhaṅga's examples of evasive speech are all questions, Channa's own volley of them. Thanissaro reports the Commentary taking them as illustrative, so that any speaking beside the point under formal questioning counts, and the Sub-commentary supplies a case of its own: asked "Have you committed this offense?", the monk answers "I've been to Pāṭaliputta," then "From there I went to Rājagaha," then "I got some pork there."17 Harassment is the opposite tactic to the same end, remaining silent so as to obstruct the inquiry.18

    Intention

    The factor is fulfilled only if the motive is to conceal one's own offences. That is what separates the offence from the many innocent reasons a monk might have for answering off the point or saying nothing, and where the motive is absent, neither evasion nor silence is an offence, whatever the Saṅgha has charged.19

    The pācittiya is not the end of the Saṅgha's recourse. A monk who goes on being uncooperative may be censured as a maker of strife and trouble by a tajjanīya-kamma, and if he finally admits the offence he was hiding, he is liable to a tassa-pāpiyasikā-kamma, a further punishment for not admitting a true charge from the start. That machinery belongs to the Khandhakas and the Adhikaraṇa-samatha rules.20​ 21

    What the rule asks of a monk under questioning is plain: answer what is asked. Every one of its exemptions is for a monk who cannot or should not answer, because he does not understand, or is ill, or fears a schism, or will not lend himself to an illegitimate procedure. None is for the monk who would simply rather not say.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • A dukkaṭa for speaking evasively or remaining silent to conceal one's offences when questioned in the Saṅgha, before any formal charge has been brought.22​ 23
    • A dukkaṭa for continuing in evasion or silence where the procedure that charged him was illegitimate, whatever he perceives it to be.24 Thanissaro confines this to a monk who deserved the charge and goes on hiding his offence.25
    • By Ajahn Brahm's extension, which he offers as seeming appropriate, a dukkaṭa for answering evasively or keeping silent when questioned by any monk at any time; the Vibhaṅga itself concerns only questioning in the Saṅgha.26

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence, even after a charge has been brought: if he asks because he does not know; if he does not speak because he is sick; if he does not speak because he thinks there will be quarrels or disputes, or a fracture or schism, in the Saṅgha; if he does not speak because he thinks the legal procedure will be illegitimate, done by an incomplete assembly, or done to one who does not deserve it; if he is insane; if he is the first offender.27​ 28

    Related Offences

    • Pc 13 — the following rule, built in the same grammatical form; where this rule meets a monk obstructing the Saṅgha's inquiry into his own offence, Pc 13 meets a monk criticising, without grounds, an official the Saṅgha has appointed.
    • Sg 12 — Channa again, and the same fault at its graver end: there his refusal to be admonished by the Saṅgha, pursued through the formal admonitions, is a saṅghādisesa; here evasion under questioning is a pācittiya.
    • Pc 71 — refusing to train when admonished about a rule; both rules protect the Saṅgha's capacity to correct its own members.

    Related Suttas

    • AN 8.14 — the monk "like a wild colt": reproved for an offence, "he answers evasively, diverts the discussion to an irrelevant subject", or he says neither that he did nor that he did not commit it, "but he vexes the Sangha by keeping silent", the rule's two tactics side by side.29
    • MN 65 — the Buddha tells Bhaddāli why the monks do not settle quickly the case of a monk who, corrected, "prevaricates, leads the talk aside", and do settle quickly the case of one who says, "Let me so act that the Sangha will be satisfied."30

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, the two formal charges given in summary. Brahmāli's recitation and the Manual, Thanissaro's BMC1, Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were checked; Ñāṇatusita gives the rule translation and the glosses. Ajahn Brahm's added exemption for silence "through acute embarrassment" has no counterpart in the Vibhaṅga and is not carried; the Concise BMC follows Thanissaro without adding. The Commentary and Sub-commentary are known here only through Thanissaro. The class transcript for this chapter (session 022, 3 May 2012) lapses into silence after the Pc 11 material, and no other session of the series treats this rule.
