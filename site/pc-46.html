    Pc 46

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Rājagaha in the Bamboo Grove. A family that was supporting Venerable Upananda the Sakyan had invited him to a meal, and they had invited other monks too. But since Upananda was visiting other families before that meal, the other monks said to that family, "Please give the meal."

    "Please wait, sirs, until Venerable Upananda arrives."

    A second time. And a third time those monks said, "Please give the meal before it is too late."

    "But we prepared the meal because of Venerable Upananda. Please wait until he arrives."

    Then, after visiting those families, Upananda arrived late, and those monks did not eat as much as they had intended. The monks of few desires complained and criticised him, the Buddha questioned him, established that it was true, rebuked him, and laid down the rule against visiting families beforehand.

    Soon afterwards a family that was supporting Upananda sent fresh food to the Saṅgha, instructing that it should be shown to Upananda and then given to the Saṅgha. Upananda had gone to the village for alms, and the Buddha said, "Well then, monks, receive it and put it aside until Upananda returns." When he heard that visiting families before the meal had been prohibited, Upananda visited them after the meal instead; he returned late, and the food had to be returned to the donors. The Buddha extended the rule to afterwards.

    Soon afterwards it was the robe-giving season, and the monks, being afraid of wrongdoing, did not visit families; they got only a small amount of robe-cloth. The Buddha allowed visiting families during the robe-giving season. Soon afterwards the monks were making robes and needed a needle, thread and scissors, and again did not visit families; the Buddha allowed visiting them at a time of making robes. Soon afterwards there were sick monks who needed medicines, and again the monks did not visit families; the Buddha allowed visiting them after informing an available monk, and stated the rule.1​ 2

    Synopsis — Upananda keeps monks and a family waiting through a morning because he is calling on other supporters, and the monks go short of their meal; told not to call beforehand, he calls afterwards instead, and a gift of food goes back to its donors. Three amendments follow in the other direction, each because monks kept the rule so carefully that something needful went undone — cloth, sewing materials, and then medicine — and the last of them supplies the escape the rule now carries: inform an available monk.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk who has been invited to a meal visits families beforehand or afterwards without informing an available monk, except on an appropriate occasion, he commits an offense entailing confession. These are the appropriate occasions: it is the robe-giving season; it is a time of making robes."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu who has been invited to a meal visits other households beforehand or afterwards, without having informed an available bhikkhu, except on given occasions, he incurs a pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu, being invited for a meal and without taking leave of an available bhikkhu, go calling on families before or after the meal, except at the proper occasions, it is to be confessed. Here the proper occasions are these: a time of giving cloth, a time of making robes. These are the proper occasions here."5

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Visiting other lay families on the way to an invitation or on the way back, not having asked permission from another monk, except at the time of giving and making robes, is an offence of Pācittiya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu who has been invited for a meal, not having asked [permission from] a bhikkhu who is present [in the monastery], should go visiting families before the meal or after the meal, except at the [right] occasion, [this is a case] involving expiation. Here the occasion is this: the occasion of a giving of robe [-cloth]s; the occasion of a making of robes; this is the occasion here."7

    The renderings agree on everything but two words. Ñāṇatusita's "a bhikkhu who is present [in the monastery]" for santaṁ takes the wider of the two readings of an available monk, discussed under Effort.8 The other word decides what a monk must do before he leaves. The Pāli is anāpucchā, the negated absolutive of āpucchati.9 Brahmāli's two versions say "informing", Thanissaro's says "taking leave of", which for him is explicitly not asking, and Ajahn Brahm's and Ñāṇatusita's say asking permission, Ñāṇatusita supplying "[permission from]" in a bracket of his own.10​ 11​ 12​ 13 The recitation line also has "other households" for "families" and compresses the two occasions to "given occasions".14 Whether the monk informs or asks is a real disagreement, argued under Effort.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    An invitation to a meal is not leave to spend the morning among lay households, and this rule guards a monk's time and conduct on such a day by asking him first to inform a fellow monk. Thanissaro reads that purpose from the rule's own limits: since it reaches only lay houses and lets a monk go freely to monasteries and nunneries, its concern is that an invitation not become "an excuse to visit lay people and spend their time in inappropriate activities".15 The five sub-stories give the rule its shape. In the first two someone is kept waiting: a family and monks for their meal, and a gift of food that has to go back to its donors. The other three loosen the rule, because monks who kept it too carefully went without cloth, without a needle and without medicine, and the last of them gave the rule its escape: inform an available monk.16

    Thanissaro gives two factors, object and effort, and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual defines the two terms that set its reach, a meal and an available monk.17​ 18 Thanissaro's two factors give the order, and the available monk belongs to Effort.

    Object

    The object is a family's house. The Vibhaṅga defines a family by the four classes, aristocratic, brahmin, merchant and worker, which is a way of saying any lay family, and Thanissaro makes the object "a family residence", its yard a matter of wrong conduct.19​ 20 The rule reaches houses and not errands. The non-offence clauses let a monk go freely to another monastery, a nunnery or the dwelling of monastics of another religion, and what is left prohibited is lay houses.21

    Effort

    The effort is entering such a house before or after a meal to which one has been invited, without informing an available monk. The rule binds only on a day with an invitation: where there is none, the permutations make it no pācittiya, though the non-offence clauses do not say so.22 "Afterwards" begins once he has eaten anything of the meal, even "what fits on the tip of a blade of grass". The offence is graded by the threshold. Entering the vicinity of the house is an offence of wrong conduct, and so is crossing the threshold with the first foot; the pācittiya falls with the second.23

    Disputed — whether the monk must inform another monk or ask his permission.

    Thanissaro, on the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī's authority, makes it a statement of where one is going, "I am going to the house of so-and-so", not a request for permission, though the other monk is free to say the going is improper, and disrespect for his objection falls under Pc 54; the Concise BMC agrees.24​ 25 Ajahn Brahm argues from usage that āpucchati means asking permission across the Vinaya. The rule's purpose, he draws from it, is to restrain monks from going among lay people without good reason; if informing were the point, a novice or a lay resident would do, as at Pc 14 and Pc 15, where here only a monk will. A monk who is asked, he adds, should give permission unless the going is obviously inappropriate.26 The usage cuts both ways. At Bhikkhunī Pc 94, a rule named after this very word, Brahmāli renders it "without asking permission"; at Kd 6:34.17, one of the passages Ajahn Brahm cites, he renders it "He had not informed Meṇḍaka", as the context requires.27​ 28 On Thanissaro's report the content is an announcement made to someone with the standing to object, and Ajahn Brahm's own rider, that permission should be given unless the going is plainly improper, comes to much the same; the two sides are closer than the renderings suggest. That reading is mine, and it does not answer Ajahn Brahm's point about who may receive the statement. A monk who asks a fellow monk's leave, saying where he is going, has done what either reading asks.

    Who counts as an available monk is a narrower question, and the Vibhaṅga answers it functionally: one to whom it is possible to speak before entering.29 Thanissaro reports the Commentary reading this as a matter of proximity: a monk met on the way out of the monastery, close enough to address in an ordinary voice, is available, and there is no need to go out of one's way to find one. Thanissaro calls this "in direct contradiction" to the Vibhaṅga, which he reads as obliging a monk to go out of his way to inform another if one is in the monastery and free to be told.30 Ñāṇatusita's gloss takes the same side: any bhikkhu "who is living in the monastery".31 I take the wider duty to be the safer course. Brahmāli's account of his own monastery shows how lightly it sits in practice: a monk going to the city tells another at breakfast or the meal, "because everybody is around", but does not go to someone's kuṭi to do it, since that is "a private area".32

    The two occasions are the robe-giving season and the time of making robes, defined as at Pc 32, and the rule is also one of those the kaṭhina privileges relax.33​ 34 The exemptions existed, Thanissaro explains, so that a monk could visit his supporters for the thread, cloth or scissors he needed at such times.35 Above all of this sits a separate duty: a monk still living in dependence must ask his mentor's permission before entering a village at any time, invited or not.36

    Ajahn Brahm finds the rule's line between an invitation and almsround hard to defend. He argues that the stories imply visiting before or after almsround is caught too, and that there is no relevant reason to treat an invitation and almsround differently.37 Both he and Thanissaro note that Upananda had been on almsround that morning. Thanissaro draws from it only that the Buddha did not approve of such visiting, and gives the practical reason for stopping short of a rule: when alms-going could take all morning, there was no convenient way to draw a hard and fast line between it and inappropriate visiting. What remains is a gap in the code, and Thanissaro names it himself: on a morning with no invitation, before noon, neither this rule nor Pc 85 applies. His answer is counsel, not a rule: inform a fellow monk whenever possible when leaving the monastery for errands or visits involving lay people, even when no rule requires it.38

    Brahmāli suggested the duty's inner side. Having to tell someone, he presumed, brings up "a little bit of shame", so that a monk with no good reason finds it embarrassing to say he is going to the village: "'Really, what are you doing there?' 'I'm just hanging out.' It doesn't sound so good."39 What the requirement asks of him is a moment of having to say it aloud.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — entering the vicinity of the house, and crossing the threshold with the first foot; the pācittiya falls on the second.40
    • Dukkaṭa — where he has not been invited but perceives that he has, or is unsure of it. Where he has been invited, all three perceptions carry the full pācittiya.41

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if it is an appropriate occasion; if he enters after informing an available monk; if, when there is no available monk, he enters without informing anyone; if the road passes someone else's house; if the road passes the vicinity of someone else's house; if he is going between monasteries; if he is going to a nunnery; if he is going to the dwelling place of the monastics of another religion; if he is returning to the monastery; if he is going to the house where he has been invited; if there is an emergency; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.42

    Thanissaro reports the Commentary taking the emergency as danger to one's life or to one's resolve in the celibate life.43

    Related Offences

    • Pc 85 — entering a village at the wrong time without taking leave: the complementary rule, covering noon to dawn where this one covers the invited morning.
    • Pc 32 — group meals, where the two occasions are defined, and which the kaṭhina privileges also relax.
    • Pc 14, 15 — where āpucchati does its work elsewhere, and where a novice or a lay resident may be told.
    • Pc 54 — disrespect: if the monk informed objects to the going and his objection is treated with disrespect, the offence falls there.
    • NP 1, 2, 3 — the other rules the kaṭhina privileges relax.
    • Pc 42, 43, 44, 45 — the four rules before this one, each laid down on account of Upananda.
