    NP 6

    Story (Vatthu)

    First sub-story. While the Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove, Venerable Upananda the Sakyan — a skilled teacher — was approached one day by the son of a wealthy merchant, who bowed and sat down. Upananda instructed, inspired, and gladdened him with a teaching. Afterwards the merchant's son said, "Venerable, please tell me what you need. I can give you robe-cloth, almsfood, a dwelling, and medicinal supplies."

    "If you wish to give me something, give me one of your wrap garments."

    "It's shameful, venerable, for a gentleman to walk around in only one wrap. Please wait until I get back home. I'll send you this wrap or a better one."

    A second time and a third time Upananda said the same thing, and got the same reply. Then he said, "What's the point of inviting me if you don't want to give?"

    Being pressured by Upananda, the merchant's son gave him one of his wraps and left. People asked why he was walking around in only one wrap, and he told them what had happened. They complained and criticised: "These Sakyan monastics have great desires. They're not content. Even to make them an appropriate offer isn't easy. How could they take his wrap when the merchant's son was making an appropriate offer?" The monks heard, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised Upananda, "How could Venerable Upananda ask the merchant's son for a robe?" They told the Buddha, who had the Saṅgha gathered, established that the man was unrelated to Upananda, rebuked him — "people who are unrelated don't know what's appropriate and inappropriate, what's good and bad, in dealing with each other" — and laid down the preliminary rule: a monk who asks an unrelated male or female householder for a robe commits an offence entailing relinquishment and confession.1

    Second sub-story. Soon afterwards a number of monks travelling from Sāketa to Sāvatthī were robbed by bandits. Knowing the rule and afraid of wrongdoing, they did not ask anyone for robes, and so walked naked to Sāvatthī, where they bowed down to the resident monks. The resident monks said, "These Ājīvaka ascetics are good people, as they bow down to the monks."

    "We're not Ājīvakas! We're monks!"

    They asked Venerable Upāli to examine them, and when the naked monks told him what had happened, Upāli said, "They are monks. Please give them robes." The monks of few desires complained, "How can monks go naked? Shouldn't they have covered up with grass and leaves?" They told the Buddha, who gave a teaching and addressed the monks: "If a monk's robes are stolen or lost, I allow him to ask an unrelated householder for robes. At the first monastery where he arrives, if the Sangha has a communal robe, a bedspread, a floor cover, or a mattress cover, he should take that and put it on, thinking, 'When I get a robe, I'll return it.' If there's none of these things, he should cover up with grass and leaves before going on. He should not go on while naked. If he does, he commits an offence of wrong conduct." And he amended the rule to its final form.2

    Synopsis — Upananda pressures a merchant's son into handing over a wrap he is wearing; the Buddha forbids a monk to ask an unrelated householder for a robe, then, after robbed monks walk naked rather than ask, amends the rule to permit asking when one's robes are stolen or lost.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk asks an unrelated male or female householder for a robe, except on an appropriate occasion, he commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession. These are the appropriate occasions: his robes are stolen or his robes are lost."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu asks an unrelated householder for robe-cloth and then receives it, except if his robe has been stolen, lost, or destroyed, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu ask for robe-cloth from a man or woman householder unrelated to him, except at the proper occasion, it is to be forfeited and confessed. Here the proper occasion is this: The bhikkhu's robe has been snatched away or destroyed. This is the proper occasion here."5

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Accepting a cloth having asked for it from a layperson who is not a relation, except when one's robe has been stolen or destroyed, is a nissaggiya pācittiya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should request a robe [-cloth] from an unrelated male householder or female householder, except at the [right] occasion, [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture. Here the occasion is this: he is a bhikkhu whose robe has been robbed or whose robe has been lost; this is the occasion here."7

    The renderings agree. Two of them build into the rule what the Vibhaṅga puts in its word-analysis: the recitation's "and then receives it" and Ajahn Brahm's "Accepting a cloth having asked for it" both state that the full offence falls on receiving, the asking alone being wrong conduct. The recitation's "stolen, lost, or destroyed" names three where the Pāli has two, since the Vibhaṅga's "lost" already takes in burning, flood, pests and wear, as its definition under Effort sets out.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A monk does not ask lay people for cloth: the rule holds him to contentment, and keeps the confidence of those who give. Upananda, having taught a merchant's son well, was offered robe-cloth, almsfood, a dwelling and medicine, and asked instead for the wrap the young man was wearing, and pressed him for it three times until he gave it and walked home in one; people's complaint names what the rule guards: "These Sakyan monastics have great desires. They're not content. Even to make them an appropriate offer isn't easy."8 The second stage shows how far the rule bends. Monks robbed on the road went naked to Sāvatthī rather than ask, and the Buddha allowed a robbed monk to ask, and told him to cover himself meanwhile with what the Saṅgha had, or with grass and leaves.9 Asking is for need, and a robe gone is need.

    NP 6 and NP 7 are read together: this rule allows the asking when robes are stolen or lost, and NP 7 limits how much may then be taken. The definition of unrelated is NP 4's.10 Thanissaro gives three factors, object, effort and result.11

    Object

    Robe-cloth of one of the six kinds, not smaller than what can be assigned to another, as at NP 1.12​ 13

    Effort

    Asking an unrelated householder for it, for oneself, outside the appropriate occasion. A householder is anyone who lives at home, man or woman; Brahmāli's note is that a monastic should not own a home and live in it, wherever else he may stay.14 Asking for the benefit of someone else is no offence, and the Commentary reads this both ways: a monk may ask for a fellow monastic from his own relatives and inviters, or from the other's.15​ 16 Relatives are outside the rule, and so are those who have invited a monk to ask; Brahmāli gives the reason for the first, that a relative "will normally not be afraid of telling you when you go too far", where an unrelated donor finds it hard to say no.17​ 18 Perception does not excuse: if the householder is unrelated, the offence is full whether he takes them for unrelated, is unsure, or thinks them related.19

    The appropriate occasion. His robe is stolen, taken by kings, bandits, scoundrels or anyone, which Thanissaro extends to confiscation by an official; or it is lost, burned, carried away by flood, eaten by rats or termites, or worn through use; worn, Thanissaro adds from the Sub-commentary, means no longer able to cover the body properly.20​ 21 Robes taken on trust by another monk or novice count, by the Commentary, as stolen.22 The robe whose loss opens the occasion is one of the three, Brahmāli argues from NP 7, which lets a monk ask for two robes when all three are gone, and, he notes, the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī seems to support him; the wider reading Thanissaro reports from the Sub-commentary is NP 7's.23

    Left with nothing to wear. The Vibhaṅga does not let a robbed monk go on naked: he takes a communal robe, a bedspread, a floor cover or a mattress cover from the first monastery he reaches, "thinking, 'When I get a robe, I'll return it'", or failing these covers himself with grass and leaves, and to go on naked is wrong conduct; the Khandhaka makes nakedness taken up as a practice, "like the monastics of other religions", a serious offence.24​ 25 Thanissaro gives the Commentary's allowances: it frees him and those who help him from Pc 11's penalty for cutting the grass, lets him leave the borrowed cloth at any convenient monastery if he must go far, and lets him wear cloth of an unallowable kind or colour that he is given until he can get proper cloth.26

    Result

    He gets the cloth. The asking outside the occasion is wrong conduct, and when he gets the cloth it is to be relinquished, by NP 1's procedure.27

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    By means of his own property, in the exemptions, means, as Thanissaro adds from the Sub-commentary, without falling under NP 20.28

    Brahmāli draws the principle these rules share. The Vinaya limits closely what a monk may ask lay people for, and where it says nothing, of laptops for instance, the rule still holds: "you shouldn't basically ask lay people really for anything unless you are desperate".29

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — asking outside the appropriate occasion, before the cloth is got.30
    • Dukkaṭa — if the householder is related and he takes them for unrelated, or is unsure.31
    • Dukkaṭa — going on naked when his robes are gone, rather than covering himself.32

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if it is an appropriate occasion; if he asks relatives; if he asks those who have given an invitation; if he asks for the benefit of someone else; if it is by means of his own property; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.33

    Related Offences

    • NP 7 — how much a robbed monk may accept when he asks.
    • NP 20 — trade, which bounds getting cloth by one's own property.
    • Pc 11 — damaging plants, whose penalty gives way to the robbed monk's grass and leaves.
    • Pc 47 — the invitation to ask for requisites.
    • NP 22, Pc 39, Sk 37 — asking lay people for a bowl, for fine foods, and for curry or rice.

    Related Suttas

    • MN 3 — "Bhikkhus, be my heirs in Dhamma, not my heirs in material things"; it names the heir Upananda made himself when, offered all four requisites, he asked for the wrap off the merchant's son's back.34
