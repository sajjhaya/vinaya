    Parājika

    The four pārājikas are the gravest offences in the code, and they differ from every other class in one respect: every other offence can be recovered from, and a pārājika cannot. A bhikkhu who commits one ceases then and there to be a bhikkhu. No act of the Community expels him; the deed itself does, and he may not ordain again in this life.1 The word is rendered "defeat", and the sense fits: the training is lost, as a game once lost stays lost however long one plays on. One door is left open. According to the Samantapāsādikā he may go forth again as a novice (sāmaṇera), though traditionally this has been allowed only where the Saṅgha judged it suitable in the circumstances.2

    Ignorance is no shelter here. A bhikkhu who breaks one of these rules without knowing it was a rule falls all the same, and for that reason, Thanissaro notes, the Buddha had the four taught to each new bhikkhu as soon as possible after his ordination.3

    There are four pārājika rules for bhikkhus and eight for bhikkhunīs. The Commentary, closing its discussion of the class, counts twenty-four in all, and Thanissaro sets them out as eight actual pārājikas, four derived, and twelve "equivalent pārājikas".4 Eleven of the twelve are the abhabbapuggala, the "persons incapable" of valid ordination: people who should never be ordained, whose ordination is invalid if it goes ahead, and who must be expelled for life once found out. They are a person who

    1. is a paṇḍaka;
    2. is a non-human (e.g., nāgas, petas, devas, and yakkhas);
    3. is a hermaphrodite;
    4. poses as a bhikkhu without being a bhikkhu;
    5. ordains in another religion without first giving up his status as a bhikkhu;
    6. has murdered his father;
    7. has murdered his mother;
    8. has murdered an arahant;
    9. has sexually molested a bhikkhunī;
    10. has maliciously caused a Buddha to bleed; or
    11. has caused a schism in the Saṅgha, knowing or suspecting that his position was contrary to the Dhamma-Vinaya.

    The twelfth is a different kind of case, the loss of a status already held and not a bar to ordaining at all: a bhikkhunī who leaves the Saṅgha and takes up the life of a laywoman (Cv.X.26.1).

    Ajahn Brahm reports the same count from the Samantapāsādikā, "virtually 24 pārājikas", and divides it differently. His twenty-four are the bhikkhus' four, the bhikkhunīs' four further rules, the same eleven disqualified types, "tantamount to pārājikas", four anuloma ("in line with") cases that fall under the first pārājika, and, last, a bhikkhunī who stays in the robes but lives more as a housewife than a bhikkhunī, and is expelled for it.5 His twenty-fourth is a different case from Thanissaro's twelfth, where the bhikkhunī has left the Saṅgha. In either form the list is the Commentary's synthesis, not one the Canon gives.

    The shameless are not the only ones who fall. Ajahn Brahm warns that an ordinary bhikkhu whose awareness is muddled, who knows little of the Vinaya, and who still keeps some attitudes that are "acceptable" in lay life can commit a pārājika carelessly. His examples would not seem grossly immoral by lay standards. A bhikkhu takes an article through customs knowing that duty is owed, and does not declare it because declaring it would be bothersome. A bhikkhu in charge of monastery business deliberately leaves a tax or fee unreported, reasoning that laypeople commonly cut the same corner. A bhikkhu advises a woman in difficult circumstances to have an abortion, and she follows his advice. A vain bhikkhu, pressed by laypeople about his qualification to teach meditation, claims an experience of jhāna he does not have.6 Each of these falls. His counsel is to keep well clear of the four, because a doubt here cannot be settled as other doubts are. For any other rule a bhikkhu ends his doubt by confessing the offence; for a pārājika, in his words, "he is compelled to end his doubt before ending his monkhood."

    Footnotes (Pārājika — Class Overview)

    1. Thanissaro, BMC1, Pārājika, introduction to the class — a bhikkhu who commits any of the four pārājikas "is automatically no longer a bhikkhu" and "can never again in this lifetime be ordained as a bhikkhu"; the falling is intrinsic to the act, not imposed by a subsequent act of the Community. The rendering of pārājika as "defeat" is discussed there and in Brahmāli's Introduction to the Monks' Analysis.↩︎

    2. Ajahn Brahm, Vinaya Notes, vol. 1, Summary of the 4 Pārājikas — such an ex-bhikkhu "is barred for the rest of his life from re-ordaining as a bhikkhu though, according to the Samantapāsādikā (page 492) he may 'go forth' as a sāmaṇera (novice). Traditionally, ex-bhikkhus have been allowed to 'go forth' as sāmaṇeras only in cases where the Saṅgha had considered it suitable in the circumstances."↩︎

    3. Thanissaro, BMC1, Pārājika, closing discussion — "Ignorance of these rules does not exempt an offender from the penalty, which is why the Buddha ordered that they be taught to each new bhikkhu as soon as possible after ordination" (Mv.I.78.2–5).↩︎

    4. Thanissaro, BMC1, Pārājika, closing discussion — "the Commentary concludes its discussion of the pārājikas by noticing that there are altogether 24—eight actual, twelve equivalent, and four derived—pārājikas for bhikkhus and bhikkhunīs"; "The twelve equivalent pārājikas include the eleven disqualified types who should not be ordained as bhikkhus in the first place. If they happen to be ordained, their ordination is invalid; once they are found out they must be expelled for life" (Mv.I.61–68; BMC2, Chapter 14). The eleven apply to bhikkhunīs as well; the twelfth, a bhikkhunī who leaves the Saṅgha and takes up the role of a lay woman, applies only to bhikkhunīs (Cv.X.26.1), the bhikkhunīs having no formal disrobal procedure.↩︎

    5. Ajahn Brahm, Vinaya Notes, vol. 1, Summary of the 4 Pārājikas — "The Samantapāsādikā concludes the explanation of the pārājika by noticing that there are virtually 24 pārājikas in the Vinaya": the four for bhikkhus, "the 4 additional pārājikas for the bhikkhunīs", the eleven abhabba puggala ("Hence they are tantamount to pārājikas"), "the 4 'anuloma' (in line with) pārājikas", and "the 24th pārājika", "a bhikkhunī who, not disrobing, behaves more as a housewife than a bhikkhunī and is for this reason expelled."↩︎

    6. Ajahn Brahm, Vinaya Notes, vol. 1, Summary of the 4 Pārājikas — "even an ordinary bhikkhu who has muddled awareness, lacks knowledge of the Vinaya and who still retains some of the unrefined attitudes which are 'acceptable' in lay life, can easily, carelessly, commit a pārājika"; the cases "may not seem grossly immoral by lay standards, but they all result in a pārājika according to the Vinaya's standards"; "It is imperative, then, for every bhikkhu to know these pārājika rules well and to keep well clear of even coming near to transgressing any of them … For the other rules it is usual for a bhikkhu to solve such a doubt by confessing the offence and accepting the consequences but for a pārājika the consequences are too serious to act thus; he is compelled to end his doubt before ending his monkhood."↩︎
