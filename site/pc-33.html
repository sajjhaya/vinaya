    Pc 33

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying in the hall with the peaked roof in the Great Wood near Vesālī. A succession of fine meals had been arranged in the city, and a certain poor worker, watching them, thought, "Why don't I prepare a meal? It must be really worthwhile, seeing as these people prepare a meal with such respect." He went to his boss Kira and asked to be paid: "Sir, I wish to prepare a meal for the Sangha of monks headed by the Buddha. Please give me my salary." Kira had faith and confidence too, and gave him his salary and much extra. The worker went to the Buddha, bowed, sat down, and invited him for the following day together with the Saṅgha of monks. The Buddha said, "The Sangha is large." The worker said, "No problem! I've prepared much jujube, supplemented with jujube drinks." The Buddha consented by remaining silent, and the worker understood, got up, circumambulated him with his right side toward him, and left.1

    The monks heard what the meal was to consist of, and so they ate in the morning after walking for alms. Meanwhile people who heard whom the poor worker had invited brought him much food of various kinds. The following morning he prepared it and sent word that the meal was ready. The Buddha and the Saṅgha went to his house and sat down in the dining hall, and the worker served them; but the monks kept saying, "Give just a little." He protested: "Venerables, don't accept so little because you think I'm just a poor worker. I've prepared much food of various kinds. Please accept as much as you like." They answered, "We're not accepting so little because of that, but because we ate in the morning after walking for alms." The worker complained and criticised them, "How could the venerables eat elsewhere when invited by me? Am I not able to give as much as they need?" The monks of few desires complained in the same terms; the Buddha questioned the monks, established that it was true, rebuked them, and laid down a first preliminary ruling: "If a monk eats a meal before another, he commits an offense entailing confession."2​ 3

    Three amendments followed. A monk fell sick, and another brought him almsfood and told him to eat it. "I can't. I'm expecting another meal." The expected almsfood did not arrive until midday, and the sick monk "did not get to eat as much as he had intended"; the Buddha allowed a sick monk to eat a meal before another.4 Then, during the robe-giving season, people prepared a meal together with robe-cloth and invited the monks, saying, "We wish to offer a meal and give robe-cloth." The monks knew what the Buddha had prohibited and, "being afraid of wrongdoing," did not accept — and as a result little robe-cloth came to the monks. The Buddha allowed it during the robe-giving season.5 Then people invited monks who were making robes; those monks refused for the same reason, and the Buddha allowed it at a time of making robes, and stated the rule in its final form.6

    There is a fifth sub-story, and it comes after the final ruling. One morning the Buddha went to a certain family with Venerable Ānanda as his attendant, and the people there gave cooked food. Ānanda, "being afraid of wrongdoing," did not accept it. "Accept it, Ānanda." — "I can't, sir, I'm expecting another meal." — "Well then, Ānanda, assign that meal to someone else and then receive this." Afterwards the Buddha addressed the monks: "Monks, I allow you to eat a meal before another if you assign the other meal to someone else. And, monks, this is how it should be assigned: 'I give my expected meal to so-and-so.'"7

    Synopsis — A labourer spends his wages on a meal for the whole Saṅgha, the monks eat on almsround first because they expect little of a poor man's food, and the food they leave in his dishes is the injury; the rule that follows is amended three times for the sick and for the robe season, and then, in an exchange with Ānanda that the rule text was never reopened to accommodate, the Buddha shows that a monk may simply give his prior invitation away.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk eats a meal before another, except on an appropriate occasion, he commits an offense entailing confession. These are the appropriate occasions: he is sick; it is the robe-giving season; it is a time of making robes."8

    Condensed recitation translation — "Eating a meal before an invitational meal, except on given occasions, is a pācittiya."9

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "An out-of-turn meal, except at the proper occasions, is to be confessed. Here the proper occasions are these: a time of illness, a time of giving cloth, a time of making robes. These are the proper occasions here."10

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Having been invited to a meal eating something beforehand, except when ill or at the time of giving and making robes, is an offence of Pācittiya."11

    Ñāṇatusita

    "In [taking] a meal before another [invitation-meal], except at the [right] occasion, [there is a case] involving expiation. Here the occasion is this: the occasion of illness; the occasion of a giving of robe [-cloths]; the occasion of a robe-making; this is the occasion here."12

    One word carries this rule, paramparabhojana, and its renderings differ over what the rule forbids. Brahmāli's two rule statements and Ñāṇatusita's have a meal eaten before the one a monk was invited to, which is the origin story. Thanissaro's "out-of-turn meal" is wider, and he reads it to cover also the monk who accepts one invitation and eats elsewhere instead.13 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual settles the order in its first factor: other staple food eaten "beforehand".14 Ñāṇatusita gives the reason for reading "before": the monks of the story ate before the meal and lost their appetite, and eating after it is the business of Pc 35.15 I state the rule as eating a meal before another; how far beyond that it reaches is taken up under Object.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    An invitation accepted is a household's work waiting at the table, and this rule asks a monk to come to it able to eat, so that his acceptance is never turned into a slight on the giver's faith. A labourer had spent his wages on a meal for the whole Saṅgha; the monks, expecting little of a poor man's food, ate on almsround first and took only a little; and his complaint was not about the food at all: "Am I not able to give as much as they need?"16 The Manual draws the counsel that outlasts the rule's details: a monk should be careful not to disappoint donors, whatever the meal.17

    Object

    The rule bites only on the five cooked foods, at both ends: the monk must be invited to a meal of them, and what he eats elsewhere must be one of them.18 Anything else is exempt, and Thanissaro names milk, drinking conjey and fruit, with a caution in the rule's own spirit, not to take so much as to spoil one's appetite.19​ 20 What counts as an invitation the Vibhaṅga does not define in this rule. The Manual takes it from Pc 32, a personal invitation in which the monk has been named; the question of what makes an invitation is argued there.21

    The rule plainly catches the monk who eats before going to the meal he accepted. Thanissaro and the Concise BMC read it to catch also the monk who accepts one invitation and eats at another instead, at the same time; nothing in Brahmāli's or Ñāṇatusita's reading excludes that, since a meal eaten elsewhere is eaten before an invitation never kept.22 Eating after the invited meal is not this rule's concern: Thanissaro allows a monk to take his invitations in the order he received them, "(but see Pc 35)", and Ñāṇatusita sends the case to Pc 35 as well.23​ 24 That rule needs a refusal of further food, so a monk who finished a meal at which nothing more was offered is not caught by it either.25

    Effort

    Receiving the food intending to eat it is wrong conduct, and every mouthful swallowed a pācittiya.26 Perception does not mitigate: a monk who does not see the meal as a meal before another still incurs the full offence.27 Intelligibly so, since what disappoints the donor is the appetite arriving at his house, whatever the monk thought about it.

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The three occasions are sickness, defined as being unable to eat enough at one sitting, the robe-giving season and the time of making robes.28 Thanissaro gives the reason for the last two: cloth and thread were scarce, donors offered them with a meal, and without the exemption a monk making a robe, already invited, could not go first to receive them.29

    The most useful exemption came after the rule was settled. Invited by a family when he was expecting another meal, Ānanda refused the food, and the Buddha told him, "assign that meal to someone else and then receive this"; a monk may give his expected meal away, "I give my expected meal to so-and-so", and eat elsewhere.30 The word is vikappanā, the assignment used elsewhere for robe-cloth, and Brahmāli's appendix calls this passage "perhaps the most revealing" for what it means: Ānanda had to give the meal up entirely, so the word means a change of ownership, though not necessarily of hands.31 Thanissaro reports that the Commentary treats the assignment as a formality that may be said without the other monk even knowing. Brahmāli's appendix reads the passage as an assignment to a monk not present, whom Ānanda would then presumably have to inform so that the donor was not let down.32 Thanissaro would have the monk say it in the other's presence, making sure he is willing and able to go, and Ajahn Brahm would have him be "fairly certain" that the other will go and that the donor will not be offended.33​ 34

    The rest of the list looks miscellaneous, and Brahmāli read one principle out of it: what matters is whether the donor knows. A monk may take invited meals in the order the invitations came, because each later donor knows he is already invited elsewhere.35 He may also say when invited, "I'll get almsfood".36 A whole village or association may invite him and he may eat anywhere in it, and a regular, lottery or half-monthly meal, or one on the observance day or the day after, singles no one out.37 Thanissaro offers a reading of "I'll get almsfood" other than the Commentary's, which takes it as a refusal: it makes room for the village custom of an early almsround before a late invited meal; without it, "most village bhikkhus would then probably claim a perpetual 'time of illness'."38

    The Khandhaka tells the rule's story a second time and adds a boundary. When the Buddha allowed congee, people served thick rice porridge early, the monks ate their fill, and at the house of a government official who had prepared twelve hundred and fifty bowls of meat they took almost nothing. He made the poor worker's complaint again, "Am I incapable of giving them as much as they like?", and served the rest in anger: "Eat it or take it away." The Buddha ruled that a monk invited to a meal should not eat someone else's porridge beforehand, and Brahmāli's endnote refers the ruling to this rule.39​ 40 The porridge forbidden is someone else's; Ñāṇatusita, Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm all take it that a pre-meal snack from the donor himself is allowed.41​ 42​ 43 And only porridge too thick to sip, eaten with a spoon or the fingers, counts as a meal.44

    The case a monk now meets is the monastery breakfast. Brahmāli held that it is not a separate meal, being food laid out by lay people for the Saṅgha and taken as one likes, more like almsround than an invitation, and so acceptable "because it is generally known that we have breakfast"; he called this "a matter of interpretation" and said it should be settled with Ajahn Brahm.45 Whatever is eaten first, Ajahn Brahm gives the measure: not so much "that it significantly diminishes one's appetite at the meal invitation."46 The rule's whole concern is a household that has cooked for a monk, and a monk who arrives too full to honour it.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • An offence of wrong conduct for receiving the food with the intention of eating it, outside the appropriate occasions.47
    • A pācittiya for every mouthful swallowed.48
    • An offence of wrong conduct where it is not a meal before another but the monk perceives it as one or is unsure.49

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if it is an appropriate occasion; if he assigns his other meal to someone else and then eats; if he eats the food from two or three invitational meals together; if he eats the meals in the same order that the invitations were received; if he is invited by a whole village and he eats anywhere in that village; if he is invited by a whole association and he eats anywhere that belongs to that association; if, when being invited, he says, "I'll get almsfood"; if it is a regular meal invitation; if it is a meal for which lots are drawn; if it is a half-monthly meal; if it is on the observance day; if it is on the day after the observance day; if it is anything apart from the five cooked foods; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.50 Thanissaro adds, from the Commentary, a meal eaten first with the donor's explicit permission.51

    Related Offences

    • Pc 32 — the previous rule, from which the Manual takes this rule's sense of an invitation.
    • Pc 35 — eating after one has eaten and refused more, the rule that covers eating after the invited meal, where this one covers eating before it.
    • Pc 59 — the main rule on assignment, vikappanā, which this rule's fifth sub-story applies to a meal.
    • Pc 4 — the perception pattern, which runs here in the severe direction.

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, all five sub-stories; the final ruling comes after the fourth, and the fifth produces an allowance, the assignment of an expected meal, that appears in the non-offence clauses but never in the rule. The worker's employer is "his boss Kira" as Brahmāli has it. Ajahn Brahm's retelling turns the Buddha's "The Sangha is large" into advice to the worker to reconsider the number of monks, and is not carried.
