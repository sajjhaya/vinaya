    Pc 51

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was wandering in the country of Ceti on his way to Bhaddavatikā. A number of cowherds, shepherds, farmers and travellers saw him and said, "Sir, don't go to Ambatittha. There's a highly venomous dragon with supernormal powers there, in the hermitage of a dreadlocked ascetic. Don't let it harm you."

    The Buddha was silent. They repeated their request a second and a third time, and the Buddha remained silent. He then continued on to Bhaddavatikā, and he stayed there.

    Venerable Sāgata went to the hermitage of that dreadlocked ascetic and entered his fire hut. After preparing a spread of grass, he sat down, crossed his legs, straightened his body, and established mindfulness in front of him. Seeing that Sāgata had entered the fire hut, the dragon was upset and emitted smoke. Sāgata, too, emitted smoke. The dragon was not able to contain his rage and emitted flames. Sāgata entered the fire element and he, too, emitted flames. Then, after conquering fire with fire, Sāgata went to Bhaddavatikā.

    After staying at Bhaddavatikā for as long as he liked, the Buddha set out wandering toward Kosambī. When he arrived, the lay followers there received him. But the lay followers at Kosambī had heard about Sāgata fighting the Ambatittha dragon. And so after receiving the Buddha, they went to see Sāgata. They bowed, stood to one side, and said, "Venerable, what can we prepare for you that's delicious but hard to get?"

    The monks from the group of six replied, "There's a delicious liquor called Kāpotikā, which is hard for the monks to get. Prepare that."

    And the lay followers prepared Kāpotikā in house after house. Then, when they saw that Sāgata had entered the town for alms, they said to him, "Drink, venerable, drink the Kāpotikā liquor." Sāgata drank that liquor in house after house, and as he was leaving town, he collapsed at the town gate.

    Just then the Buddha, together with a number of monks, was also leaving town, and he saw Sāgata at the town gate. He said, "Monks, pick up Sāgata." Saying, "Yes, sir," they led him to the monastery, where they put him down with his head toward the Buddha. But Sāgata turned around, pointing his feet toward the Buddha.

    The Buddha said, "Previously, monks, wasn't Sāgata respectful and deferential toward me?" — "Yes." — "But is he now?" — "Certainly not." — "Just recently, didn't Sāgata fight the Ambatittha dragon?" — "Yes." — "Would he now be able to fight a dragon?" — "Certainly not." — "So, monks, should one drink that which makes one senseless?" — "Certainly not, sir."

    The Buddha rebuked them and stated the rule.1

    Synopsis — The only origin story in the chapter whose subject is an accomplished elder rather than a habitual offender or a faction, and the group of six appear in it only as the mouth that answers a question put to someone else. Nothing is said about Sāgata's state of mind. The rebuke is five questions and their five answers, and the rule follows.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk drinks this or that kind of alcoholic drink, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "Consuming alcohol is a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "The drinking of alcohol or fermented liquor is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Consuming alcohol is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "In drinking alcoholic drink made of grain [-products] or fruit [and/or flower products], [there is a case] involving expiation."6

    The Pāli is two words, a compound and its penalty, and every rendering has to decide what to do with the pair surā and meraya.7 The word-analysis distinguishes them by ingredient, grain against fruit and flowers, not by strength or process, and Ñāṇatusita concludes that "it is not possible to give a satisfactory translation with a single word"; his own rendering expands, accurately and unrecitably.8​ 9 Thanissaro's "alcohol or fermented liquor" keeps the pair and divides it the English way, by process, and Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation keeps it without characterising it, "this or that kind of alcoholic drink". The recitation line and Ajahn Brahm collapse it into one word, which is what the pair amounts to. The choice changes nothing the rule asks of a monk.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    Drink takes from a monk the two things the Buddha's questions over Sāgata single out, his respect and his power, and this rule keeps them, together with the good faith of lay followers who may, as at Kosambī, press on him the very thing that undoes him. Sāgata had overcome a dragon by entering the fire element; after the liquor offered in house after house he lay at the town gate, and then turned his feet toward the Buddha. The Buddha's last question carries the rule: "So, monks, should one drink that which makes one senseless?"10

    Thanissaro treats it under object and effort, and places perception inside the object, where it does not mitigate.11

    Object

    Surā is the fermented product of flour, cookies or rice, with yeast added or in combination; meraya is the fermented product of flowers, fruit, honey or sugar.12 Thanissaro's modern examples are whiskey, beer, vodka and gin on one side and wine, mead and rum on the other, and together the two terms cover every kind of alcoholic drink.13

    Disputed — whether the rule reaches narcotics and other intoxicants.

    Thanissaro extends it by the Great Standards: the Canon criticises alcohol because it destroys the sense of shame, weakens discernment and can bring a stupor, as it did to Sāgata, so it seems reasonable to him to include other intoxicants, narcotics and hallucinogens, though not coffee, tea, tobacco or betel.14 Ajahn Brahm reaches the same result and states the criterion, that a substance "significantly distort or impair one's awareness", from the fifth precept's own word for heedlessness; the Concise BMC follows Thanissaro.15​ 16 Brahmāli declines the extension, and on a narrower ground than it may seem. Drugs are not in the rule, he holds, which does not make them acceptable: "It just means that it doesn't come under this rule." Drugs are "not really the same as alcoholic drinks", and an extension would be "a bit of a stretch".17 He permits nothing; he denies only that this rule is where the prohibition lives. The text leans his way, since the two terms are defined by ingredient, while the reason the Canon gives for avoiding alcohol leans Thanissaro's. I take Thanissaro's reading as the safer guide for practice and Brahmāli's as the closer to the words. Neither source argues the point at length.

    A drink becomes alcohol before it can make anyone drunk. Among the practices the Vesālī monks claimed were allowable at the Second Council was drinking palm juice that had started to ferment but was not yet a proper alcoholic drink. The answer was no: it had been prohibited at Kosambī, in the analysis of the Monastic Code, and the offence is this rule's.18​ 19 For drinks that ferment a little and are not thought of as alcohol at all, ginger beer or soy sauce, Brahmāli's test was what society conventionally counts as an alcoholic drink, and ginger beer, which children drink, would probably be fine.20 The two tests need not collide, since the Council's ruling concerns a liquor set to ferment as liquor, but they are different tests, and a monk deciding such a case should know that the canonical one exists.

    Effort

    "If he drinks even what fits on the tip of a blade of grass, he commits an offense entailing confession."21 There is no amount too small to count, and a small glass of wine, though not enough to make anyone drunk, is as full an offence as a bottle. The Vibhaṅga does not say how the offences are counted. Thanissaro gives the Commentary's count, one for each separate sip, and extends it to each separate effort where an intoxicant is taken otherwise.22

    Perception

    Perception does not mitigate. Where the drink is alcoholic, every perception carries the full pācittiya, the pattern of Pc 4, so that a monk who drinks champagne thinking it carbonated apple juice still commits the offence.23​ 24 Ajahn Brahm finds this less unfair than it looks, since one should be able to notice the colour or the smell beforehand.25

    The penalty is only a confession, and the tradition does not for that reason treat the rule lightly. Drinking stands first among the four stains by which an ascetic "does not shine, blaze, and radiate", which Ajahn Brahm and Brahmāli's own introduction both cite, and that introduction ends its paragraph on this rule without qualification: "Not drinking alcohol is a fundamental aspect of the monastic life."26​ 27​ 28 Sāgata had conquered fire with fire. What brought him down was liquor offered by admirers, a cup at a time, in house after house.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — where the drink is non-alcoholic but he perceives it as alcoholic, or is unsure of it. Where it is alcoholic, all three perceptions carry the full pācittiya.29

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he drinks a non-alcoholic drink that has the color, smell, or taste of an alcoholic drink; if it is cooked in a bean curry; if it is cooked with meat; if it is cooked with oil; if it is in syrup from emblic myrobalan; if he drinks a drink that is normally alcoholic, but which is actually without alcohol; if he is insane; if he is the first offender.30

    The cooked clauses rest on the alcohol cooking off. In Thanissaro's report the Commentary reads them as sauces, stews and meat dishes to which wine is added before cooking, and Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm both exclude food in which the alcohol has not evaporated, such as a rum baba.31​ 32 Oil cooked with alcohol is a medicine, and the Khandhaka sets its test: it may be drunk "if there is no discernible color, smell, or taste of alcohol", and a batch with too much may be kept "for external use"; to drink it otherwise, Thanissaro says, violates this rule.33 Brahmāli carried that test to modern medicines. A few drops of a homeopathic remedy in an alcohol solution he would not call drinking, while a spoonful of cough syrup may be, though "it's very imprecise to define". That is his practical judgment, not the Vibhaṅga's, which sets the line at the tip of a blade of grass.34

    The last clauses concern ariṭṭha, a drink that is not alcohol. Thanissaro divides the clauses differently from Brahmāli, joining the emblic myrobalan to the ariṭṭha, and the difference is one exemption: on Brahmāli's division alcohol in emblic-myrobalan syrup is exempt, and on Thanissaro's only an unfermented ariṭṭha made with it is.35​ 36​ 37 The Commentary Thanissaro cites gives ariṭṭha alcohol's colour, smell and taste, and closes the allowance at the moment of fermentation: "That which has the starter thrown in, however, is alcoholic."38

    Related Offences

    • The Great Standards (Kd 6:40.1) — the instrument by which Thanissaro, Ajahn Brahm and the Concise BMC extend this rule to narcotics, and Brahmāli declines to.
    • Kd 22:2.8 — the Second Council's ruling on palm juice, which refers to this rule by its place.

    Related Suttas

    • DN 31 §8 — the dangers of drink, among them "weakening of the intellect": the loss of power the Buddha's questions over Sāgata single out.39
    • AN 4.50 — drinking liquor, the first of the defilements because of which ascetics "do not shine, blaze, and radiate"; abstaining from it is what Brahmāli calls "a fundamental aspect of the monastic life".40​ 41

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; the five-question exchange is the story's canonical device and is kept entire. Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm read "a salamander" where Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga has "a dragon" (his introduction has "a lizard"), Thanissaro noting that he follows the Thai and Sri Lankan editions. Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual, his introduction to the Monks' Analysis, the recitation, the class transcript (session 028, 12 June 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1 and BMC2, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were checked, with three Khandhaka passages, AN 4.50 in Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation and DN 31 in Walshe's. Three class uses are kept: Brahmāli's side of the Disputed passage, his conventional test for fermented drinks, and his judgment on medicines in alcohol; his other points are carried by the written sources. The Commentary is known here only through Thanissaro. Transcript quotations are in the house spelling, with disfluencies removed.
