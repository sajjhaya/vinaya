    NP 7

    Story (Vatthu)

    While the Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery, the group-of-six monks approached some monks whose robes had been stolen and said, "The Buddha has allowed a monk whose robes have been stolen or lost to ask an unrelated householder for a robe. You should ask for a robe."

    "It's not necessary. We've already gotten robes."

    "We'll ask for you."

    "Do as you please."

    So the group-of-six went to householders and said, "Monks have arrived whose robes have been stolen. Please give them robes." And they asked for many robes. Soon afterwards, in the public meeting hall, one man said to another, "Sir, monks have arrived whose robes have been stolen. I've given them robes." The other replied, "So have I." And another said the same. They complained and criticised: "How can the Sakyan monastics ask for many robes without moderation? Are they going to trade in cloth or set up shop?" The monks heard, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised the group-of-six, "How could the monks from the group of six ask for many robes without moderation?" They told the Buddha, who had the Saṅgha gathered, questioned the monks, rebuked them, and laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — the group-of-six, asking on behalf of robbed monks who needed nothing more, solicit robes from many householders without restraint; the Buddha caps what a robeless monk may accept when a householder invites him to take as much as he likes.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If an unrelated male or female householder invites that monk to take many robes, he should accept at most one sarong and one upper robe. If he accepts more than that, he commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If that bhikkhu is invited by an unrelated householder to take as much robe-cloth as he likes, he should accept cloth for one robe less than the number stolen, lost, or destroyed. If he accepts more than that, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "If that unrelated man or woman householder presents the bhikkhu with many robes (pieces of robe-cloth), he is to accept at most (enough for) an upper and a lower robe. If he accepts more than that, it is to be forfeited and confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "When one's robes are stolen or destroyed or otherwise lost, one may ask for robes or cloth from a layperson who is not a relation to this amount: When lacking all three of the ticīvara one may ask for an antaravāsaka and an uttarāsaṅga or cloth enough to make these robes; when lacking any two of the ticīvara one may ask for just one robe or cloth to make one up; but when lacking only one of the ticīvara one may not ask at all. Accepting cloth in excess of this, having asked from a layperson who is not a relation, is a nissaggiya pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If the unrelated male householder or female householder should invite him to take as many robe [-cloth]s [as he likes], [then] robe [-cloths for] an upper [robe] together with an inner [robe] can be accepted at the most from that [robe-cloth] by that bhikkhu; if he should accept more from that [robe-cloth], [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture."6

    The renderings agree on the limit and state it two ways. The full rule, Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita give it as the rule's own words do, a sarong and an upper robe at most. The recitation and Ajahn Brahm give it as the Vibhaṅga works it out, one robe fewer than the number lost, so that a monk who has lost only one may take none, which the rule's own words do not say. The requirement is the same, and Effort gives the Vibhaṅga's working of it.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule asks moderation of a monk who has cause to ask: his asking stays within his loss. The group of six told monks who had been robbed, and already given robes, that they would ask on their behalf, and went from householder to householder until the donors compared notes in the meeting hall: "How can the Sakyan monastics ask for many robes without moderation? Are they going to trade in cloth or set up shop?"7 NP 6 lets a robbed monk ask; this rule sets how much, so that a donor's sympathy for the newly robeless is not worked beyond the need.8

    Everything but the limit is NP 6's: unrelated and householder, the perception series and the relinquishment.9​ 10 And the rule reaches only a monk who has asked under NP 6's exception: a householder who offers unasked, or one who had invited him to ask, is outside it.11 Thanissaro gives three factors, object, effort and result.12

    Object

    Robe-cloth of the six kinds, not smaller than what can be assigned to another.13

    Effort

    Asking an unrelated householder who had not invited him for more than the limit. The Vibhaṅga sets the limit by what was lost: two robes if three are lost, one if two, and none if one.14 If the donor brings much cloth and invites him to take as much as he likes, he takes only enough for the robes allowed.15 A monk who has lost one robe may take nothing, Brahmāli explains, because two robes are enough under the Vinaya; and he gives the Commentary's allowance that a monk who regularly wears only two may ask for two.16 Perception does not excuse, and Thanissaro adds that it is no defence to feel the householder would gladly give the excess when no invitation was made.17

    Disputed — whether the limit applies to the loss of any robe or only of one of the three.

    The Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī, Thanissaro reports, restricts the protocol to the determined set of three, in terms that suggest a monk who loses a spare robe may not ask at all; the Sub-commentary reads the same restriction as a loophole, leaving a monk who loses a spare free to ask for as much as he likes, charges the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī with ignoring the rule's purpose of moderation, and applies the protocol to any robe lost.18 Thanissaro sides with the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī from the history: each monk once had a single set of three, and NP 6's allowance relieved the hardship of having little or nothing to wear, so a lost spare gives no right to ask.19 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual takes the same reading of NP 6.20 Both sides argue from the rule's purpose, and the texts do not decide between them. A monk who has lost only a spare robe and does not ask for another is safe on either reading.

    Disputed — whether a monk may ask beyond the limit for another's sake.

    NP 6 allows asking for another; this rule's non-offence clauses do not mention it, and the Commentary concludes from the Story, where the group of six did exactly that, that here one may not; Ajahn Brahm, noting the same silence, reads it the same way.21​ 22 The Sub-commentary's strongest reply, in Thanissaro's account, is consistency, that what is barred here should be barred at NP 6 too. Thanissaro answers that it misses the Story's point: if every monk could ask for a robbed monk's sake, "there is no limit to the amount of cloth they could request".23 The Story seems to bear him out, but a story read for its point is not a ruling, and the Vibhaṅga gives none; a monk who asks for another's sake within the limit is outside the dispute.

    Result

    He gets the excess cloth. Asking beyond the limit is wrong conduct for the effort, and when he gets the cloth it is to be relinquished.24

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    By the Commentary, the intention to return the remainder is his saying "After making two robes, I will return the remainder"; and a donor who gives for a reason other than the theft or loss is, in its example, one impressed by the monk's learning.25​ 26

    When the remainder may be kept is a matter of timing, and Ajahn Brahm reads the clause closely. If the donor presses more on him while he asks, even saying "This is all for you", he takes only the allowance and refuses the rest, reading the rule's "accepts" by the Vibhaṅga to NP 18; but if, after he has taken the allowance, the donor offers the remainder, he may receive it.27 Brahmāli's endnote to the clause takes the remainder instead to be "any leftover cloth after the robe(s) have been made", which is Thanissaro's reading: the monk may take more than he needs if he means to return the excess once the robes are made, and may keep it if the donor tells him to.28​ 29 On either reading the monk keeps only what the donor freely leaves with him.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — asking for more than the limit, before the excess is got.30
    • Dukkaṭa — if the householder is related and he takes them for unrelated, or is unsure.31

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he takes too much but intends to return the remainder; if they give saying, "The remainder is for you"; if they give, but not because his robes were stolen or lost; if it is from relatives; if it is from those who have given an invitation; if it is by means of his own property; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.32

    Related Offences

    • NP 6 — the asking this rule limits, and its shared definitions.
    • NP 18 — where "accepts" is defined, as Ajahn Brahm reads it here.
    • Pc 47 — the invitation that takes a request outside this rule.

    Related Suttas

    • MN 3 — "Bhikkhus, be my heirs in Dhamma, not my heirs in material things"; the measure this rule sets on a robbed monk's asking keeps need from turning into an inheritance in material things.33

If the person is related, but the monk is unsure of it, he commits an offense of wrong conduct."↩︎

    32. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, NP 7, non-offenses: "There is no offense: if he takes too much, but with the intention of returning the remainder; if they give, saying, 'The remainder is for you;' if they give, but not because his robes were stolen; if they give, but not because his robes were lost; if it is from relatives; if it is from those who have given an invitation; if it is by means of his own property; if he is insane; if he is the first offender." His endnote quotes Sp 1.526: "the meaning is that he says, 'After making two robes, I will return the remainder.'" His endnote to "if they give, saying, 'The remainder is for you'": "Presumably this refers to any leftover cloth after the robe(s) have been made."↩︎
