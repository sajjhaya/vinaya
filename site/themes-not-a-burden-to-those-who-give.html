    Not a Burden to Those Who Give

    At Āḷavī the monks were building huts for themselves by begging, and because the huts were too large to finish, the asking never stopped: "Please give a man, a servant, an ox, a cart, a machete, a hatchet, an ax, a spade, a chisel; give creepers, bamboo, reed, grass, clay." People came to dread the sight of a monk. "They turned away, took a different path, ran off, and closed their doors," and they ran from cows too, taking them for monks. When Venerable Mahākassapa came to Āḷavī after the rains and walked for alms, the doors closed on him as well, and he asked the monks why a town where "it was easy to get by on alms" had become one where "it's hard to get by".1

    The Buddha answered with three stories. A sage asked the dragon king Maṇikaṇṭha for the gem at his neck, and the dragon left and never returned; his elder brother drew the lesson in a verse, "One is detested for asking for too much." A monk troubled by the noise of a flock of birds asked each of them for a feather, and the flock left the grove. And when Raṭṭhapāla's father asked why his own son, alone of all who came to him, did not beg from him, Raṭṭhapāla answered, "The beggar is disliked, / And so is one who doesn't give when asked." The Buddha then said it without a story: "It's hard, monks, for householders to acquire and protect their possessions."2 Sg 6 limits the size of a hut a monk builds by begging and has the Saṅgha approve its site. The reason for the limit is in that last sentence.

    The givers in the next stories did not have to be asked. At Pc 34 Kāṇamātā baked cookies so that her daughter would not go back to her husband empty-handed. An alms-collecting monk came in and was given some, and he told another monk, who was given some too, and then a third, and the cookies were gone. It happened three times, and the husband took another wife. Then a lay follower about to leave with a caravan gave his provisions away to monks in the same chain, stayed behind to prepare more, followed the caravan alone and was robbed. People asked, "How can the Sakyan monastics receive without moderation?"3 No monk in either story asked for anything. Each took what he was offered, and the harm lay in the sum. So the rule makes the monk keep the count the giver did not keep, at two or three bowlfuls: "If he accepts two or three bowlfuls, he should take it away and share it with the monks. This is the proper procedure."4 The monk who told the next monk where the cookies were becomes the monk who tells him that the house has given enough.

    At Pc 47 Mahānāma the Sakyan had more tonics than he could use and invited the Saṅgha to ask for them, first for four months, then for four more, then for life, and each time the monks were too afraid of wrongdoing to accept until the Buddha allowed it. The group of six, whom he had told to dress properly, turned his invitation against him: "We need a doṇa measure of ghee." He asked them to wait a day, while people fetched it from the cow-pen, and they said, "Why do you give an invitation if you don't wish to give?"5 The rule that followed reads an invitation as the giver made it. If he names the tonics or the period, a monk who asks for others, or after it, commits an offence, and the Vibhaṅga's way through is to say what he needs and leave the choice with the giver: "You have invited me to ask for these tonics, but I need such-and-such a tonic."6

    The cost need not be in goods. At Pc 57 King Bimbisāra went to the hot springs to wash his hair, found monks bathing there, and waited respectfully for them to finish. They bathed until dark, and with the town gates shut the king spent the night outside the city. The Buddha's question to the monks named both halves of the fault: "Is it true, monks, that monks bathed without moderation, even after seeing the king?"7 Nothing in the rule makes the bath more than washing. When a brahmin asked the Buddha whether he went to bathe in the Bāhukā River, which many held "to give liberation", he answered that a fool may bathe in such rivers forever "Yet will not purify dark deeds", and named another bath: "It is here, brahmin, that you should bathe, / To make yourself a refuge for all beings."8 The bath was the monks' comfort, and the Buddha's question set it beside what it had cost the king.

    Even where a supporter offered freely, the rules set a ceiling. At Pc 19 Venerable Channa, whose story is told in Characters of the Vinaya, had the dwelling his supporter built him roofed and plastered over and over until it collapsed under the weight, and the rule allows two or three layers.9 Brahmāli, teaching the rule, takes a step further Ajahn Brahm's measure, that a monk's dwelling should be no better than his supporters': "it's nice to live in a place that is simpler, even much simpler, than the poorest of your supporters."10

    The rules answer one question at a time: how large a hut, how many bowlfuls, which tonic, how often to bathe, how many layers of roof. As a set, they measure what a monk takes by what it costs the one who gives it. The size of the hut follows the labour asked of the town, the bowlfuls follow the house that baked for a journey, and the invitation follows what the giver said. The suttas can reasonably be read as giving the same measure from the monk's side. The Buddha named "four trifles, easily gained and blameless", and among lodgings the trifle is the foot of a tree.11 And Sg 6 began with the almsround of the monk the Buddha held up as content "with any kind of robe", who "does not engage in a wrong search, in what is improper, for the sake of a robe".12 Sg 6 asks of every monk what the Buddha praised in him.
