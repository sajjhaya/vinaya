    Pc 71

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Kosambī in Ghosita's Monastery. At that time Venerable Channa was misbehaving. The monks told him, "Don't do that, Channa, it's not allowable," and he would reply, "I won't practice this training rule until I've questioned a monk who is an expert on the Monastic Law."1

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised him: "How could Venerable Channa say this when legitimately corrected by the monks?" They told the Buddha, who questioned Channa: "Is it true, Channa, that you said this?"

    "It's true, sir."

    The Buddha rebuked him: "Foolish man, how could you say this when legitimately corrected by the monks? This will affect people's confidence."2

    Synopsis — Ven. Channa, corrected by the monks for misbehaving, answered each time that he would not train in the rule until he had put the question to an expert in the Monastic Law; the Buddha laid down a rule against the answer. It is the third Pācittiya rule Channa's incorrigibility produced, and the only one of the three that ends not with the penalty but with what a monk who genuinely does want to know should do instead.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk, when legitimately corrected by the monks, says, 'I won't practice this training rule until I've questioned a monk who is an expert on the Monastic Law,' he commits an offense entailing confession. A monk who is training should understand, should question, should enquire. This is the proper procedure."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu, when rightly corrected by a bhikkhu about a breach of a rule laid down by the Buddha, uses a ploy as an excuse for not keeping it, he incurs a pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu, admonished by the bhikkhus in accordance with a rule, say, 'Friends, I will not train myself under this training rule until I have put questions about it to another bhikkhu, competent and learned in the discipline,' it is to be confessed. Bhikkhus, a bhikkhu in training should understand, should ask, should ponder. This is the proper course here."5

    Brahm

    "Being admonished by another monk, replying that one will not observe that particular rule until one has enquired about it from some other knowledgeable monk, or words similar to this (with the intention of avoiding training), is an offence of Pācittiya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu when being righteously spoken to by bhikkhus should say so, 'Friends, I shall not train in this training precept for as long as I cannot question another bhikkhu [about it] who is a learned memoriser of the discipline,' [this is a case] involving expiation. Bhikkhus, [the training precept] is to be understood, is to be questioned about, is to be investigated by a bhikkhu who is training [in it]. This is the proper procedure here."7

    The renderings agree on the offence and divide over two things. The first is the rule's last sentence, which carries the rule on past its penalty: "A monk who is training should understand, should question, should enquire. This is the proper procedure."8 Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga, Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita keep it; the recitation and Ajahn Brahm drop it.9​ 10 The second is the word the rule turns on, sahadhammikaṁ, rendered "legitimately", "rightly", "in accordance with a rule" and "righteously". The Vibhaṅga settles its sense in one line: "the training rules laid down by the Buddha—this is called 'legitimately'."11 So the word describes what the correction cites, not how it is delivered, and the recitation folds that gloss into the rule. Its "uses a ploy as an excuse" goes a step further and gives Channa's sentence the wider reach discussed under Effort.12

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    To be open to correction is how a monk is trained, and this rule keeps him from closing that door while appearing to hold it open. Channa does not refuse. He agrees to be corrected, only not yet, and not by you: until an expert has been found, he goes on as before. The Buddha's rebuke names what is at stake beyond Channa himself — "This will affect people's confidence" — for a monk who treats every rule that binds him as an open question is one the people who support him cannot rely on. The rule then does something no other rule in its chapter does. It ends by telling the willing monk what to do instead, and what it tells him is the very thing Channa proposed: to question. What it takes away is only the suspension of practice while the question is pending.

    Thanissaro gives three factors — object, intention and effort — and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual two, the second folding intention and effort together.13​ 14 I follow Thanissaro's three, since a sincere monk needs to find himself outside the intention factor.

    Object

    The correction must cite a rule. Sg 12 uses the phrase "legitimately corrected" of the same monk, at the same monastery, where he made himself impossible to correct at all,15 and the Vibhaṅga here divides corrections into two kinds. A correction about something laid down produces the pācittiya. A correction about something not laid down — that a practice does not lead to self-effacement, say, or to a reduction in things — produces only an offence of wrong conduct.16 The lighter penalty is not because the correction was mistaken. It is because what was cited is a standard of conduct, not a rule, and there is no expert in the Monastic Law whose ruling on it would settle anything.

    The lighter branch circulates in a second form, and it matters which one a monk carries. Ajahn Brahm, citing the same passage, gives it as the case where "one is admonished incorrectly, not having broken a rule".17 His version asks whether the accusation is true; the Vibhaṅga's asks what the accuser cites. The two come apart in a case that happens: a monk wrongly accused of breaking a real rule answers with Channa's sentence. On Ajahn Brahm's reading that is wrong conduct; on the Vibhaṅga's it is the full offence, since a rule was cited and the reply put off keeping it. I follow the Vibhaṅga here, since its permutations name the five standards by which the lighter case is identified, and an accusation's truth is not among them. The one correcting must also be fully ordained; a correction from a novice or a layperson, about anything, is wrong conduct at most. Perception does not mitigate: whatever the offender thinks about his corrector's ordination, the offence follows the fact.18​ 19

    Intention

    The Vibhaṅga's word-analysis says only what is said and by whom, but the modern sources all add that the monk must not want to train: Thanissaro makes it a factor, the Manual writes it into effort as "a ploy to avoid having to keep the rule", and Ajahn Brahm's rule line carries it in brackets.20​ 21​ 22 It is the factor that decides most real cases, because the same sentence can be said honestly. A monk who genuinely does not know whether a rule applies, and says so, has not done what Channa did.

    Effort

    The word-analysis prints Channa's sentence and one small extension: naming someone wise, competent or learned instead of an expert on the Monastic Law makes no difference.23 Read alone, that would confine the rule to one form of words. Thanissaro reads it more widely. He gives the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī as taking the rule, "drawing probably on the Great Standards", to cover anything said as a ploy to avoid the rule without open disrespect, and he gives three modern sentences that would qualify, among them "I'll worry about that rule when I come to it."24 The Concise BMC takes the widening over as the rule's effort factor, and Ajahn Brahm's "or words similar to this" carries it too.25​ 26 I take it as the rule's reach.

    The widening fixes the boundary with Pc 54. A monk who says plainly that he will not train in a rule shows the open disrespect that rule punishes; this one is for the monk who puts off the same refusal with a reason. In Thanissaro's words, the factor "closes any loopholes left by Pc 54."27 The Parivāra adds a rung below the full offence: wrong conduct while the sentence is being said, the pācittiya once it has been said.28

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The Vibhaṅga gives one exemption, the offending sentence with the suspension taken out: "I'll find out and I'll train."29 Both monks propose to go and ask. The difference is what they promise in the meantime. Channa suspends his practice until the answer comes; the exempt monk keeps the rule while he finds out.

    There is a second way out, borrowed from Pc 54's non-offence clauses: a monk admonished with an interpretation of a rule that differs from his teachers' may simply say that his teachers taught otherwise. Thanissaro makes the borrowing, and the Concise BMC prints it as a numbered exemption of this rule, labelled as borrowed.30​ 31 I find it sound, though the Vibhaṅga did not carry the clause across, because both clauses describe a monk with a reason other than evasion, and this rule's intention factor fails the moment such a reason is true. Ajahn Brahm gives the practical form. A monk in doubt may confess anyway, out of confidence in the monk reproving him, then find out, keeping the rule in the meantime; one who disagrees may instead suggest politely that the question be taken to a respected senior monk.32 The rule's closing sentence says the same thing positively. The Vibhaṅga glosses it word by word: the monk in training is one who wants to train, and to question is to ask, "Venerable, how is this? What's the meaning of this?"33 And its verb is Channa's own: he said he would question an expert, and the rule replies that a monk in training is one who questions.34

    The Khandhakas finish Channa's story, and the ending is this rule turned inside out. Before his death the Buddha directed that the Saṅgha impose on him the supreme penalty: whatever Channa said, no monk was to correct him, instruct him or teach him. The monk who would not be corrected was given exactly that. When Ānanda brought the news to Ghosita's Monastery, Channa cried out "I'm ruined!" and fainted; then, ashamed and troubled, he went into seclusion, practised diligently, and became one of the perfected ones. When he asked Ānanda to lift the penalty, he was told: "The supreme penalty was lifted the moment you realized perfection."35

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • An offence of wrong conduct where a fully ordained monk corrects him about something not laid down, and he answers with Channa's sentence.36
    • An offence of wrong conduct where one not fully ordained corrects him, about what is laid down or what is not, and he answers the same way.37
    • An offence of wrong conduct for the effort, while the sentence is being said — the Parivāra's rung, not the Vibhaṅga's.38

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he says, "I'll find out and I'll train"; if he is insane; if he is the first offender.39 Thanissaro and the Concise BMC add a second clause from Pc 54 — saying that one's teachers taught differently — for the reasons given above.40​ 41

    Related Offences

    • Sg 12 — being difficult to admonish. Channa's own saṅghādisesa, for refusing correction from anyone at all; this rule shares its phrase "legitimately corrected".
    • Pc 54 — disrespect after admonition. The near neighbour: that rule takes the monk who shows contempt openly, this one the monk who dresses the same refusal as a request for time.
    • Pc 12 — evasive speech when formally questioned; with Pc 54 and this rule, one of the three Pācittiyas Thanissaro groups with Sg 12 as penalties for behaving improperly under admonition.
    • Pc 73 — feigning ignorance of a rule at the recitation. The same move at a different moment: a literally true sentence used to put off an obligation.
    • Sg 9 — using a pretext to charge a monk with expulsion. The Pātimokkha's home for lesa, "pretext", which Brahmāli's recitation renders "uses a ploy", the phrase it uses here.42
    • Pc 42 — where Thanissaro sets out the principle that perception of the other's ordination does not mitigate.43

    Related Suttas

    • AN 4.122 — the monk who, exhorted and instructed by his fellows, is angered and gives up the training: where refusing correction ends, a refusal Channa's answer dresses up as delay.44
    • MN 65 — the monk who, corrected, "prevaricates, leads the talk aside": the move this rule names, for Channa did not refuse the training but put it off until an expert had been asked.45

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; the Vibhaṅga prints Channa's sentence five times over and it is printed once here, with the complaint, interrogation and rebuke given in the standing summary form. The Manual, the recitation, the class (session 030, 16 July 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were all checked; the class's points on sincerity and on what a monk may say are carried by the written sources, and it is not cited. The Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī's widening of the effort factor is Thanissaro's report of it. Ajahn Brahm's advice to keep observing a rule while finding out cites the Vinayamukha, and is given here as his. The account of Channa's end is from Kd 21; the suttas are quoted from Bhikkhu Bodhi's translations.
