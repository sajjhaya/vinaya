    Pātimokkha

    Definition

    The etymology of the term pātimokkha remains a matter of conjecture. The Mahāvagga glosses it as "the beginning, the head, or the entrance (mukha), the foremost (pamukha) of skilful qualities" (Mv.II.3.4).1 The term names the basic code of training rules, and also the sermon in which the Buddha set out the principles common to the teaching of every Buddha: "the non-doing of any evil, the doing of what is good, and the purification of the mind: this is the Buddha's message" (Dhp 183).2 Whatever its etymology, the term denotes a set of principles basic to the practice of the religion as a whole.

    In the Pāli recension, the bhikkhu pātimokkha contains 227 rules across eight classes, in roughly descending order of severity:

    1. pārājika: defeat
    2. saṅghādisesa: formal meeting (suspension)
    3. aniyata: undetermined
    4. nissaggiya pācittiya: forfeiture and confession
    5. pācittiya: confession
    6. pāṭidesanīya: acknowledgement
    7. sekhiya: training
    8. adhikaraṇasamatha: settlement of issues

    Three of these eight classes carry no penalty as such. The aniyata rules give procedures for judging uncertain cases instead of naming an offence outright; the sekhiya rules state only "this is a training to be followed", and breaking one is an offence only when it is done out of deliberate disrespect; and the adhikaraṇasamatha rules are procedures for settling disputes, not prohibitions on conduct. Only five types of penalty are named anywhere in the pātimokkha, and none of them is a physical punishment.3 The purpose of undergoing a penalty is neither to erase bad kamma nor to formally absolve guilt. It is personal and social at once: to strengthen one's own resolve against repeating the offence, and to reassure one's companions in the training that one remains committed to it, in spirit as much as in letter.4

    Age and the Other Recensions

    The pātimokkha is probably the oldest part of the Vinaya. Brahmāli notes that the word pātimokkha occurs "in total over one hundred times in the four main Nikāyas", including in the pātimokkha itself at Pc 72 and Pc 73 and in its introduction, and concludes that "it is reasonable to think that the Pātimokkha is older than the Vinaya Piṭaka": a kind of proto-Vinaya Piṭaka, from which the full Piṭaka later grew.5 Nor was it always the size it is now. In a discourse of the Aṅguttara a monk says that "more than hundred and fifty training precepts come up for recitation every half-month" (A I 230), which, Ñāṇatusita notes, the commentary suggests may be a reckoning from that time, while rules were still being laid down. Ñāṇatusita adds that the seventy-five sekhiyas may not at first have been counted as training rules at all, since their number differs from one school's pātimokkha to another's, and Brahmāli's estimate for the early pātimokkha is likewise "in excess of 150 training rules".6

    The other early schools transmitted pātimokkhas of their own, the Prātimokṣasūtras, and the close agreement among the surviving codes on the most fundamental matters is one reason to think that their earliest parts go back to the Buddha's lifetime.78 One example shows the kind of difference they hold. The Mahāsāṅghika-Lokottaravāda version of the money rule (NP 18) has the bhikkhu take the gold and silver "with his own hand", and adds a clause the Pāli lacks: or should he "even so much as say, 'Deposit it here.'"9 The northern schools' title is itself a difference. They call the code a sūtra, but the Pāli tradition, Ñāṇatusita observes, has "not one single reference to a Pātimokkhasutta in the entire Pali Canon and its commentaries". In his view the title grew from a misreading of sutta in Pc 73, the pātimokkha's conclusion and the Sutta Vibhaṅga, and the recensions show it happening: the Pāli's pātimokkhe uddissamāne, "while the Pātimokkha is being recited", becomes sūtre prātimokṣe in the Mahāsāṅghika-Lokottaravāda, and the single compound prātimokṣasūtra in the Sarvāstivāda and Mūlasarvāstivāda.10 The argument is his: the northern recensions are not read here in their own editions. The Pāli recension is followed throughout.

    Vibhaṅga and the Commentaries

    Beyond the penalties stated in the bare pātimokkha, further penalties are derived from the rules by the Vibhaṅga and the commentaries, covering two kinds of case: (1) a bhikkhu attempts an action covered by a rule, but the action does not reach completion; or (2) a bhikkhu commits an action not directly covered by any rule, but closely resembling one that is.11 Penalties of this derived sort, when traced back to the pārājika or saṅghādisesa rules, take the form of a thullaccaya (grave offence) or dukkaṭa (wrong-doing); when traced back to the nissaggiya pācittiya, pācittiya, or pāṭidesanīya rules, they are dukkaṭa alone, the one exception being the rule against insults, whose derived penalty is dubbhāsita (wrong speech). Breaking a sekhiya rule out of disrespect is likewise a dukkaṭa.12 Every derived offence of this kind is clearable through confession alone. Where a bhikkhu proves recalcitrant all the same, the Community may impose formal disciplinary transactions on him: stripping him of the privileges of seniority, banishing him from a particular community or, in the most serious cases, suspending him from the wider Bhikkhu Saṅgha, with restoration once he has shown that he has mended his conduct.13

    Penalty Methodology

    The Vinaya's system of penalties rests on three basic principles: confession, forfeiture, and ostracism.

    Confession. Several passages in the suttas (DN 2, MN 140, MN 61) state that it is growth in the discipline of the noble ones to see one's own transgression as a transgression, make amends in line with the Dhamma, and exercise restraint going forward; and the Buddha instructs his son Rāhula that any harm done by word or deed should be confessed to a knowledgeable companion in the holy life.14 Across these passages, the willingness to confess one's own faults is essential to spiritual progress. It is never a mere procedural formality.

    Forfeiture. In most cases forfeiture is a symbolic adjunct to confession: the object is forfeited, the offence confessed, and the object then returned. Only where an item is altogether improper for a bhikkhu to own — money, gold, or gems being the clearest case — must it be forfeited for good and not returned.15 Forfeiture is a check against greed and a reminder of the contentment and fewness of wishes the Buddha repeatedly named as basic to the training (AN 8.53, AN 4.28).16

    Ostracism. Admirable friendship and companionship are, in the Buddha's own words to Ānanda, "the entirety of the celibate life" (SN 45.2). To be set apart from one's well-behaved companions in the training is therefore one of the few things a serious bhikkhu would naturally fear, and the prospect is an effective deterrent against the conduct that would bring it about.17

    Of the three, confession gives the Vinaya's penalty system a means of rehabilitation and ostracism a deterrent, each growing directly out of principles basic to the practice of the Dhamma. Neither is a legal apparatus imposed from outside.18

    Factors of Offences

    In analysing what constitutes an offence, the Vibhaṅga divides an action into five factors: the object, the perception, the intent, the effort, and the result.19 Some rules require all five for the full offence; others require only some of them, and where a required factor is missing the penalty changes. A bhikkhu who sits idly while a flood sweeps a person downstream commits no offence under the killing rule (Pj 3), even if the person drowns, because he has made no effort.20 Other rules, conversely, leave a factor out of account. A bhikkhu who sets out to scare a fellow bhikkhu (Pc 55) incurs the offence whether or not the other is scared: the rule looks to effort and intention, not to result.21

    To read for the factors a rule requires, and for those it leaves aside, is one of the more important habits a student of the Vinaya can develop, both for understanding the texts and for living carefully day to day. The pātimokkha rules govern social relationships, among bhikkhus and with the laity alike, and what looks at first like excessive attention to minutiae usually proves to be a rule with far-reaching consequences. The rule that a bhikkhu may eat only what has been given to him (Pc 40) looks simple. Yet with the Khandhaka's ban on eating food he has cooked himself, it means that a bhikkhu can never cook for himself, live on food he has grown, or retreat into self-sufficiency: he remains dependent on, and in contact with, the laity who support the Community.22

    Great Standards

    Who may make or unmake a rule is settled early. At NP 15 Venerable Upasena, told of an agreement the Saṅgha at Sāvatthī had made on its own authority, answers that "we don't lay down new rules, nor do we get rid of the existing ones", and the Buddha approves: "Good, Upasena. One should not lay down new rules, nor should one get rid of the existing ones. One should practice and undertake the training rules as they are."23 After the Buddha's passing the same words settled the First Council's question. The Buddha had told Ānanda that the Saṅgha might abolish the minor training rules if it wished, Ānanda had not asked which rules those were, and the elders could not agree on them; Venerable Mahākassapa then put a motion, and the Saṅgha resolved that it "doesn't lay down new rules, nor get rid of existing rules, and it undertakes to practice the training rules as they are."24 Ajahn Brahm traces to this resolution the "conservative ethos, the strict adherence to the oldest tradition" of the Theravāda. With the Second Council, which held the monks of Vesālī to the old tradition when they tried to allow monks money, it leads him to conclude that "the Theravāda Vinaya we have today is very much the same as the Buddha laid down."25

    Yet neither the Vibhaṅga nor the Khandhakas anticipate every contingency a bhikkhu might face: the pātimokkha grew as an organic response to events as they arose, not as an airtight legal system designed in advance.26 For situations that did not arise during his own lifetime, the Buddha formulated the Great Standards:

    "Bhikkhus, whatever I have not objected to, saying, 'This is not allowable,' if it fits in with what is not allowable, if it goes against what is allowable, that is not allowable for you.

    "Whatever I have not objected to, saying, 'This is not allowable,' if it fits in with what is allowable, if it goes against what is not allowable, that is allowable for you.

    "And whatever I have not permitted, saying, 'This is allowable,' if it fits in with what is not allowable, if it goes against what is allowable, that is not allowable for you.

    "And whatever I have not permitted, saying, 'This is allowable,' if it fits in with what is allowable, if it goes against what is not allowable, that is allowable for you." (Mv.VI.40)27

    A second, related set of Great Standards, formulated shortly before the Buddha's passing at Bhoganagara, governs how the authenticity of a disputed teaching is to be judged. The test is not the personal authority of whoever transmits it: his words are set against the Suttas and the Vinaya, to see whether they "stand with the Suttas" and "tally with the Vinaya."28 Thanissaro makes this second standard his main method for resolving disagreements between manuscript editions and between canonical and commentarial layers: consistency with the wider Canon settles a disputed reading, not the assumed reliability of any one printed edition or commentarial tradition.29 This book works in the same spirit, without claiming to have resolved every such question: where named sources disagree, the entries mark the passage Disputed and set the disagreement out instead of settling it without saying so.
