    Venerable Channa

    Every rule Channa occasioned was laid down at Kosambī, in Ghosita's Monastery, and every one but two begins the same way: "Venerable Channa was misbehaving."1 What the misbehaviour was the Vibhaṅga mostly does not say. Its interest is in what he did when he was told.

    His answer, the first time, is Sg 12's. "Who are you to correct me? I should correct you! The Buddha is mine; the Teaching is mine. The Master realized the Truth because of me." The rest of the Saṅgha, he said, had gone forth from various names, families and castes and been lifted up all at once, like grass, sticks and fallen leaves whirled up by a strong wind.2 The rule that followed makes a monk who will not be corrected the object of a formal correction, repeated stage by stage, and the offence falls only if he will not stop. Thanissaro names the fault in one phrase: "prideful unwillingness to accept admonishment from anyone".3

    The Pācittiyas follow him down the same road. Examined about an offence in the midst of the Saṅgha, he answered with questions: "Who has committed an offense? What offense was committed? … Who are you talking about? What are you talking about?" When that was forbidden he kept silent instead, and Pc 12 was laid down against both.4 At Pc 54, told "Channa, don't do that; it's not allowable," he "just did it again out of disrespect".5 At Pc 71 he found a more reasonable-sounding refusal: "I won't practice this training rule until I've questioned a monk who is an expert on the Monastic Law."6 Each is a way of not hearing.

    The second thread is the use he made of other people's generosity. At Sg 7 a supporter offered to build him a dwelling, and in clearing the site Channa felled a tree revered as a shrine "by village, town, district, and kingdom". The Buddha's rebuke names the cost: "People perceive trees as conscious. This will affect people's confidence."7 At Pc 19 an official built him another, and Channa had the finished dwelling roofed and plastered over and over until it collapsed under the weight, and then spoiled a brahmin's barley field gathering grass and sticks for more.8

    The Pātimokkha does not finish his story; the Khandhakas do. He was ejected by the Saṅgha for not recognising his offences and for not making amends for them,9 and at the First Council Ānanda reported the Buddha's instruction, given at his final extinguishment, that the Saṅgha should impose on Channa the supreme penalty: "Whatever Channa says, the monks shouldn't correct him, instruct him, or teach him."10 It is the thing he had asked for, granted as a punishment. When Ānanda told him, he exclaimed "I'm ruined!" and fainted, and then he stayed by himself, "secluded, heedful, energetic, and diligent", until he became one of the perfected ones. When he asked Ānanda to lift the penalty, Ānanda told him it had been lifted "the moment you realized perfection".11

    Thanissaro reads the Channa Sutta as a second telling of the ending, one that differs from the first.12 In the Khandhaka he is alone under the penalty and becomes a perfected one in seclusion; in the sutta he seeks instruction from other monks and breaks through when Ānanda teaches him. In the Channa Sutta he goes from dwelling to dwelling at Isipatana, saying to the elders, "Let the elder venerable ones exhort me, let them instruct me, let them give me a Dhamma talk in such a way that I might see the Dhamma." The answer he is given does not reach him, and he goes to Ānanda at Ghosita's Monastery in Kosambī, where every one of his rules had been laid down, and asks the same of him. Ānanda's first words to him are these: "Even by this much am I pleased with the Venerable Channa." He teaches him the Kaccānagotta Sutta, and Channa breaks through.13

    On the sutta's telling, the rules against refusing correction were laid down because of a monk who, in the end, went door to door begging for it. That is the use of his story to a monk who keeps them. The rules do not ask a monk to be without faults; they ask him to stay correctable, because there is little the training can do for a monk who cannot be told. Channa's own last words in the sutta say what he found when he could be told at last: "So it is, friend Ānanda, for those venerable ones who have such compassionate and benevolent brothers in the holy life to admonish and instruct them."14
