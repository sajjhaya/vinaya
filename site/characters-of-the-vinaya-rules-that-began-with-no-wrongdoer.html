    Rules That Began with No Wrongdoer

    Most rules begin with a monk who wanted something he should not have had. A few begin with a monk whose motive no one questions, and these stories are worth reading on their own, because they show what a rule is for when it is not restraining a fault.

    The plainest is Pc 38. Venerable Belaṭṭhasīsa, Ānanda's preceptor, lived in the wilderness, dried the rice he brought back from almsround, and moistened it when he was hungry, so that he went to the village only after a long time. Asked why, he told the monks; asked whether he ate food he had stored, he said, "Yes."1 Thanissaro notes that "This is one of the few rules where the original instigator was an arahant", and that "Even though he was doing this out of frugality rather than greed, the Buddha still rebuked him." His own reading of why is offered as a guess: such a habit would let a monk avoid the almsround, "thus depriving themselves of the excellent opportunity that alms-going provides for reflecting on their dependency on others", and deprive the laity of their daily chance to give.2

    At Pc 22 it was the senior monks' turn to instruct the nuns, and Venerable Cūḷapanthaka's turn came round. The nuns expected little: "Venerable Cūḷapanthaka will just be repeating the same thing over and over." He gave them one verse, again and again, and when he overheard them, he rose into the air and taught them with his powers and many other sayings of the Buddha, until the nuns said, "No previous instruction has been as effective as this one from Venerable Cūḷapanthaka!" He kept on "until the dark of night". The town gates had closed, the nuns slept outside, and in the morning people said, "These nuns aren't celibate."3 Nothing in the story is wrong except the hour, and the hour was enough.

    Ānanda stands at the start of two such rules. At Pc 83 the Buddha himself sent him to teach King Pasenadi's household. One morning he arrived when the king was in bed with Queen Mallikā; she rose quickly and her gown fell. "Ānanda turned around right there and returned to the monastery. And he told the monks what had happened."4 The rule against entering a royal compound unannounced follows, and the teaching that comes with it is a list of ten dangers, nine of them suspicions in other people's minds.5 At Pc 41 he distributed the Saṅgha's surplus cookies to the wanderers, gave one woman two by mistake three times over, and set her neighbours saying, "This monastic is your lover." She answered for him: "He gave me two, thinking they were one."6 The rule itself came from a second incident, a monk's gift to an Ājīvaka.

    Two more rules were eased for a difficulty of Ānanda's. At NP 1 and again at NP 21 he had something he wanted to give to Sāriputta, who was away; at NP 1, "Knowing that the Buddha had laid down a rule against having extra robes", he thought, "What should I do now?" The answer at NP 1 is the ten days a monk may now keep an extra robe.7

    NP 15 begins with a monk the Buddha praised. Venerable Upasena came to see him with his followers during a retreat the Saṅgha had agreed no one should break. "Upasena, your followers are inspiring," the Buddha said; and when Upasena learned of the agreement, he answered, "We, however, don't lay down new rules, nor do we get rid of the existing ones. We practice and undertake the training rules as they are." "Good, Upasena," the Buddha replied, and allowed monks who kept the wilderness, almsfood and rag-robe practices to visit him whenever they pleased.8 Then other monks, "Longing to see the Buddha", discarded their blankets to qualify, and the Buddha saw the blankets lying about the dwellings.9 The rule that follows was occasioned by devotion.

    Read together, these stories show that a rule need not accuse the monk who first broke it. What each one guards is outside his mind: the gate that closes at dark, the bedroom a monk walks into, the blankets left lying about and, on Thanissaro's reading, the daily almsround and the families who give. It seems fair to say that a monk of blameless motive, even an arahant, as Thanissaro takes Belaṭṭhasīsa to be, is bound by them, because the training is shared. A monk who keeps a rule whose purpose he cannot see in his own case has these elders for company. The Buddha approved Upasena's answer: "We practice and undertake the training rules as they are."

    1. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 38, origin story: "At that time Venerable Belaṭṭhasīsa, Venerable Ānanda's preceptor, was staying in the wilderness."; "But do you eat food that you've stored?" "Yes."↩︎

    2. Thanissaro, BMC1, Pc 38: "This is one of the few rules where the original instigator was an arahant"; "Even though he was doing this out of frugality rather than greed, the Buddha still rebuked him. The story doesn't give the precise reasons for the rebuke. Perhaps it was because the Buddha saw that such behavior would open the way for bhikkhus to avoid going on alms round, thus depriving themselves of the excellent opportunity that alms-going provides for reflecting on their dependency on others and on the human condition in general; and depriving the laity of the benefits that come from daily contact with the bhikkhus and the opportunity to practice generosity of the most basic sort every day."↩︎

    3. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 22, origin story: "Today the instruction won't be effective. Venerable Cūḷapanthaka will just be repeating the same thing over and over."; "And Cūḷapanthaka kept on instructing those nuns until the dark of night and then dismissed them, saying, 'Go, sisters.'"; "These nuns aren't celibate. They spend the night in the monastery with the monks and only now do they enter town."↩︎

    4. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 83, origin story: "Well then, Ānanda, teach the king's harem."; "The queen saw Ānanda coming and she quickly got up, but her burnished golden dressing gown fell off."↩︎

    5. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 83, origin story: "Monks, there are these ten dangers of entering a royal compound."↩︎

    6. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 41, origin story: "Well then, Ānanda, give the cookies to those who take leftovers."; "This monastic is your lover." "He's not. He gave me two, thinking they were one."↩︎

    7. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, NP 1, origin story: "Knowing that the Buddha had laid down a rule against having extra robes, Ānanda thought, … 'What should I do now?'"; "How long is it, Ānanda, before Sāriputta returns?" "Nine or ten days." "Monks, you should keep an extra robe for ten days at the most."; the same exchange over a bowl at NP 21, origin story ("Knowing that the Buddha had laid down a rule against keeping an extra bowl").↩︎

    8. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, NP 15, origin story: "Good, Upasena. One should not lay down new rules, nor should one get rid of the existing ones. One should practice and undertake the training rules as they are. And, Upasena, I allow those monks who stay in the wilderness, who eat only almsfood, and who wear rag robes to visit me whenever they please."↩︎

    9. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, NP 15, origin story: "Longing to see the Buddha, they discarded their blankets and undertook the practice of staying in the wilderness, of eating only almsfood, and of wearing rag robes."↩︎
