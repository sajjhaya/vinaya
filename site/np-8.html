    NP 8

    Story (Vatthu)

    While the Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery, a certain man said to his wife, "I'm going to give robe-cloth to Venerable Upananda."

    An alms-collecting monk heard that man making that statement. He then went to Upananda the Sakyan and said, "Upananda, you have much merit. In such-and-such a place I heard a man tell his wife that he's going to give you robe-cloth."

    "He's my supporter."

    Upananda then went to that man and said, "Is it true that you want to give me robe-cloth?"

    "Yes, that's just what I was thinking."

    "If that's the case, give me such-and-such robe-cloth. For what's the point of giving robe-cloth that I won't use?"

    That man complained and criticized him, "These Sakyan monastics have great desires. They're not content. It's no easy matter to give them robe-cloth. How could Venerable Upananda come to me and say what kind of robe-cloth he wants without first being invited by me to do so?"

    The monks heard the complaints of that man, and the monks of few desires complained and criticized him. After rebuking Upananda in many ways, they told the Buddha, who had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned him. When Upananda admitted what he had done and that the man was not a relative, the Buddha rebuked him: "Foolish man, people who are unrelated don't know what's appropriate and inappropriate, what's good and bad, in dealing with each other. And still you did this. This will affect people's confidence …" He then laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — Upananda hears that a supporter means to give him robe-cloth, goes to him uninvited and tells him what kind to give; the man complains that the monks are not content, and the Buddha lays down the rule.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a male or female householder has set aside a robe fund for an unrelated monk, thinking, 'With this robe fund I will buy robe-cloth and give it to monk so-and-so;' and if that monk, without first being invited, goes to them and specifies the kind of robe-cloth he wants, saying, 'Please use this robe fund to buy such-and-such robe-cloth and then give it to me,' and he does so because he wants something fine, he commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a householder wants to buy a robe for an unrelated bhikkhu, and if that bhikkhu, without being invited, asks that householder for a fine robe, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "In case a man or woman householder unrelated (to the bhikkhu) prepares a robe fund for the sake of a bhikkhu, thinking, 'Having purchased a robe with this robe fund, I will clothe the bhikkhu named so-and-so with a robe': If the bhikkhu, not previously invited, approaching (the householder) should make a stipulation with regard to the robe, saying, 'It would be good indeed, sir, if you clothed me (with a robe), having purchased a robe of such-and-such a sort with this robe fund'—out of a desire for something fine—it is to be forfeited and confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "When a monk hears that a layperson, who is not a relation, intends to give him a robe (or robe material) and, without being asked, he tells that layperson the sort of robe he should give, desiring to get a quality robe, then on receiving that robe he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "Now, if a robe-fund has been set up for a bhikkhu by an unrelated male householder or female householder [thinking]: 'Having traded this robe-fund for a robe, I shall clothe the bhikkhu named so and so with a robe,' and then if that bhikkhu, previously uninvited, having approached [the householder], should make a suggestion about the robe [-cloth] [saying]: 'It would be good indeed, Sir, [if you] having traded this robe-fund for such and such a robe, were to clothe me [with a robe],' [if the suggestion is made] out of a liking for what is fine, [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture."6

    The renderings agree on the rule and differ in how much of it they keep. The full rule, Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita give the donor's thought, the monk's words and his motive; the recitation compresses all three into "asks that householder for a fine robe", and Ajahn Brahm's rendering moves the offence to its moment, "on receiving that robe". Where Brahmāli has the monk "specify" the robe and Thanissaro "make a stipulation", Ñāṇatusita has "make a suggestion", the gentlest of the three; the difference changes nothing, since the Vibhaṅga defines the act by what is asked, and the definition belongs to Effort.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A monk is to be content with what a donor chooses to give, and this rule holds him to it. A man told his wife he would give Upananda robe-cloth; an alms-collecting monk overheard and told him, and Upananda went to the man and told him what kind to buy, since cloth he would not use was no gift to him; the man's complaint names the fault: "They're not content."7 Ajahn Brahm puts the purpose plainly: this rule and the next encourage a monk not to be fussy about the quality of his robes, above all when they come from lay people "whose faith can easily be shaken".8

    This rule begins a cluster of three on robe funds: here one donor, at NP 9 two, and at NP 10 a fund left with a steward. A robe fund is whatever a donor sets aside to buy cloth: money, gold, gems and other valuables, or cloth, thread or cotton.9 Unrelated and householder are NP 4's and NP 6's.10 Thanissaro gives four factors, object, intention, effort and result; Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual lists six, which fall under the same four.11​ 12

    Object

    The cloth a donor has resolved to buy and to give to this monk in particular.13 The Vibhaṅga gives it no minimum size and names no materials, its attention being on the price, so Thanissaro takes any cloth of any kind to count, where the Manual takes the robe of this rule, as of NP 9 and NP 10, to be a finished robe of whatever kind; the texts are silent on funds for other requisites, but he advises against uninvited stipulations over any of them.14​ 15

    Intention

    Wanting something fine, which the Vibhaṅga glosses as wanting something good or something expensive.16 The Commentary narrows it to the price alone, and so would excuse a request that leaves the price where it was; Thanissaro finds no support for this in the Vibhaṅga, and the Manual keeps both, good quality or expense.17​ 18 It is the wanting that decides, not the outcome. Whether the robe asked for is in fact finer than the donor meant to buy is irrelevant, the Manual says: if the donor had already meant to buy a fine robe, the monk still commits the offence.19​ 20 And the reverse desire is no offence: if the donor wishes to buy something expensive and the monk has them buy something inexpensive, he too has shaped the purchase, without fault.21

    Effort

    Going to the donor, uninvited, and asking for finer cloth, longer, wider, more closely woven or softer.22 Invitation is defined for this rule as the donor's having asked him beforehand what kind of robe-cloth he needs.23 A donor may invite a monk to ask in general or within limits, and Brahmāli adds that asking beyond a limited invitation is asking uninvited.24 Perception does not excuse: if the donor is unrelated, the offence is full whether he takes them for unrelated, is unsure, or thinks them related.25

    Result

    He gets the cloth the donor bought on his word. The buying is wrong conduct for the effort; when he gets the cloth, it is to be relinquished, by NP 1's procedure.26 If the donor ignores the request and gives the cloth first intended, the word-analysis implies no offence, as Thanissaro notes; Ajahn Brahm, who puts a dukkaṭa on the request itself, says there is no further offence.27​ 28

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The rule does not reach, Ajahn Brahm notes, a robe a fellow monastic is to give, except a nun, whose cloth is NP 5's.29

    The exemption for another's benefit leaves an obvious door, and Brahmāli names it: two monks who each ask on the other's behalf both get fine robes and break no rule, but "it destroys the intent of this kind of rule". What the rule asks, he says, is "to basically be content with what is offered by people".30

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — when the donor buys the finer cloth on his word, before he gets it.31
    • Dukkaṭa — if the donor is related and he takes them for unrelated, or is unsure.32

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if it is from relatives; if it is from those who have given an invitation; if it is for the benefit of another; if it is by means of his own property; if the householder wishes to buy something expensive, but the monk has them buy something inexpensive; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.33

    Related Offences

    • NP 9 — the same with two donors asked to pool their funds.
    • NP 10 — a robe fund left with a steward.
    • NP 6, NP 7 — asking for robe-cloth, with the shared definitions.
    • Pc 47 — the invitation to ask for requisites.

    Related Suttas

    • AN 8.30 — "Here, a bhikkhu is content with any kind of robes, almsfood, lodgings, and medicines and provisions for the sick"; the cloth a donor chooses is the "any kind" a monk accepts, and Upananda's word about what to buy is the discontent the rule catches.34
    • MN 24 — Puṇṇa Mantāṇiputta, esteemed as one who, "Having few wishes himself, he talks to the bhikkhus on fewness of wishes"; the fewness of wishes that leaves the quality of a gift to the giver.35
