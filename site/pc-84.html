    Pc 84

    Story (Vatthu)

    At one time the Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. A monk was bathing in the river Aciravatī, and a brahmin came there to bathe as well. The brahmin put down a bag holding five hundred coins on the ground, bathed, forgot the bag, and left.1

    The monk thought, "This is the bag belonging to that brahmin; it wouldn't be nice if it got lost," and he picked it up. Soon the brahmin remembered. He hurried back and asked the monk whether he had seen his bag. Saying, "I did indeed," the monk gave it to him.

    The brahmin thought, "How can I avoid giving a reward to this monk?" And he said, "I didn't have five hundred coins, I had a thousand!" And he seized hold of the monk. After being released, the monk went to the monastery and told the other monks what had happened. The monks of few desires complained and criticised him, asking how a monk could pick up precious things. The Buddha rebuked him and laid down the first version of the rule, which allowed no exception at all.

    Soon afterwards there was a celebration in Sāvatthī, and people were going to the park dressed up for it, Visākhā Migāramātā among them. As she was leaving her village she thought, "What will I do when I get to the park? Why don't I pay a visit to the Buddha!" She removed all her ornaments, bound them in a bundle with her upper robe, and gave it to her slave girl, saying, "Listen, look after this bundle." Visākhā went to the Buddha, bowed, and sat down; he instructed, inspired and gladdened her with a teaching, after which she got up, bowed, circumambulated him with her right side toward him, and left. The slave girl left too, forgetting the bundle. The monks saw it and told the Buddha, who said, "Well then, monks, pick it up and put it aside," and then amended the rule to except a monastery.2

    At that time the householder Anāthapiṇḍika had a whole village working for him in the country of Kāsi, and he had told an apprentice there that if monks arrived he should make them a meal. A number of monks came to that village while wandering in Kāsi. The man invited them for the following day, had various kinds of fine foods prepared, and served them; he removed a ring from his finger before offering the food, and then said, "Venerables, please leave after you've eaten. I have to go to work." He left, forgetting his ring. The monks saw it and said, "If we just go, this ring will be lost," and so they stayed right there until he returned from work in the evening and asked why they were still there. They told him what had happened, and afterwards told the monks at Sāvatthī, who told the Buddha. The Buddha amended the rule a second time, adding a lodging to the monastery, and gave the procedure to be followed in both.3

    Synopsis — A monk who picks up a brahmin's forgotten bag of coins to keep it safe is accused of stealing half of it; the rule that follows forbids picking up valuables, and is then twice relaxed so that a monk may secure what is left behind in a monastery or a lodging, following a set procedure for returning it.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk picks up something precious or something regarded as precious, or he has it picked up, except within a monastery or inside a lodging, he commits an offense entailing confession. If he picks up something precious or something regarded as precious, or he has it picked up, within a monastery or inside a lodging, he should put it aside with the thought, 'Whoever owns it will come and get it.' This is the proper procedure."4

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu picks up anything considered valuable and belonging to someone else, or has it picked up, except within a monastery or house and for the purpose of safekeeping, he incurs a pācittiya."5

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu pick up or have (someone) pick up a valuable or what is considered a valuable, except in a monastery or in a dwelling, it is to be confessed. But when a bhikkhu has picked up or had (someone) pick up a valuable or what is considered a valuable (left) in a monastery or in a dwelling, he is to keep it, (thinking,) 'Whoever it belongs to will (come and) fetch it.' This is the proper course here."6

    Brahm

    "Picking up a valuable, except in a house or in a monastery (thinking to return it to its owner) is an offence of Pācittiya."7

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should pick up, or should make [someone else] pick up, a treasure or what is considered a treasure, except within a monastery or within a dwelling, [this is a case] involving expiation. However, by a bhikkhu having picked up, or having had picked up, a treasure or what is considered a treasure within a monastery or within a dwelling, [it] is to be put aside [thinking]: 'He to whom it belongs will take it.' This is the proper procedure here."8

    The rule's Pāli names two things, ratanaṁ vā ratanasammataṁ vā, and the renderings divide over whether to keep both. Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga, Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita all keep the pair — "something precious or something regarded as precious", "a valuable or what is considered a valuable", "a treasure or what is considered a treasure" — while the recitation and Ajahn Brahm reduce it to one term. The reduction matters because the factor analysis in Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual rests on the distinction the one-term line drops: it divides valuables into the unallowable (money, gold, silver, precious stone) and the allowable, and that division is the difference between ratana and ratanasammata. A reader who meets only the one-term rule will not see where the analysis is about to divide, and that is discussed below, under Object.9

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A monk found holding what belongs to someone else cannot prove why he is holding it, and the rule protects him from that suspicion; in a monastery or a house, where leaving a thing to be lost brings the greater suspicion, it asks him instead to take the responsibility openly, with a procedure behind him. Both halves come from the Story. The monk on the riverbank did the obvious kind thing, saw a brahmin's money about to be lost and secured it, and was accused of keeping half.10 Thanissaro reads the origin story as showing "dangers inherent in such an act even when done with the best intentions", and Ajahn Brahm states the purpose in both directions: to spare a monk the accusation of theft when he is found with another's valuable, and when he has let one be lost in a house he was visiting.11​ 12 Hence the shape of the rule, which forbids the act outright and then licenses it in the two places where a monk is likelier to be blamed for leaving a thing than for picking it up.

    Thanissaro gives four factors, object, perception, intention and effort, and that is the division followed here.13

    Object

    A valuable is defined by a list: a pearl, a gem, a beryl, mother-of-pearl, quartz, a coral, silver, gold, a ruby, a cat's eye. What is regarded as valuable is defined by use, "whatever people regard as valuable or useful".14 The second category moves with the times, and both Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm say so plainly: wallets, watches, keys, eyeglasses, cameras, a mobile phone.15​ 16 The Manual draws the distinction that is practically useful here. Valuables divide into those a monk may never own at all, money, gold, silver and precious stones, which are the ratana proper, and those he may own, which are everything else people happen to value. Brahmāli reaches the division from the non-offence clause: only ratanasammata may be taken on trust, borrowed, or treated as discarded, so only ratanasammata can be a thing a monk is allowed to end up with.17​ 18

    The item must be somebody else's. The Vibhaṅga does not say this in the definitions, but Thanissaro points out that it says it everywhere else, since the whole apparatus of putting things aside, quizzing claimants, taking on trust and borrowing only makes sense of another person's property, and he reports that the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī states it outright.19 Where the thing lies decides whether the rule applies at all. Within a monastery means inside the enclosure if the monastery is enclosed, and in the vicinity if it is not; inside a lodging is defined the same way.20 Thanissaro gives the Commentary as fixing the two vicinities: around the monastery, two clod's throws, roughly thirty-six metres; around the dwelling, the distance one can throw a basket or a pestle.21

    Perception

    The monk must not perceive the item as discarded. If he does, there is no offence, wherever it lies, though only for what is regarded as valuable, since the exemption names ratanasammata and not a ratana proper.22

    Intention

    He must be picking it up to keep it safe for its owner. This is the factor that makes the rule so narrow, and it puts the worst case outside it: picking anything up meaning to keep it, without perceiving it as discarded and without taking it on trust or borrowing it, falls under Pj 2.23 Money picked up for someone other than its owner is harder to place.

    Disputed — whether picking up money for someone other than its owner falls under NP 18 or under this rule.

    Thanissaro reports the Commentary as sending money picked up for one's own use, for the Community, or for anyone aside from the owner to NP 18, and qualifies it: that holds only where the monk perceives the money as thrown away or left for that person or Community, and otherwise the case is Pj 2.24 Ajahn Brahm spells out what the Commentary gives for money received for another's sake: only an offence of wrong conduct, the lesser grade within NP 18's scheme that Thanissaro's report compresses.25 Neither keeps that lesser penalty. Thanissaro finds no basis in the Vibhaṅga for the intention the Commentary adds, so that under NP 18 it does not matter whom the money is for. Ajahn Brahm finds the case here, since this rule's own Vibhaṅga makes receiving a ratana, for oneself or for another, a pācittiya outside the monastery and the house, and on this point "the Samantapāsādikā seems to be in error". They report the Commentary alike and differ on which rule takes its place, and the texts do not decide between them. What neither allows is the thing itself: on every reading a monk who takes up money for anyone but its owner commits an offence.

    Effort

    He takes hold of it himself, or he gets someone else to take hold of it. Where he asks another person, the offence falls when that person does as asked, not at the asking.26​ 27 A modern transaction that moves somebody's money without anyone touching anything is not this effort. Brahmāli ruled that buying a ticket online against another's card is not "having a valuable picked up": picking up "seems quite literally to mean picking up", a physical item from the ground or a shelf. His reason is worth more than the ruling. What makes a physical pick-up a problem is that it leaves the monk holding the thing and answerable for it, and "it is that responsibility which seems to be the problem in this rule"; a bank told to move a sum between accounts leaves him holding nothing.28 He added a caution that is a Dhamma point and not a Vinaya one: a card on a relative's account, perhaps drawn on money that was once one's own, is easily used in ways a monastic should not, and it may be better not to hold one at all.29

    The proper course

    The second half of the rule carries no penalty at all and is nonetheless binding: a monk who has secured something in a monastery is obliged to follow it through. He notes the item's appearance or its distinguishing marks and puts it aside. He has an announcement made: "Whoever has lost anything should come." Anyone who comes is asked to describe what he has lost; if the description matches, the item is handed over, and if it does not, he is told to keep looking. If the monk is leaving the monastery, he first places the item in the hands of suitable monks there, or, if there are none, of suitable householders.30 Thanissaro gives the Commentary's added step, the one the Vibhaṅga leaves open, what to do when nobody ever comes: after a suitable time the item is exchanged for something of lasting use to the monastery, and if the owner appears afterwards he is told what became of it and, if he is not satisfied, compensated. Thanissaro notes that the Canon's only penalty in such a case is that the Community may require an apology.31 He gives the Commentary as also advising a monk not to accept other people's belongings for safekeeping in the first place, so as not to be responsible for them, unless they are left with him regardless.32 Two further pieces of advice have been attached to this rule and neither is in the Vibhaṅga. One is that a monk who fails to secure a valuable left in a monastery incurs an offence of wrong conduct; Thanissaro reports it from the Vinaya-mukha, says no other text has it, and thinks it defensible as a neglect of the proper course.33 The other is a pair of ancient commentaries pulling in opposite directions, as he reports them: the Commentary advising a monk to leave a valuable lying where it has fallen in a part of the monastery many people pass through, the Kurundī advising a monk walking alone on a road to wait beside what he finds for the owner. His judgment on both is the right one: they lie outside what the Vibhaṅga allows, and may at most be adopted as policy where they suit.34

    Against all of this stands the riverbank. The monk there broke no rule, because there was no rule yet; he simply helped, and was rewarded with a public accusation of theft. The rule the Buddha laid down does not tell a monk to stop caring what happens to other people's property. It tells him that the safest place to care about it is inside his own monastery, where the procedure exists, the announcement can be made, and someone else can see him make it.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • The Vibhaṅga gives none. This rule has no permutation series, the source of its neighbours' derived offences, and its word-analysis assigns the full offence to both efforts without grading either.35
    • An offence of wrong conduct, from the Samantapāsādikā in Ajahn Brahm's report of it, for gold or money received for the sake of another; he rejects it in favour of this rule's pācittiya (Disputed, above).36
    • An offence of wrong conduct, from the Vinaya-mukha in Thanissaro's report of it, for failing to secure a valuable left in a monastery.37
    • A nissaggiya pācittiya under NP 18 where, on the Commentary in Thanissaro's report of it, money is picked up for one's own use and perceived as thrown away, and a pārājika under Pj 2 where anything is picked up with intent to steal. Neither is derived from this rule; both are the same physical act under a different intention.38

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he picks up a valuable, or has it picked up, within a monastery or inside a lodging and then puts it aside with the thought, "Whoever owns it will come and get it"; if he takes something regarded as valuable on trust, or borrows it, or perceives it as discarded; if he is insane; if he is the first offender.39 Taking on trust is the arrangement by which one may take a close friend's belongings without asking, and its conditions are set out in the Khandhakas, not here.40

    Related Offences

    • Pj 2 — taking what is not given, with intent to steal; the same act of picking up, distinguished only by what the monk means by it.
    • NP 18 — receiving money; where, on the Commentary in Thanissaro's report of it, a monk picks money up for his own use, perceiving it as thrown away, the case is there and not here; for money picked up for others, see the Disputed passage above.41​ 42
    • NP 19, NP 20 — the other two rules of the money group.
    • Pc 83 — the preceding rule of this chapter, which shares this rule's word ratana.

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; all three substories are kept, since the rule cannot be read without them: the first produces the prohibition, the second the monastery exception, the third the lodging exception and the procedure. Ajahn Brahm's retelling adds a detail with no warrant in the Vibhaṅga, that the brahmin was evading "a customary 5 per cent reward"; it is not used, and the Vibhaṅga's own statement that he wanted to avoid giving a reward stands instead. The Manual, the recitation, the class (session 015, 28 February 2012, the session on the money rules, where this rule is taught), Thanissaro's BMC1, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were all checked. The Commentary, the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī and the Kurundī are given through Thanissaro's report of them, and the Samantapāsādikā through Ajahn Brahm's. The Vinaya-mukha, which this book does not use, reaches it only through Thanissaro's report of it.
