    Pc 27

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time the monks from the group of six travelled by arrangement with nuns. People complained and criticised them: "Just as we walk about with our wives, so these Sakyan monastics walk about by arrangement with nuns." The monks heard the complaints, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised those monks: "How can the monks from the group of six travel by arrangement with the nuns?" They told the Buddha, who questioned them, established that it was true, and rebuked them — "Foolish men, how can you do this? This will affect people's confidence" — and laid down a preliminary ruling.1​ 2

    Soon afterwards a number of monks and nuns were travelling from Sāketa to Sāvatthī. The nuns said to the monks, "Let's go together." "Sisters, it's not allowable for us to travel by arrangement with nuns. Either you go first, or we will." "You have the higher status, venerables. Please go first." But because the nuns went behind, they were robbed and raped by bandits. When they arrived at Sāvatthī, they told the nuns there what had happened. The nuns then told the monks, who in turn told the Buddha. Soon afterwards the Buddha gave a teaching and addressed the monks: "Monks, I allow you to travel by arrangement with a nun if it's a risky and dangerous road that should be traveled with a group." He then amended the training rule.3

    Synopsis — The group of six travel by arrangement with nuns and are taken by the laity for husbands walking out with their wives; the Buddha forbids it, and when nuns on the road with a party of monks keeping the new rule choose to go behind them and are attacked, he allows the arrangement on a road too dangerous to travel without company.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk travels by arrangement with a nun, even just to the next inhabited area, except on an appropriate occasion, he commits an offense entailing confession. This is the appropriate occasion: the road is considered risky and dangerous and should be traveled with a group."4

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu travels on a country road by arrangement with a bhikkhunī, except on a dangerous road, he incurs a pācittiya."5

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu, by arrangement, travel together with a bhikkhunī even for the interval between one village and the next, except at the proper occasion, it is to be confessed. Here the proper occasion is this: The road is to be traveled by caravan (§) and is considered dubious and risky. This is the proper occasion here."6

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "By arrangement, going on a journey with a bhikkhunī, except if there is danger is an offence of Pācittiya."7

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu, having made an arrangement, should travel together with a bhikkhunī on the same main road, even [if] just the distance between villages, except at the [right] occasion, [this is a case] involving expiation. Here the occasion is this: the road, which is considered risky [and] which is dangerous, has to be gone with a company [of other travellers], this is the occasion here."8

    The renderings agree on what is forbidden, and the recitation is where they part. It drops "even just to the next inhabited area", adds "on a country road", which the full translation lacks, and reduces the occasion to "a dangerous road".9 Ajahn Brahm's "except if there is danger" makes the same reduction. Both change something a monk needs to know, because the occasion has two limbs: the road must be risky and dangerous, and it must be one that cannot be travelled without a company, as the full translation, Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita all keep; the occasion is discussed below, under Effort.10 Most work falls on the phrase the recitation drops, antamaso gāmantaram-pi, "even just to the next inhabited area": whether it measures the offence or marks its minimum is this rule's live question, taken up under the third part of Effort.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    On the road a monk and a nun travelling by their own arrangement look like a couple, and this rule keeps a monk from it, for his own restraint and for the confidence of the people; its exception keeps that restraint from leaving the nuns alone where the road is dangerous. Both halves are in the Story. The laity said of the group of six, "Just as we walk about with our wives, so these Sakyan monastics walk about by arrangement with nuns," and the Buddha rebuked them for what it would do to people's confidence. Then a party of monks kept the new rule, the nuns chose to go behind them, and they were attacked on the road.11

    The chapter's second half has been borrowing from the Nissaggiya class, Pc 25 from NP 5 and Pc 26 from NP 4, and that run stops here. This rule heads a family of its own: Pc 28 is the same rule for a boat, Pc 66 for a caravan known to include thieves, and Pc 67 for a woman who is not a nun. It carries the arrangement machinery for all of them. Thanissaro gives two factors, object and effort, with the effort in four parts: making an arrangement to travel together, travelling together as arranged, from one village to the next, and not on an allowable occasion.12 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual spreads the same analysis over six factors.13 I follow Thanissaro's division, since almost the whole rule sits in his effort.

    Object

    A nun fully ordained by both Saṅghas is grounds for the pācittiya; any other woman falls under Pc 67.14​ 15

    Effort

    Making the arrangement. The Vibhaṅga defines an arrangement by example: he proposes going together and she agrees, or she proposes and he agrees, with or without a day named, today, tomorrow or the day after.16 Both must assent. If the monk proposes and the nun does not express her agreement, he incurs wrong conduct, and, Thanissaro adds, even if they then travel together as he proposed; if she proposes and he does not express his, he incurs nothing.17​ 18​ 19 The word-analysis gives wrong conduct for the arrangement at the end of its examples, so the arrangement is itself an offence, and the pācittiya comes with the travelling.20 Any expressed agreement to go together counts, whether or not it names a time; Thanissaro sets aside the Commentary's requirement of a stated time, since the Vibhaṅga's own examples often have none.21 Assent need not be spoken: Thanissaro cites the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī for the offence being committed by body as well as speech, which he takes to mean that a written message or a text will do, though silence will not. And his test is whether a statement mentions both sides of the journey, "We'll go", "Let's go", or only one's own plans, "I'll go".22

    The test settles the cases a monk will actually meet. Where a third party organises a group, the monk and the nun may both join it, Thanissaro says, so long as they do not address each other, directly or through an intermediary, about making the trip.23 What the rule catches is an agreement between the monk and the nun; a journey each has arranged separately with someone else is not one, since going without an arrangement is among the exemptions.24 But company does not cure an arrangement once made: Ajahn Brahm says outright that travelling by arrangement within a large group of monks and laypeople is still the offence, and the rule's only allowance for travelling in a group is the dangerous road, discussed below.25

    Going as arranged. They must travel as the arrangement specified. Where it fixed no time, leaving together at any time fulfils this part; where it fixed one, only leaving within it does, and setting out earlier or later than arranged carries no penalty. A change of means does not matter: agreeing to go by train and going by car still fulfils it.26

    From one inhabited area to the next. Where the inhabited areas are close together, "a chicken's flight apart", there is a pācittiya for every one passed; in a wilderness, one for every half-yojana travelled.27 Thanissaro puts the half-yojana at about eight kilometres, as does the Manual; Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga, on a shorter yojana of his own, has six.28​ 29​ 30 Whether gāmantara means the gap between villages or between houses was argued in the commentarial period. Thanissaro decides for villages, since in the nuns' rules the same term means the country outside a village, and Ajahn Brahm reaches the same conclusion from the same texts, against a reading he attributes to the Vinayamukha, that travel within a village is included.31​ 32 Walking for alms through a town is therefore outside the rule, which the Manual confines to "any road outside of inhabited areas".33

    Disputed — whether a short trip together by arrangement on a country road, not reaching the next inhabited area, is an offence.

    The Vibhaṅga's word-analysis reads antamaso gāmantaram-pi as a measure: the offence falls interval by interval, so a trip that reaches no next inhabited area and no half-yojana does not reach it, and Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm follow that count.34​ 35 The Manual gives the measure as its factor, "Between inhabited areas or more than 8 km, whichever comes first", and then suggests, tentatively, that the phrase is rather an idiom of minimality: "It may be, however, that the point of the phrase in the Pali rule … is that even a short distance of travelling together involves an offence, regardless of whether there are any villages en route or not."36 The tradition applies the word-analysis, and a monk who follows it keeps the rule as the Vibhaṅga states it; one who treats any shared journey by arrangement as within the rule asks no more of himself than the Manual's tentative reading would. Which the phrase means, the texts leave open.

    Except on an appropriate occasion. The road must be both risky and dangerous: risky when a place has been seen along it where criminals camp, eat, stand, sit or lie down, dangerous when criminals have been seen there injuring, robbing or beating people. These are the definitions of NP 29 and Pd 4, moved from a dwelling to a road.37 And the road must be one that cannot be travelled without a company. The Manual finds no text that defines the company, and suggests one other person of each gender, and in a second note one other person beside the monk and the nun, without reconciling the two.38 The allowance lasts only as long as the danger. If they set out thinking the road dangerous and then see that it is not, the nuns are to be dismissed: "Go, Sisters."39

    Perception does not mitigate where the arrangement is real: if there is one, the full offence falls whether he perceives it, is unsure of it, or does not perceive it, and Thanissaro refers the reader to Pc 4.40​ 41

    The two sub-stories point in opposite directions, and the final rule holds both: the laity's suspicion in the first, and in the second the new rule kept on a dangerous road, where the nuns, going behind, were attacked. What the rule asks, as both stories show, is a monk's care for how he is seen and for the nuns' safety together.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • An offence of wrong conduct for making the arrangement itself, on the word-analysis's closing clause; the pācittiya is incurred by travelling as arranged.42
    • An offence of wrong conduct where the monk makes the arrangement and the nun does not express her agreement, even if they subsequently travel together as he proposed.43
    • An offence of wrong conduct where there is in fact no arrangement, but the monk perceives that there is one or is unsure.44
    • A pācittiya for every inhabited area passed, or, in a wilderness, for every half-yojana travelled.45

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if it is an appropriate occasion; if he goes without an arrangement; if the nun has made an arrangement but he has not expressed his agreement; if they go, but not according to the arrangement; if there is an emergency; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.46 Thanissaro gives the Commentary's illustration of the emergency: savage tribes attacking the countryside.47

    Related Offences

    • Pc 28 — travelling by arrangement in the same boat with a nun, except to cross over; the same rule on water, sharing this rule's definition of an arrangement and its perception series.
    • Pc 66 — travelling by arrangement with a caravan known to include thieves; the same effort, with the danger inside the company.
    • Pc 67 — travelling by arrangement with a woman; the same rule with the object widened, catching every case this one does not.
    • Pc 30 — sitting alone in private with a nun; one of the rules on being alone with a woman, where this rule reaches further, since the presence of other people does not cure an arrangement.
    • NP 29, Pd 4 — the rules built on the risky and dangerous wilderness dwelling, whose definitions this rule moves to the road.
    • Pc 4 — where the perception series this rule follows is set out: a monk who does not perceive that he has made an arrangement is no better off for not perceiving it.

    Related Suttas

    • SN 47.7 — the monkey caught at five points for straying "outside your own resort into the domain of others", the sensual pleasures: the danger to a monk's own restraint behind the rule.48
