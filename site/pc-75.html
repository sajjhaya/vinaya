    Pc 75

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. The monks from the group of six raised their hands in anger against the monks from the group of seventeen. Expecting to be hit, they cried. Other monks asked them why, and they told them what had happened.1

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised them, "How could the monks from the group of six do this?" The Buddha questioned them, established that it was true, rebuked them, and stated the rule.2

    Synopsis — The monks of the group of six raised their hands in anger against the monks of the group of seventeen, who cried, expecting to be hit. The rule that follows makes the threatening gesture itself the offence.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk raises a hand in anger against a monk, he commits an offense entailing confession."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu, motivated by anger, physically threatens a bhikkhu, he incurs a pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu, angered and displeased, raise the palm of his hand against (another) bhikkhu, it is to be confessed."5

    Brahm

    "Being angry at another monk, acting as if to give him a blow is an offence of Pācittiya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu who is resentful [and] displeased should brandish the palm of the hand [threateningly] like [one holds] a dagger to a bhikkhu, [this is a case] involving expiation."7

    The five renderings divide three ways over talasattika, the word the rule turns on. Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga ("raises a hand") and Thanissaro ("raise the palm of his hand") translate the word; the recitation ("physically threatens") and Ajahn Brahm ("acting as if to give him a blow") translate the act the word covers; and Ñāṇatusita translates the image. He takes the word from tala, the palm, and satti, a spear or dagger, and calls the whole "an idiom denoting a threatening gesture with the hand".8 The rule's own picture, then, is of a hand held up as though it were a blade. On every rendering the act is a gesture of the body, which is taken up below, under Effort.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    Pc 74 guards a monk's restraint of his anger at the blow; this rule moves the same guard back to the moment before it, because the harm begins there. The monks of the group of seventeen were not struck. They cried because they expected to be.9 A raised hand reaches the one it is raised against whether or not it ever falls, and a monk who has let his anger get as far as the gesture has already lost the restraint the two rules protect.

    Almost everything else in this rule is Pc 74's. The intention clause, the object, the permutations and the exemption are the same, and Thanissaro states the relation in one sentence: the rule "is similar to the preceding one, differing only in the factor of effort".10​ 11​ 12 The factors are its three: object, effort and intention.

    Object and Intention

    A fully ordained monk, and anger, as at Pc 74. Raising a hand in anger against anyone else is an offence of wrong conduct, and perception does not mitigate.13 A gesture made for any other reason is no offence: Ajahn Brahm's example is scaring off a stray dog by pretending to throw stones at him.14

    Effort

    The word-analysis gives two ways of raising a hand, not Pc 74's three: raising "any part of his body or anything connected to his body", down to a water-lily leaf.15 The released thing has dropped out, and the reason is plain enough on the face of it: one cannot raise a thing one has already let go of. Thanissaro and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual give the same two limbs, Thanissaro naming the foot beside the hand.16​ 17 Ajahn Brahm adds a third, "picking up a stone, threatening to throw it", citing a passage of the word-analysis that does not contain it.18 Nothing is lost by its absence, for a stone picked up and brandished is a thing connected to the body while it is in the hand, and the second limb catches it.

    The threat itself lies in the rule's word and not in the word-analysis, which says only "raises". Thanissaro adds "in a threatening manner", and the addition is right: without it a monk who lifts his arm in irritation while he talks would be caught, and Ñāṇatusita's reading of the word as a threatening gesture is what keeps the rule to the motion of striking.19​ 20 A spoken threat is not this rule at all; depending on what is said it may be insulting speech under Pc 2, a groundless charge under Pc 76, or nothing.

    The offence is complete at the raising, and the Vibhaṅga does not ask whether the other monk was frightened.21 Below it the Parivāra sets a lesser rung: wrong conduct for the effort while the hand is being raised, the full offence once it has been.22 The hand stopped halfway is still a hand raised in anger, and the Vinaya has a name for it.

    What if the raised hand comes down by accident? Thanissaro gives the Commentary's ruling: an offence of wrong conduct where a monk meant only to raise his hand and a blow lands. He takes that wrong conduct to be for the blow, the pācittiya for the raising standing beside it.23 I follow him: the raising was complete before the hand fell, and nothing that happens afterwards undoes it. And because the blow was not meant, it does not bring Pc 74 in; that rule needs a blow given, and this one fell.

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The exemption is Pc 74's: there is no offence for a monk who, being harassed, raises his hand aiming at release.24 The point a monk needs here is how far it reaches. Thanissaro reports a reading that counts a monk who raises his hand at an animal making a mess as aiming at release from the mess, and objects that this "would open a large loophole" for a monk who wanted to justify raising his hand against another monk whenever he was displeased, and limits the allowance to physical danger.25 I follow his limit. Release in the exemption is release from harm, and a monk who is merely displeased has nothing to be released from.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — raising a hand in anger against anyone not fully ordained, however he is perceived.26
    • Dukkaṭa (Parivāra) — for the effort, while the hand is being raised and before the raising is complete.27
    • Dukkaṭa (the Commentary, in Thanissaro) — where the raised hand accidentally lands; on his reading, beside the pācittiya for the raising.28

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if, being harassed by someone, he raises his hand aiming at release; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.29

    Related Offences

    • Pc 74 — hitting a monk in anger: this rule's anchor. Same story, intention, object and exemption; the effort differs by one mode, and the completed act by a blow.
    • NP 25, Pc 17 — snatching back a robe in anger, and evicting a monk from a dwelling in anger. With Pc 74 and this rule, the rules whose text carries kupito anattamano.
    • Pc 55 — frightening a monk. There the wish to frighten is the intention and the result does not matter; here the wish is anger, the fear is in the story only, and the gesture completes the offence by itself.
    • Pc 2, 76 — insulting speech, and a groundless charge of a saṅghādisesa: where a threat is spoken, not made with the body.
    • Sg 2 — lustful bodily contact with a woman: the case Pc 74 sends there.
    • Pc 42 — where Thanissaro sets out the principle that perception of the other's ordination does not mitigate.
    • Pc 17, 52, 55, 60, 74, 77 — with this rule, the rules generated by the group of six's dealings with the group of seventeen, whose own ordination under twenty generates Pc 65. Not all of it is quarrelling: Pc 52's tickling and Pc 60's hidden bowls are horseplay that turned out badly.

    Related Suttas

    • MN 21 — the simile of the saw, as at Pc 74.30
    • Dhp 3–5 — hostility is never pacified by hostility, as at Pc 74.31

    The Group of Six and the Group of Seventeen (Characters of the Vinaya)

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; Ajahn Brahm's retelling has the seventeen having been hit before, one sense of the Pāli pahārasamuccitā ("regularly beaten; expecting a beating", in the DPD); the Story keeps Brahmāli's "Expecting to be hit". The Manual, the recitation, the class (session 031, 23 July 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1 and BMC2, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were all checked; the class's points on a spoken threat and a raised foot are carried by Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita, and it is not cited. Ñāṇatusita's Analysis is the volume cited, since his Bhikkhu Pātimokkha prints this rule's English without the intention clause. The Commentary is Thanissaro's report of it. MN 21 is quoted from Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation, and Dhp 3–5 from Bhikkhu Suddhāso's.
