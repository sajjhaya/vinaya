    Pc 36

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time two monks were travelling through the Kosalan country on their way to Sāvatthī. One monk misbehaved and the other said to him, "Don't do that! It's not allowable." Because of that the first monk became resentful. They then carried on to Sāvatthī.1

    Soon afterwards an association in Sāvatthī was offering a meal to the Saṅgha. When the second monk had finished his meal and refused an invitation to eat more, the resentful monk brought back some almsfood from his own family. He then said to the other, "Please eat!" — "There's no need. I'm full." — "The almsfood is nice, please eat." And because he was pressured, he ate the almsfood. The resentful monk then said to him, "Who are you to correct me when you eat food that's not left over even though you have finished your meal and refused an invitation to eat more?" — "Shouldn't you have told me?" — "Shouldn't you have asked?"2

    The second monk told the monks what had happened, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised the other, "How could a monk invite another monk to eat food that's not left over, when the other has finished his meal and refused an invitation to eat more?" The Buddha questioned the monk, established that it was true, rebuked him, and stated the rule.3

    Synopsis — A monk corrected on the road nurses the correction until Sāvatthī, then presses food on the monk who corrected him — food he knows the other may not eat — and springs the trap the moment it is swallowed; the two-line exchange that closes the story, each asking why the other did not speak, turns out on Thanissaro's reading to be a question with two right answers.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk invites a monk, whom he knows has finished his meal and refused an invitation to eat more, to eat fresh or cooked food that is not left over, saying, 'Here, monk, eat,' aiming to criticize him, then when the other has eaten, he commits an offense entailing confession."4

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu, aiming to criticize, tricks a bhikkhu into breaking the previous rule, he incurs a pācittiya."5

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu, knowingly and wishing to find fault, present staple or non-staple food he has brought to a bhikkhu who has eaten and turned down an offer (of further food), saying, 'Here, bhikkhu, chew or consume this' — when it has been eaten, it is to be confessed."6

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Persuading another monk who you know has 'finished his meal' to eat again that day, except from food left over from another monk's meal, desiring to get him into trouble, is an offence of Pācittiya."7

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu, knowingly [and] desiring to cause offence, should invite a bhikkhu, who has eaten [a meal and] who has been invited [to take more], to take uncooked food or cooked food which is not left over [saying]: 'Here, bhikkhu, chew and eat!,' when [the food] has been eaten, [this is a case] involving expiation."8

    This rule borrows its object from Pc 35, whose definitions of the meal, the refusal, food not left over and the two kinds of food it repeats word for word, and the renderings of those words are discussed there.9 What is its own are two words, jānaṃ, "knowing", and āsādanāpekkho, and only the second divides the translators: Brahmāli has "aiming to criticize him", Thanissaro "wishing to find fault", Ajahn Brahm "desiring to get him into trouble", and Ñāṇatusita "desiring to cause offence", with "to take revenge" and "to rebuke" as alternatives.10 The difference does not change the rule, because the Vibhaṅga settles the word by listing what the monk has in mind, discussed below under Intention. Jānaṃ is uncontested, and its work is done under Perception. The recitation line is the furthest compression in the chapter: "tricks a bhikkhu into breaking the previous rule" replaces the food, the words spoken and the requirement that the other actually eat. It takes no side, since every source reads the rule that way.11

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A fellow monk's offering should never be a snare, and this rule keeps the training from being turned into a weapon between monks, for the ease of good monks and the peace of the Saṅgha. One monk corrected another on the road, "Don't do that! It's not allowable", and nursed the correction until Sāvatthī; there he pressed food on the monk who had corrected him, food he knew the other could no longer eat, and sprang the trap once it was swallowed: "Who are you to correct me when you eat food that's not left over".12

    Pc 35 is a rule about a monk and his donor; this one is about two monks. Thanissaro observes that none of the texts mentions a monk trying to trick another into an offence under any other rule.13 He gives five factors: object, effort, perception, intention and result.14 The effort is Pc 35's; the object borrows its food; and the last three are this rule's own.

    Object

    The object is staple or non-staple food that the monk perceives not to be left over, and Thanissaro draws the point out: whether it actually is left over does not matter, only what the one offering it assumes.15 A monk who sets the trap with food that, unknown to him, has been made left over is still caught; one who hands over food he believes left over is not. The rule is aimed at the design, and the design lives in the mind of the one who makes it.

    Effort

    The effort is offering such food to a monk who has eaten and refused, in the sense Pc 35 defines, with words like "Here, monk, eat"; the Vibhaṅga glosses the invitation as "Take as much as you like."16

    Perception

    Here the rule turns the other way from the rules around it. Where the other monk has refused and the one offering knows it, the offence is a pācittiya; where he is unsure, wrong conduct; where he does not know, no offence at all. Believing wrongly that the other has refused is wrong conduct.17​ 18 The reason is in the rule's own words. Jānaṃ makes knowledge part of the offence, as it does at Pc 29; Pc 35 and Pc 37, on either side, lack the word, and in both perception does not mitigate. The Vibhaṅga defines it in its standing way: he knows by himself, or others have told him, or the monk has told him.19

    Intention

    The rule requires that the monk be aiming to criticise the other, and the Vibhaṅga spells out what that means: he offers the food thinking that with it he will accuse him, remind him, counter-accuse him, counter-remind him, or humiliate him.20 Thanissaro reads the first four as the steps of a formal accusation and the fifth as the plain wish to shame, so the term covers a procedural purpose and a personal one at once.21 Without either there is no offence. Ajahn Brahm gives the examples: a monk who offers more food not knowing that the other has finished, or wrongly thinking it left over, has no wish to get him into trouble and commits nothing.22

    Result

    The offence is complete when the other monk finishes eating. Before that, every step falls on the monk who set the trap as wrong conduct: the offering, the other's receiving it to eat, and every mouthful he swallows.23​ 24 So the offender watches his own offence being completed by someone else, and could stop it at any point before the last mouthful by speaking. Ajahn Brahm's version of the factor list puts the result at the first mouthful, which the Vibhaṅga does not support.25

    Every one of those offences falls on the monk who set the trap. Thanissaro, following the Commentary, rejects a reading of the permutation series that would give some of them to the monk who was tricked, since it would contradict what Pc 35 gives a monk who eats under a misperception; the Concise BMC records the same.26​ 27 What follows is uncomfortable. The tricked monk, as Thanissaro reports the Commentary, is treated under Pc 35; since perception does not mitigate there, Thanissaro concludes, he is not excused for having been deceived.28 And where the monk who set the trap goes on to make the other feel abashed, Pc 2, on insulting speech, applies as well, so that trap and taunt are two offences and not one.29

    The rule does not reach everything. A monk who tricks another into breaking Pc 35 without any wish to blame him, only for the satisfaction of watching, would incur no penalty under this or any other rule. Thanissaro's counsel is to look past the rule to its principle: "that one should not deliberately trick another person into breaking a rule or vow that he or she has pledged to uphold."30 What the rule legislates against, then, is deception put to the use of accusation. And it leaves the story's last exchange standing. "Shouldn't you have told me?" asks the one who was tricked; "Shouldn't you have asked?" answers the one who tricked him. On Thanissaro's reading both were right: the monk with the grudge should have told, and the other should have asked.31​ 32

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    All of these fall on the monk who sets the trap.33​ 34

    • Dukkaṭa — offering the food with the intention of accusing, reminding, counter-accusing, counter-reminding or humiliating the other; again when the other receives it intending to eat; and again for every mouthful he swallows.35
    • Dukkaṭa — where the other has refused but the trapper is unsure of it, or where the other has not refused but the trapper perceives that he has, or is unsure.36
    • Dukkaṭa — offering post-midday, seven-day or lifetime tonics for the purpose of food, with the same three steps.37

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he gives it after having it made left over; if he gives it, saying, "Have it made left over and then eat it"; if he gives it, saying, "Take this food for the benefit of someone else"; if he gives the leftovers from a sick person; if he gives, saying, "When there's a reason, use these post-midday tonics," "… use these seven-day tonics," "… use these lifetime tonics"; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.38

    The texts leave the giving "for the benefit of someone else" unqualified, and Thanissaro thinks it can hold only where the other monk is ill or has not eaten and refused; otherwise the exemption would swallow the rule.39

    Related Offences

    • Pc 35 — this rule's anchor: its definitions are this rule's, and the offence this rule engineers is an offence under it.
    • Pc 2 — insulting speech, which applies as well where the trapper goes on to make the other monk abashed.
    • Pc 29 — the nearest rule with jānaṃ in its wording, with the same definition of knowing and the same lenient perception grid.
    • Pc 4 — the pattern this rule departs from, in which perception does not mitigate.

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; the dialogue is verbatim, including the closing exchange, which is the point of the story. I keep this rule lean on the definitions it shares with Pc 35, its anchor. Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual, the recitation, the class transcript (session 026, 31 May 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were checked; the class is not cited, since Thanissaro carries the point it makes. The Commentary's position on who is liable is known here through Thanissaro and the Concise BMC, which agree. Ajahn Brahm's five factors are reproduced from BMC1 with an acknowledgement, and differ from it at the fifth; his retelling of the story numbers the two monks the other way round, which is not carried into the Story. The suttas on moderation in eating are given at Pc 34 and Pc 35, and are not repeated here.
