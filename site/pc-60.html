    Pc 60

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. On one occasion when the monks from the group of seventeen had not put away their requisites, the monks from the group of six hid their bowls and robes.

    The monks from the group of seventeen said to them, "Give us our bowls and robes." The monks from the group of six laughed, but the monks from the group of seventeen cried. The monks asked them, "Why are you crying?" — "'Cause the monks from the group of six have hidden our bowls and robes." The monks of few desires complained and criticised them: "How could the monks from the group of six hide the bowls and robes of other monks?" They told the Buddha, who questioned the group of six and rebuked them: "Foolish men, how could you do this? This will affect people's confidence." A monk who hides a monk's bowl, robe, sitting mat, needle case or belt, or has it hidden, even just for a laugh, commits an offence entailing confession.1

    Synopsis — The group of six hid the requisites of the group of seventeen, who were children, and laughed while they cried. The rule that follows names five things a monk works with and makes hiding any of them an offence even where nothing worse than a joke was meant.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk hides a monk's bowl, robe, sitting mat, needle case, or belt, or he has it hidden, even just for a laugh, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu hides a bhikkhu's bowl, robe, sitting-cloth, needle-case, or belt, even if just for fun, he incurs a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu hide (another) bhikkhu's bowl, robe, sitting cloth, needle box, or belt—or have it hidden—even as a joke, it is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Hiding another monk's bowl, robe, sitting cloth, needle case or waistband, even for a joke, is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should hide a bhikkhu's bowl or robe or sitting-cloth or needle case or body-belt, or have [it] hidden, even if just desiring amusement, [this is a case] involving expiation."6

    The renderings agree on the rule and differ in two small places. Brahmāli's recitation line and Ajahn Brahm's rule line give only "hides", where the Pāli has "should hide or have hidden", and the other renderings keep both; Ajahn Brahm restores the causative in his explanation.7​ 8 And nisīdana is "sitting cloth" everywhere but in Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation, which has "sitting mat": his Appendix I argues from the Suttas that the thing was an outdoor underlay, used to sit on the ground, rather than the largely ceremonial cloth of modern practice.9 Neither difference changes what the rule requires. The causative half carries a question of its own: how many offences one request makes.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A monk's bowl and robes are nearly all he owns, and this rule keeps a fellow monk's few things out of his games, for his own restraint and so that those he lives with can live at ease. The group of seventeen, who were boys, had left their bowls and robes lying about, and the group of six hid them, so that the seventeen had to ask for their own things back.10​ 11

    Thanissaro gives the offence three factors: object, one of the five named requisites belonging to a monk; effort, hiding it or having it hidden; and intention, doing it as a game.12

    Object

    The five things named are a monk's working equipment, and the Vibhaṅga defines each: a bowl of iron or of clay; a robe of one of the six kinds of cloth, not smaller than can be assigned to another; a sitting mat with a border; a needle case with or without needles; and a belt of cloth strips or of pigs' intestines.13 Hiding anything else of a monk's, such as his razor in Ajahn Brahm's example, is an offence of wrong conduct, and so is hiding anything at all that belongs to someone not fully ordained; what one perceives about the owner's status makes no difference, the pattern Thanissaro refers to Pc 42.14​ 15​ 16

    Effort

    The monk may hide the thing himself or have someone else hide it. For the second, the Vibhaṅga gives one pācittiya for the asking, however many things the other then hides.17 Thanissaro reads a pācittiya for the request and another when the other does it, and the Concise BMC follows him; one manuscript in Oldenberg's edition reads only a dukkaṭa at the same clause, the same three readings, and the same manuscript, as at Pc 56.18​ 19​ 20 On every reading, one request carries one pācittiya for the hiding. Nor does hiding need a hiding place. A monk who takes a showering monk's things from outside the door has, in Brahmāli's words, done "pretty much hiding it … You can't find it."21

    Intention

    The rule says "even just for a laugh", and the Vibhaṅga glosses it "aiming to have fun".22 The word is the laughter of Pc 52 and the play of Pc 53, as Ñāṇatusita notes, and the first non-offence clause, "if he is not aiming to have fun", is theirs in the same Pāli words.23​ 24 Taken alone, the gloss might seem to leave a monk who hid a bowl out of spite outside the rule. The Sub-commentary, which Thanissaro cites, closes the gap: the game may be friendly or malicious, and hiding a monk's things for the pleasure of annoying him, or for a friendly laugh, is the full offence all the same.25 Ajahn Brahm gives the point in the same terms.26

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The Khandhaka gives examples of what is improperly put away, as Thanissaro notes of the first: a bowl hung from a wall peg falls and breaks, and a bowl left on a bed gets crushed by someone sitting down.27​ 28 Thanissaro gives the Commentary's teaching as an admonition such as "A contemplative should not leave his requisites scattered around", and he adds a caution of his own: hiding things for that purpose can train a careless student, "but it should be used with discretion, for it can easily backfire."29

    The origin story puts the rule in two verbs: the six laughed, and the seventeen cried. A joke has two sides, and the rule will not let the laughter on one side excuse the tears on the other.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — hiding, or having hidden, any requisite of a monk not named in the rule.30
    • Dukkaṭa — hiding, or having hidden, the bowl, robe or any other requisite of someone not fully ordained, under all three perceptions.31

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he is not aiming to have fun; if he puts away what has been improperly put away; if he puts something away with the thought, "After giving a teaching, I'll give it back"; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.32

    Related Offences

    • Pc 52, 53 — tickling and playing in water: with this rule, the chapter's rules on horseplay, whose first non-offence clause, "not aiming to have fun", is this rule's in the same Pāli words.33
    • Pc 56 — lighting a fire: the same single-request clause, and the same three readings of the causative.34

    Related Suttas

    • MN 128 — the bodily acts of loving-kindness, "both openly and privately", that let fellow monks live at ease, where hiding their things for fun left the seventeen in tears.35

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; the demand, the laughing and crying, the question and the answer are verbatim, and the rebuke is given in the standing form. That the group of seventeen were children is Thanissaro's, and is carried in the Synopsis on his authority. Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual, the recitation, his Appendix I, the class transcript (session 029, 19 June 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1 and BMC2, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were checked, with the Khandhaka passage on the care of bowls and MN 128 in Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation. The class is cited for one modern case; its points on badly stored bowls and the list of requisites are carried by Thanissaro and the Vibhaṅga. The commentaries are known here only through Thanissaro.
