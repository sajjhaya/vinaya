    Pc 17

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī, in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time the monks of the group of seventeen were repairing a large dwelling nearby, intending to stay there for the rains. The monks of the group of six saw them and said, "These monks from the group of seventeen are repairing a dwelling. Let's throw them out." But some of them said, "Let's wait until they've finished repairing it."

    Soon afterwards the monks of the group of six said to those of the group of seventeen, "Leave, this dwelling is ours."

    "Shouldn't you have told us beforehand? We would've repaired another one."

    "Doesn't this dwelling belong to the Sangha?"

    "Yes, it does."

    "Well then, leave! This dwelling is ours."

    "The dwelling is large. You can stay here and so can we."

    But they said, "Leave, this dwelling is ours," and, in anger, they grabbed them by the necks and threw them out. The monks of the group of seventeen cried. When other monks asked them why, they told them what had happened.

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised them: "How could the monks from the group of six angrily throw other monks out of a dwelling belonging to the Sangha?" They told the Buddha, who questioned the monks, confirmed that it was so, and laid down the training rule.

    Synopsis — The group-of-six monks wait for the group-of-seventeen to finish repairing a large Saṅgha dwelling for the rains, then claim it as their own, and when the seventeen offer to share it they seize them by the necks and throw them out in anger; the Buddha forbids evicting a monk from a Saṅgha dwelling out of anger.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk, in anger, throws a monk out of a dwelling belonging to the Sangha, or has him thrown out, he commits an offense entailing confession."1

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu, motivated by anger, throws a bhikkhu out of a dwelling belonging to the Saṅgha, or has him thrown out, he incurs a pācittiya."2

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu, angered and displeased, evict a bhikkhu from a dwelling belonging to the Community—or have him evicted—it is to be confessed."3

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Being angry causing a monk to be turned out from a dwelling belonging to the Sangha is an offence of Pācittiya."4

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu, being resentful and displeased, should drive out a bhikkhu or have [him] driven out from a dwelling belonging to the community, [this is a case] involving expiation."5

    The five renderings differ on how hard the verb should land. Nikkaḍḍhati is literally "drag out", but Ñāṇatusita cautions that "'drag out' or 'throw out' might be too strong", and renders it "drive out".6 Brahmāli and the recitation keep the force the Story shows, "throws … out", where the group of six took the others by the neck; Thanissaro's "evict" and Ajahn Brahm's "turned out" are cooler, and fit the Commentary's point, which Thanissaro gives, that an order to leave is eviction as much as a push.7 The anger phrase, kupito anattamano, is the phrase of NP 25, and is read as it is there.8

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule keeps anger from deciding who may live in the Saṅgha's dwellings, for the concord of the Saṅgha and the ease of the monks who live in them. The monks of the group of seventeen had done the work; the group of six waited until it was finished, refused an offer to share a dwelling large enough for both, and threw them out by the neck. The Story shows how close anger lies to wanting: as Thanissaro observes, the group of six's anger "was simply a function of frustrated greed".9

    Thanissaro finds three factors in the offence: object, effort and intention.10 The intention is the anger of NP 25, where a monk takes back robe-cloth in a temper; that rule works the anger factor out in full.

    Object

    A monk is grounds for the pācittiya. A monk's belongings, an unordained person, and an unordained person's belongings are grounds for a dukkaṭa: the same act, graded down by who or what is put out.11

    Effort

    Eviction covers both taking hold of the monk and putting him out and simply ordering him to go, with the same penalty for each: so Thanissaro reports the Commentary, and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual likewise has "either physically or verbally".12​ 13 The Vibhaṅga counts the offences by the thresholds crossed: a pācittiya for putting him from the room to the entryway, another from the entryway outside, but one only if a single effort drives him through many doors.14

    Having another do it is an offence too, and here the sources part on when the pācittiya falls. Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga puts it on the asking: "if he asks another, he commits an offense entailing confession."15 Ajahn Brahm also makes having him thrown out a pācittiya, without saying when it falls.16 Thanissaro gives the bare order a dukkaṭa, with the pācittiya once the monk has actually been evicted; he judges the reading that puts the pācittiya on the order probably mistaken, finding it only in the Thai edition, and it is the reading Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga carries.17 The gap is narrow: on the first reading an order never carried out is already the full offence; on the second it stays a dukkaṭa until the eviction is done. No hinting softens the order either way.18

    As in the neighbouring rules, perception of whether the dwelling is the Saṅgha's does not mitigate, and the place-grading of Pc 15 and Pc 16 returns: eviction from around a Saṅgha dwelling, from an assembly hall, from under a roof cover, from the foot of a tree, from the open, or from another individual's dwelling is a dukkaṭa; from one's own, no offence.19​ 20

    Intention

    The factor is anger, and there is no offence where anger is not the prime impulse.21 The Vibhaṅga names those a monk may put out, with their belongings, because a reason other than temper drives the act: one who is shameless; one who is insane; one who is quarrelsome and argumentative and creates legal issues in the Saṅgha; and a pupil or student who is not conducting himself properly.22 Thanissaro reports two further points: the Commentary lets the maker of quarrels be put out of the monastery as a whole, though not the merely unconscientious, and the Sub-commentary holds that anger arising in the course of an eviction with a proper reason does not make it an offence, so long as it is not the prime impulse.23

    A monk most needs care at that last point, for anger is easily the prime impulse while a good reason stands in front of it. Brahmāli's counsel is to take a difficult monk to the Saṅgha and let the Saṅgha authorise his removal, rather than act alone, since "your own perception is blurred by your own preferences".24

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • A dukkaṭa for angrily throwing out a monk's belongings, an unordained person, or an unordained person's belongings.25
    • On Thanissaro's reading, a dukkaṭa for ordering another to do the eviction, becoming the pācittiya once the monk is put out; Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga puts the pācittiya on the asking (above, under Effort).26​ 27
    • A dukkaṭa for angrily evicting a monk, or throwing out his belongings, from around a Saṅgha dwelling, an assembly hall, a roof cover, the foot of a tree, the open, or another individual's dwelling.28

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence in throwing out, or having thrown out, one who is shameless, one who is insane, one who is quarrelsome and creates legal issues in the Saṅgha, or a pupil or student who is not conducting himself properly, or the belongings of any of them; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.29

    Related Offences

    • NP 25 — there a monk who has given robe-cloth takes it back angry and displeased (kupito anattamano); here the same impulse, in the same words, falls on a fellow monk's lodging. The anger factor is worked out there.
    • Pc 16 — the companion rule: crowding a monk out there, throwing him out here, both grown from the group of six's designs on a Saṅgha dwelling.
    • Pc 74, Pc 75 — striking a monk and raising a hand against him, the same impulse turned to a blow or a threat; Ñāṇatusita ties the phrase in NP 25, this rule and Pc 74 together.
    • Pc 14, Pc 15 — the furnishings pair that opens this run of lodging rules, whose place-graded dukkaṭas this rule shares.

    Related Suttas

    • MN 128 — monks at Kosambī "stabbing each other with verbal daggers", and the verse that hatred is never allayed by hate, "It is allayed by non-hatred": the anger this rule keeps out of the Saṅgha's dwellings.30

    A note on sourcing

    The Story, the rule, the definitions of anger and of throwing out, the permutations and the non-offence clauses are from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation, and the recitation line from his Pātimokkha recitation translation. The Manual confirms that eviction may be physical or verbal. Thanissaro's BMC1 supplies the three factors, the object grading, the Commentary's reading of eviction and of the maker of quarrels and the Sub-commentary's of incidental anger, all cited through him, his reading of the order to evict, and the observation that the anger of the Story was frustrated greed. Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes confirms the rule. The Concise BMC was checked and condenses BMC1 without adding to it. Ñāṇatusita gives the rule translation and the grammar of nikkaḍḍhati. The class transcript (session 023, 10 May 2012) gives Brahmāli's counsel on bringing a difficult monk to the Saṅgha. MN 128 is quoted in Bodhi's translation.
