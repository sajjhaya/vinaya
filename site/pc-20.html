    Pc 20

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Āḷavī at the Aggāḷava Shrine. At that time the monks there were doing building work. They poured water that they knew contained living beings onto grass and clay, and they had others do the same.

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised them: "How can the monks at Āḷavī pour water that they know contains living beings onto grass and clay, and have others do the same?" After rebuking those monks in many ways, they told the Buddha. He had the Saṅgha gathered, questioned the monks, confirmed that it was so, rebuked them, and laid down the training rule.1

    Synopsis — The monks of Āḷavī, mixing mortar for their building work, use water they know has living creatures in it and have others do the same; the Buddha forbids pouring such water onto grass or clay.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk pours water that he knows contains living beings onto grass or clay, or has it poured, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu pours water onto the ground, or has it poured, when he knows it contains living beings that will die, he incurs a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu knowingly pour water containing living beings — or have it poured — on grass or on clay, it is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Knowing that some water contains living beings, discharging it onto the ground or mixing it with other substances, causing their death is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should knowingly pour out, or should have [someone else] pour out, water containing living beings on grass or clay, [this is a case] involving expiation."6

    The five renderings differ in how much of the Vibhaṅga's analysis they fold into the sentence. Thanissaro and the full translation keep "onto grass or clay"; Brahmāli's recitation says "the ground". The recitation also adds "that will die" to what the monk knows, which matches the gloss Thanissaro reports from the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī, that knowing includes knowing they will die from the pouring.7​ 8 Ajahn Brahm goes further and writes death into the rule as a result, "causing their death"; whether death is needed for the offence is taken up under Effort.9

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    Small creatures live in water, and this rule asks a monk not to kill them by careless use of it, for his own harmlessness and for the confidence of those who watch how he lives. The monks of Āḷavī were building again, and mixing their mortar with water they knew had living things in it.10 The rule is built on that knowing: it does not ask a monk to see what cannot be seen, only to act on what he knows.

    The rule is narrow, as Ajahn Brahm says, covering circumstances like those of the Story, and Pc 62, on knowingly using such water, extends it.11 Thanissaro gives this rule four factors, object, perception, effort and intention, and Pc 62 three, differing only in its effort and in having no factor of intention.12​ 13 The object, the perception and the checking of water are therefore set out here, and Pc 62 points back to them.

    Object

    The object is water containing living beings that can be seen. Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual takes the limit from the commentarial word "having seen", and gives the reason: seeing was the only way of knowing that beings were there in the Buddha's day.14 Mosquito larvae are included, as Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm both say; beings too small to see are not.15​ 16

    Perception

    He knows, by himself or because others have told him.17 Thanissaro reports the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī taking knowing by himself as having seen or heard that the beings are there, and adds that knowing includes knowing they will die from the pouring.18 Here, unlike the pattern Thanissaro sets out at Pc 4, perception is the whole of the matter. If the water has beings in it and he knows it, the offence is full; if he is unsure, it is wrong conduct; if he does not know, there is no offence. If it has none and he thinks it has, or is unsure, it is wrong conduct.19

    Effort

    The effort is to pour the water, or have it poured. Thanissaro's examples are emptying old water from a flower vase onto the ground and pouring water into a basin of cement mix.20​ 21 Giving the order is enough, and there is no allowance for the allowable expression, kappiya-vohāra, that Pc 10 and Pc 11 permit.22

    How many offences does one order bring? The Vibhaṅga answers: "If he only asks once, then even if the other pours a lot, he commits one offense entailing confession", and Brahmāli's endnote gives the same principle from the Commentary on Pc 10.23​ 24 The same clause, with its own verb, recurs at Pc 10, Pc 17, Pc 26, Pc 56 and Pc 60. Thanissaro counts two here, a pācittiya for the order and another when it is obeyed, and the Concise BMC follows him; but under NP 26 he himself names Pc 10, Pc 20 and Pc 56 among the rules "where a single request carries only one offense even if the person requested does the action many times."25​ 26 On the Vibhaṅga's words and on Thanissaro's own general statement, one order that is obeyed is one offence.

    A second reading, which Thanissaro reports from the Commentary, turns the rule round, forbidding a monk to pour grass or clay, or anything lethal, into water with beings in it. Thanissaro objects that this defies the word order, but allows that the Great Standards could bring the case under the rule, and in his summary counts it a pācittiya.27 Whether the beings die does not matter: Thanissaro says so, the permutations grade the offence without mentioning death, and the Manual lists no result factor; Ajahn Brahm's "causing their death" gives no argument against them.28​ 29​ 30 The strainer belongs to Pc 62, and watering plants with water that holds no beings, by Thanissaro's pointer, to the Commentary on Sg 13.31

    Intention

    The intention is simply to pour.32 Knocking the water over is no offence, nor pouring it out unmindfully, since the Vibhaṅga exempts the unintentional, the unmindful and the one who does not know.33 A further motive does not excuse it: a monk who sees the insects and pours the water on a burning log to put out the fire offends all the same. The Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī's claim that the rule applies only where there is no intent to kill, apparently to keep it clear of Pc 61, Thanissaro rejects: the Vibhaṅga allows nothing of the kind, and where the creatures survive it would give the monk who meant to kill the lighter penalty.34

    Because the whole rule rests on what the monk knows, the habit it asks of him is named by Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm alike: a monk "should always check water before using it". For a large tank, Ajahn Brahm describes the practice of some monasteries, two or three cupfuls drawn as samples, and if they hold nothing living, the rest may be used.35​ 36

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • A dukkaṭa where the water contains living beings and the monk is unsure of it, and he pours it or has it poured.37
    • A dukkaṭa where the water contains none and he takes it to contain them, or is unsure.38

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if it is unintentional; if he is not mindful; if he does not know; if he is insane; if he is the first offender.39 Thanissaro's examples: not knowing the water has beings in it; seeing tadpoles in a kettle already heating and dumping it out in a knee-jerk reaction so that they will not be boiled; knocking over a goldfish bowl.40

    Related Offences

    • Pc 62 — knowingly using water containing living beings; the extension of this rule, with three factors against its four, and the home of the water strainer.
    • Pc 61 — killing an animal; the commentarial attempt to keep the two rules apart is discussed under Intention.
    • Pc 11 — destroying vegetation; it opens with the same monks of Āḷavī at their building work.
    • Sg 13 — whose Commentary, Thanissaro notes, carries the treatment of watering plants.

    Related Suttas

    • Snp 1.8 — goodwill toward "whatever living beings there are", "fine, or gross" alike: the small life in water that the rule protects.41
    • Dhp 129 — "Everyone dreads violence, / everyone fears death; / recognizing that you are similar, / do not harm or cause others to harm", the rule's two acts, pouring and having it poured, in a verse.42

    A note on sourcing

    The Story, the rule, the definitions, the single-request clause, the permutations and the non-offence clauses are from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation, with his endnote on the clause, and the recitation line from his Pātimokkha recitation translation. The Manual gives the visible-beings limit. Thanissaro's BMC1 supplies the four factors, the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī's points on knowing and its claim about intent, the Commentary's second reading, all cited through him, his examples, the count of offences at this rule and his statement of the single-request principle at NP 26, and the duty to check water. Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes gives the rule's narrowness, the larvae, through the Vinaya-mukha, and the sampling method. The Concise BMC was checked and follows BMC1, including its count of two. Ñāṇatusita gives the rule translation. The class transcript (session 023, 10 May 2012) was read for its questions, on what counts as a living being, how to check a tank and what to do with such water; the written sources answer the first two, and the class is not cited. Snp 1.8 is quoted in Bodhi's translation and Dhp 129 in Suddhāso's.
