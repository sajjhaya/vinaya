    Pc 6

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī, in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time Venerable Anuruddha, walking through the Kosalan country on his way to Sāvatthī, arrived one evening at a certain village, where a woman kept a guesthouse. Anuruddha went to her and said, "If it is not inconvenient for you, I would like to stay in your guesthouse for one night." She agreed. Other travellers arrived and asked the same; she told them a monastic was already there, and that they might stay if he agreed. They asked Anuruddha, and he consented.

    As soon as she had seen Anuruddha, the woman had fallen in love with him. She went to him and said, "Sir, you will not be comfortable surrounded by these people. Why do I not prepare a bed for you in the main house?" Anuruddha consented by remaining silent. After preparing the bed, she put on jewellery and perfume, went to him, and said, "You are attractive, sir, and so am I. Why not take me as your wife?" Anuruddha remained silent. She said it a second time, and got no response; and a third time, offering all her property with herself, and again he was silent. She then threw off her wrap and walked back and forth before him, stood, sat, and lay down. But Anuruddha controlled his senses and neither looked at her nor spoke to her.

    Then the woman said, "It is astonishing and amazing. Many people pay a hundred or a thousand coins to be with me. But this monastic does not want me and all this property, even when I beg him." She dressed, bowed with her head at his feet, and said, "Sir, I have made a mistake. I have been foolish, confused, and unskilful. Please forgive me, so that I may restrain myself in the future."

    "You have certainly made a mistake. But since you acknowledge it and make proper amends, I forgive you. For this is called growth in the training of the noble ones: acknowledging a mistake, making proper amends, and undertaking restraint for the future."

    The following morning she served Anuruddha fine foods with her own hands, and when he had eaten he instructed, inspired, and gladdened her with a teaching. She said, "Wonderful, sir, wonderful! Just as one might set upright what is overturned, reveal what is hidden, show the way to one who is lost, or bring a lamp into the darkness so that those with eyes might see—just so have you made the Teaching clear in many ways. I go for refuge to the Buddha, the Teaching, and the Saṅgha of monks. Please accept me as a lay follower gone for refuge for life."

    Later, having reached Sāvatthī, Anuruddha told the monks what had happened. The monks of few desires complained and criticised him: "How can Venerable Anuruddha lie down in the same sleeping place as a woman?" After rebuking him, they told the Buddha, who questioned Anuruddha, rebuked him, and laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — Anuruddha, given a bed inside a woman's house, holds unmoved through her attempt to seduce him — and though she is converted by his composure, the rule against a monk lying down in the same sleeping place as a woman follows.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk lies down in the same sleeping place as a woman, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu and a woman lie down together in the same room, he incurs a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu lie down together (in the same dwelling) with a woman, it is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Lying down at the same time and place, with a woman is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should make use of a sleeping place together with a woman, [this is a case] involving expiation."6

    This is the second rule on lying down together, and its translation ground is Pc 5's: the same act, and the same choice between "room", "dwelling" and "place" for the space that counts, discussed there. The new word is the object, mātugāma, "a woman".

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule protects a monk from the situations that could draw him toward the gravest offences, and his good name from the suspicion of those who see him. The Story shows what such a situation is. Anuruddha took a bed inside the house, the woman tried three times to make him her husband and then lay down before him, and he kept his senses guarded, neither looking at her nor speaking; she ended by going for refuge.7 Thanissaro draws the lesson plainly: Anuruddha was advanced enough in the practice to come through with his mindfulness intact, but "many a lesser bhikkhu … would have succumbed right from the woman's first request, and so the Buddha formulated this rule for his protection."8

    The rule borrows nearly all its machinery from Pc 5: the sleeping place is defined the same way, lying down together is counted the same way, and perception follows the pattern set out at Pc 4. Thanissaro reduces the difference to two points, the object and the absence of any count of nights.9

    Object

    A woman is a human female, "even a girl born on that very day, let alone an older one", related to the monk or not.10​ 11 Lying down with a female spirit, ghost or animal, or with a paṇḍaka, is a dukkaṭa; the term paṇḍaka is left untranslated, as Brahmāli leaves it, and is treated at Sg 2.12 Neither perception nor intention excuses: a monk offends whether or not he knows she is there, and the presence of another man does not lift the offence.13

    Effort

    Here the three-night allowance falls away. The offence is complete the moment he is lying down in the sleeping place while a woman lies there, and Thanissaro quotes the Commentary putting the contrast in a sentence: "In the preceding rule, the offense is on the fourth day. Here it is right from the first day."14 The Vibhaṅga frames the act "when the sun has set", and whether that confines the rule to the night is disputed.15

    Disputed — whether the rule applies only after sundown, or at any hour.

    Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual makes the window an element of the offence, between sunset and dawn, and grounds it on Pc 5, where the time after dawn is not part of the night.16 Thanissaro reads the phrase as marking only that no dawn need pass: the non-offence clauses give no exemption for the daytime, and a 24-hour "night" began at sundown, so the offence falls at any hour.17 The Concise BMC argues the other way at length, among its points that a non-offence clause need not exempt the opposite of what the word-analysis already states, and that Anuruddha's was a night's lodging.18 Ajahn Brahm reads the Vibhaṅga as confining the rule to the night and calls that limit "unreasonable".19 No text decides it, and a monk who keeps the rule at every hour is safe on every reading.

    Does a separate room spare him? That depends on how wide the sleeping place is, the question left open at Pc 5, which matters more here. Taking the dwelling as at Pc 5, Thanissaro notes, a monk sleeping in his mother's house offends even in a separate room with another man present.20 On Brahmāli's narrower reading, a room, he may stay in his parents' house in a room of his own; but Brahmāli is quick to add that a monk and a woman alone in a house look improper whatever the rooms, and runs into Pc 44 and Pc 45 besides.21 Ajahn Brahm, reading the rule by its purpose, would count any situation that carries the chance of a serious offence or the suspicion of one, a sleeping carriage on a train or lying in the open on the beach, as the same sleeping place.22 Thanissaro puts the point in one sentence: "Like Caesar's wife, a bhikkhu must not only be pure, he must look pure if he is to maintain his reputation."23

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • A dukkaṭa for lying down in the same sleeping place as a female spirit, ghost or animal, or a paṇḍaka.24
    • A dukkaṭa where the sleeping place is half-roofed and half-walled.25
    • A dukkaṭa where it is not a woman but the monk perceives one, or is unsure.26

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if the place is fully roofed but not walled, fully walled but not roofed, mostly not roofed or mostly not walled; if one sits while the other lies, or both sit; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.27 There is no three-night allowance here.

    Related Offences

    • Pc 5 — the anchor of the pair, carrying the sleeping place, the lying down and the "single dwelling" dispute that this rule borrows.
    • Pc 44, 45 — sitting or lying down alone with a woman in a private place; this rule reaches what those do not, separate rooms of one dwelling and the presence of another man.28
    • Pj 1, Sg 2 — the offences the situations covered here could lead to.29
    • Pc 4 — the source of the perception pattern this rule follows.
