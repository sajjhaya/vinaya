    Pācittiya

    The ninety-two pācittiya rules make up the largest class in the Pātimokkha, and its machinery is simple. A bhikkhu who breaks one of them does one thing to be clear of it: he confesses. There is no article to give up, as there is under the nissaggiya pācittiyas, and no penance or probation, as there is under the saṅghādisesas; in Brahmāli's words, "there is no penalty for committing these offenses apart from the confession itself."1 The name is the second half of the nissaggiya pācittiya's, and its uncertain etymology is discussed in the Nissaggiya Pācittiya overview.

    The confession is laid down in the Khandhaka on the observance day, and it is short. The bhikkhu goes to a single fellow monk, arranges his upper robe over one shoulder, squats on his heels, raises his joined palms, and says, "I've committed such-and-such an offense. I confess it." The other asks, "Do you recognize it?" — "Yes, I recognize it." — "You should restrain yourself in the future."2 Two things are asked of the one confessing, then: that he see the offence as an offence, and that he undertake restraint from here on.3

    Easy to clear is not the same as light to carry. In the Aṅguttara Nikāya the Buddha sets the four classes of offence side by side, each pictured as a man publicly owning a deed and its punishment. The pārājika is a thief led out through the south gate to be beheaded. The pācittiya is a man who wraps himself in a black cloth, loosens his hair, puts a sack of ashes on his shoulder, and tells a crowd that he has done "an evil deed, reprehensible, punishable by a sack of ashes", and the Commentary, as Bhikkhu Bodhi reports it, explains that the ashes are for striking him on the head.4 It is a lesser figure than the executioner's, and still not a comfortable one. The sutta uses the picture to set up a "keen perception of peril", so that one who has never committed such an offence will not, and one who has will make amends.

    What makes the class hard to hold in the mind is its range. It opens with the deliberate lie (Pc 1), and it contains the rules against drinking alcohol (Pc 51), killing an animal (Pc 61) and eating after midday (Pc 37), each of which Brahmāli's introduction calls fundamental to the monastic life or to Buddhist morality, alongside a needle case of bone or ivory (Pc 86) and the size of a sitting mat (Pc 89).5 The Vibhaṅga has its own word for the whole class. When the ninety-two are finished it says, "The section on minor rules has been completed," khuddakaṁ samattaṁ.6 What that word may tell us about the "lesser and minor rules" of the Buddha's last days is discussed at Pc 72. Here "minor" names a place in the order of the classes, not a licence. A monk who drinks, or who deliberately kills a mosquito, has broken a minor rule.

    The ninety-two are divided into nine chapters (vagga) of ten rules each, except the eighth, which has twelve. The Vibhaṅga closes each chapter with its name and number, and the English names are Brahmāli's:7

    1. Musāvāda-vagga, on lying: Pc 1–10
    2. Bhūtagāma-vagga, on plants: Pc 11–20
    3. Ovāda-vagga, on the instruction: Pc 21–30
    4. Bhojana-vagga, on eating: Pc 31–40
    5. Acelaka-vagga, on naked ascetics: Pc 41–50
    6. Surāpāna-vagga, on drinking alcohol: Pc 51–60
    7. Sappāṇaka-vagga, on containing living beings: Pc 61–70
    8. Sahadhammika-vagga, on legitimate correction: Pc 71–82
    9. Ratana-vagga, on precious things: Pc 83–92

    A chapter's name is a handle for finding one's place, not a promise about everything in it. Some chapters do keep to one subject: the third concerns the relationship between monks and nuns, and the fourth, in Brahmāli's words, "is all about food." Others range more widely. The second takes its name from plants, yet its remaining rules, as he notes, "mostly concern proper conduct in relation to dwellings and furniture."8

    The class ends as it would be heard at the fortnightly recitation. When the ninety-two have been recited, the reciting monk turns to the assembly: "Venerables, the ninety-two rules on confession have been recited. In regard to this I ask you, 'Are you pure in this?'" He asks a second time, and a third, and then says: "You are pure in this and therefore silent. I will remember it thus."9 The silence is an answer, and it is a true answer only if the confessions have already been made. That is the situation the confession formula was laid down for: a monk who found, on the observance day itself, that he had an offence he had not yet confessed.10 The rules follow individually below. They ask a great deal, but the way back from any one of them is a short walk to a fellow monk, and it is that walk, made in time, that lets a monk keep silent in the assembly and have the silence mean something.
