    NP 4

    Story (Vatthu)

    At Sāvatthī, Venerable Udāyī's former wife had gone forth as a nun. She often visited him, and he often visited her, and he shared his meals with her.

    One morning Udāyī robed up, took his bowl and robe, and went to her. He uncovered his genitals in front of her and sat down. She uncovered her genitals in front of him and sat down. Staring at her with lust, he emitted semen. He said to her, "Sister, get some water. I'll wash the robe."

    "Give it to me, venerable, I'll wash it."

    She took some of the semen in her mouth and inserted some into her vagina, and by that she became pregnant. The nuns said, "This nun doesn't abstain from sex. She's pregnant." She said, "Venerables, I do abstain from sex," and she told them what had happened.

    The nuns complained and criticised Udāyī: "How could Venerable Udāyī get a nun to wash a used robe?" They told the monks, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised him in the same words, and told the Buddha. He had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned Udāyī: "Is it true, Udāyī, that you did this?" — "It's true, sir." — "Is she a relative of yours?" — "No." — "Foolish man, a man and a woman who are unrelated don't know what's appropriate and inappropriate, what's inspiring and uninspiring, in dealing with each other. And still you did this."1

    Synopsis — Udāyī's former wife, now a nun, washes a robe he has soiled; the resulting scandal prompts the Buddha to forbid a monk from having an unrelated nun wash, dye, or beat a used robe.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk has an unrelated nun wash, dye, or beat a used robe, he commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu has an unrelated bhikkhunī wash or dye a used robe, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu have a used robe washed, dyed, or beaten by a bhikkhunī unrelated to him, it is to be forfeited and confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Having a used robe washed, beaten or dyed by a bhikkhunī who is not a relation is an offence of nissaggiya pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should have a used robe [-cloth] washed, dyed, or beaten by an unrelated bhikkhunī, [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture."6

    The renderings agree, and one compresses. The recitation names only washing and dyeing and drops beating, which the full rule, the definitions and every other rendering keep as the third action; a monk who has a nun beat his robe commits the full offence all the same. The Pāli verbs are causative, "should have washed", and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual takes the causing to be done by the asking alone, a reading discussed below, under Effort.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule asks of a monk restraint with a nun who is not his relative: he is not to make her his laundress. The Buddha's reason is in the Story: "a man and a woman who are unrelated don't know what's appropriate and inappropriate, what's inspiring and uninspiring, in dealing with each other", and Udāyī's familiarity with his former wife had gone as far as the Story tells.7 The rule asks a monk to do his own washing, or to have it done by someone the rule does not reach.

    This is the first rule of the Pātimokkha that concerns nuns, and NP 5 follows with the same definitions. Thanissaro gives three factors, object, effort and result; the Manual lists eight, which fall under the same three, and I keep to Thanissaro's.8​ 9

    Object

    A used robe of one's own. The Vibhaṅga defines it as "a sarong or an upper robe, even worn once", and the Commentary extends "used" to a robe rolled up as a pillow or draped over the shoulder or head.10​ 11 Another monk's robe is wrong conduct only, which is why the full offence is read as reaching one's own robe; a sitting mat or sheet is wrong conduct; any other requisite is no offence.12​ 13 What counts as a robe is disputed.

    Disputed — whether the used robe is one of the three robes only, or any robe a monk can wear.

    Thanissaro gives the Commentary's reading, any finished robe dyed and marked for wearing, determined as one of the three or not, so that a spare robe determined as requisite cloth falls under the rule; the Manual gives the factor as "any wearable robe" and argues it from the non-offence clause, where only "other requisites" are exempt.14​ 15 Ajahn Brahm holds the rule to the three: the used robe is "any one of that monk's ticīvara" worn at least once, and for all other cloths there is no offence.16 The Vibhaṅga's own definition, a sarong or an upper robe, settles neither reading. The wider reading is the safer to keep, since a monk who keeps it breaks neither; but the narrower reading is genuinely held, and safety does not make the wider one right.

    Effort

    Telling an unrelated nun to wash, dye or beat the robe. Any one of the three is enough.17

    Unrelated. The Vibhaṅga counts as related "a descendant of one's male ancestors going back eight generations, either on the mother's side or on the father's side"; Brahmāli's endnote counts the fourteen ancestors this gives, seven on each side, of whom anyone descended is a relative.18 The Manual, counting seven generations, puts the limit at a sixth cousin, where the endnote's reckoning would reach one degree further; in-laws are not included.19 Ajahn Brahm finds the Commentary's reckoning "rather unlikely", noting that the same phrase describes a brahmin's lineage, "pure back through seven generations", and that it certainly does not take in a former wife.20 Since no one now knows kin so distant, the sources agree on the practice: treat as related only a blood relative one knows to be one, and, Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm add, one with whom ties of kinship are actually felt.21​ 22​ 23 And so, as a former wife, the woman of the Story was unrelated in the rule's sense.

    A nun. One fully ordained by both Saṅghas. One ordained by the nuns' Saṅgha only is wrong conduct, and a trainee or a novice nun is no offence; Ajahn Brahm adds that neither is any other woman staying in the monastery, such as a mae chee.24​ 25​ 26​ 27

    Telling. The verbs are causative, and since the non-offence clause exempts a nun who washes unasked, Brahmāli takes the causing to be fulfilled simply by asking; the Commentary counts a gesture as telling, so that a monk who throws his robe down beside a nun at her washing has told her.28​ 29 A nun who insists on washing his robe without being asked brings no offence; it is the asking the rule forbids.30 To beat a robe was to fold it wet and strike it against a stone to drive the dirt out, as is still done at Indian bathing places.31

    Perception does not excuse. If the nun is unrelated, the offence is full whether he takes her to be unrelated, is unsure, or thinks her related; if she is related and he takes her for unrelated, or is unsure, it is wrong conduct.32​ 33

    Result

    The nun does what she was asked. The robe is to be relinquished when it has been washed, or dyed, or when she has struck it once with her hand or an implement.34 If he had her do two of the three, the Vibhaṅga adds one offence of wrong conduct to the full offence, and two for all three.35 The relinquishment is NP 1's, and the returned robe counts as extra cloth there.36 The Commentary's case of a trainee who takes the robe, is ordained while holding it and then washes it, which it judges the full offence, shows that what counts is her standing when she does the work.37

    The rule itself is narrow: it reaches only a fully ordained nun, only a used robe, and only what he asks. The Buddha's reason in the Story is wider, and a monk who keeps to the letter while leaning on a nun's service has kept the rule and missed its point.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — in the telling, before the nun has done anything; and for each of a second and third action beyond the first.38​ 39
    • Dukkaṭa — for a robe belonging to someone else, for a sitting mat or a sheet, and for a nun ordained on one side only.40
    • Dukkaṭa — if she is related and he takes her for unrelated, or is unsure.41
    • Dukkaṭa — by the Commentary, for each effort she makes toward the task; Thanissaro finds no basis for it in the Vibhaṅga.42

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if a related nun does the washing and an unrelated nun helps her; if a nun washes it without being asked; if the robe is unused; if it is any requisite apart from a robe; if it is a trainee nun or a novice nun; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.43

    Related Offences

    • NP 5 — accepting robe-cloth from an unrelated nun, with this rule's definitions.
    • NP 17 — having an unrelated nun wash, dye or card wool, on the same frame.
    • Pc 25, 26 — giving robe-cloth to an unrelated nun, and sewing a robe for one; the wider family of rules on cloth between monks and nuns.

    Related Suttas

    • SN 35.127 — the instruction Piṇḍola Bhāradvāja gives King Udena as the Buddha's, that monks regard women of a mother's age as mothers, of a sister's as sisters, of a daughter's as daughters; the thought a monk sets up toward a woman leaves no room for the familiarity that ran between Udāyī and his former wife.44
    • AN 4.159 — Ānanda's visit to a nun who sent word that she was sick, and his teaching to her, "This body has originated from sexual intercourse, but in regard to sexual intercourse the Blessed One has declared the demolition of the bridge", after which she confessed her behaviour as a transgression: the footing on which a monk and a nun meet, which the rule keeps.45
