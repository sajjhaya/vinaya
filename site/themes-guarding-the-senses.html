    Guarding the Senses

    King Udena once asked Piṇḍola Bhāradvāja how the young monks, "lads with black hair, endowed with the blessing of youth", who had "not dallied with sensual pleasures", kept the celibate life pure all their lives. Piṇḍola answered three times, each time with something the Buddha had taught. The first was to regard women old enough to be one's mother as a mother, those of an age to be one's sisters as sisters, and those young enough as daughters. The king was not satisfied: "The mind is wanton", and lust arises even towards these. The second was to review the body, from the soles of the feet up, as full of impurities. That, said the king, is easy for monks developed in body, virtue, mind and wisdom and hard for the rest: "Sometimes, though one thinks, 'I will attend to the body as foul,' one beholds it as beautiful." The third was to "dwell guarding the doors of the sense faculties", grasping neither the signs nor the features of what is seen, heard, smelt, tasted, touched or thought. This the king accepted, from his own experience: when he entered his harem "unguarded in body, speech, and mind", lust assailed him forcefully; when he entered it guarded, it did not.1

    The rules on sexual conduct are laid out at a series of distances from the act. Pj 1 forbids intercourse itself; Sg 1 the intentional emission of semen; Sg 2 bodily contact with a woman, with a lustful mind; Sg 3 lewd words to her; Sg 4 asking her for sex as a service. The two Aniyata rules deal with a monk seen sitting with a woman in private, and Pc 30, Pc 44 and Pc 45 forbid such a seat outright, with a nun and with a woman. Udāyī stands at the origin of most of them, and his story is told in Characters of the Vinaya. Each rule draws its line at a deed of body or speech that others can see and the Saṅgha can judge.

    Each therefore has an edge inside which a monk incurs no offence and has still not kept the training. A monk who emits while thinking sensual thoughts, making no physical effort, has no offence under Sg 1, whether or not he wished for it; Thanissaro adds the Commentary's warning that one should not let oneself be overcome by such thoughts.2 A monk who stands still while a woman embraces him has no offence under Sg 2, and Ajahn Brahm gives the case in full: even if he "enjoyed every minute of the contact", so long as he made no effort to encourage, assist or prolong it.3

    At both edges the suttas take up what the rule leaves. The Buddha's wet, sappy wood, which Thanissaro brings to this rule, will not catch fire however long the fire-stick is worked, and an ascetic who lives "bodily withdrawn from sensual pleasures" while the desire and fever for them are not abandoned within is "incapable of knowledge and vision and supreme enlightenment".4 An ascetic who never has intercourse, but "consents to being rubbed, massaged, bathed, and kneaded" by women, has committed "a breach, flaw, blemish, and blotch of the celibate life".5

    The suttas also say where the guard is kept, in images of animals. The tortoise draws its four legs and its neck into its shell, and the jackal waiting for one of them to come out leaves "finding no opening"; so, the Buddha tells the monks, "live with the doors of the senses guarded".6 The quail seized by the hawk, and the monkey caught by the hunter's pitch, had strayed "outside one's own resort into the domain of others", and for a monk the domain of others "is the five cords of sensual pleasure", his own resort "the four establishments of mindfulness".7​ 8 It was into the domain of others that Udāyī sent Seyyasaka for a cure, and Sg 1 is the rule that followed.

    Read one at a time, each of these rules shows a line and the offence for crossing it. Read in order, they suggest a direction: taken broadly, each step away from the act brings the rule closer to the senses, and at the last step, a seat in private, there is nothing left for a rule to forbid but the occasion itself. The suttas begin where the rules end, at the doors of the senses, and it seems reasonable to read the two as one teaching: the rules mark how far a monk may stray before the Saṅgha must act, and the suttas name the place where he stays. Udena knew the difference from his own harem. What kept him was the guard he entered with.
