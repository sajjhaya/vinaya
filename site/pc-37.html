    Pc 37

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Rājagaha in the Bamboo Grove. There was a hilltop fair in Rājagaha, which the monks from the group of seventeen went to see. When people saw the monks, they bathed them, anointed them, fed them cooked food, and gave them fresh food. They took that food and brought it back to the monastery. And they said to the monks from the group of six, "Help yourselves!"1

    "But where did you get this food?" And they told them what had happened.

    "So, do you eat at the wrong time?" — "Yes."2

    The monks from the group of six complained and criticised them, "How can those monks from the group of seventeen eat at the wrong time?" They told the monks, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised them in the same terms. The Buddha questioned the monks, established that it was true, rebuked them, and stated the rule.3

    Synopsis — The seventeen go to a fair, are washed and fed and sent home with the leftovers, and offer a share to the six — who ask where it came from, establish by two questions that the seventeen have been eating in the afternoon, and then denounce them; the rule against eating between noon and dawn is the one the Pātimokkha's habitual offenders reported.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk eats fresh or cooked food at the wrong time, he commits an offense entailing confession."4

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu eats between noon and dawn, he incurs a pācittiya."5

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu chew or consume staple or non-staple food at the wrong time, it is to be confessed."6

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Eating food in the period after noon until the next dawn is an offence of Pācittiya."7

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should chew uncooked food or eat cooked food at the wrong time, [this is a case] involving expiation."8

    The renderings agree, and the food-words are Pc 35's, their naming Pc 29's. Where they differ is in what they put into the line. The Vibhaṅga defines the wrong time as "when the middle of the day has passed, until dawn", and Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga keeps "the middle of the day" where the recitation puts "noon". Which noon is the one qualification on which a monk's practice turns: Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm both take it as the sun at its zenith, and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual puts "solar noon" into its definition of the wrong time.9​ 10​ 11​ 12​ 13 For the second boundary Thanissaro writes "dawnrise" and keeps "dawn" for a different Pāli word; the difference is his apparatus, not a different rule.14 Both boundaries belong to Circumstance.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    To stop eating at noon is a mark of restraint that people have long looked for in a renunciant, and this rule keeps a monk within it, for his own practice and for the confidence of those who judge ascetics by it. Among the things the Dīgha Nikāya says an ordinary person would praise in the Buddha is that "he eats once a day and not at night, refraining from eating at improper times".15 The monks of the origin story went to a hilltop fair, were bathed, anointed and fed, and carried the food home to share; and the report that brought the rule came, for once, from the group of six, which Ajahn Brahm does not let pass: "the group of six monks (of all people!)".16​ 17

    This is the rule every reader already knows, which is the reason to be careful with it. Its statement is simple and its edges are not: what counts as noon, what counts as dawn, and what counts as eating are three separate questions. Thanissaro treats the rule under the object, the wrong time and the effort of eating, and I keep his order.18​ 19

    Object

    The object is fresh or cooked food, as Pc 35 defines it: everything edible except the tonics and, as Thanissaro adds, water.20​ 21

    Circumstance

    The wrong time runs from noon to dawn, and noon means the sun at its zenith, not twelve o'clock. Thanissaro draws the consequence: noon is reckoned by local time and not by standard or daylight-saving time, so a monk offered food on an aeroplane should check where the sun is.22 Brahmāli met the problem on the ground. Many monks follow twelve o'clock, he observed, presumably because it is easier to know than solar noon, and that can put them on the wrong side of the line: on his first visit to India solar noon fell at half past eleven, and everything had been arranged too late. His monastery keeps a chart of solar noon through the year, which in Perth falls as late as twenty past twelve in January.23 The point cuts both ways. A monk who stops at twelve every day of the year has sometimes kept a stricter rule than the one he undertook. One question has a settled answer: a meal begun before noon may not be finished after it, and Thanissaro reports the Commentary saying so explicitly.24 Within the right time the rule sets no number of meals. Brahmāli held that nothing in the Vinaya requires one meal a day, since the Buddha allowed rice porridge before the main meal, and that the one-mealer the suttas praise is an ideal, "not a binding ideal", though he thought the rules themselves point to an original single meal, the exceptions coming later.25

    Dawn is harder. The Vibhaṅga does not define it, and the Commentary and Sub-commentary, Thanissaro reports, pass over it as obvious. A later Vinaya manual divides the lightening sky into four stages, two reddenings and two whitenings before sunrise, and communities differ over which of the four ends the night.26​ 27 Thanissaro argues for the last whitening, which in modern terms is the onset of civil twilight, from the purpose of the food rules, taken up at the end of this Explanation: dawnrise "can be no earlier than the point recognized by the Vinayālaṅkāra." He adds the caveat that matters away from the equator: the interval from civil twilight to sunrise varies with the season, "with the variations most extreme at higher latitudes."28 Ven. Ñāṇadassana, as Ajahn Brahm's editor reports him, also reaches civil twilight, though he takes it to be the second reddening, and the Burmese practice Ajahn Brahm reports takes that stage; by the manual's own reckoning the second reddening and the last whitening stand twenty-four minutes apart.29 Where a Community draws its line therefore decides how early breakfast may be. The practice on the ground is looser. At Brahmāli's monastery dawn has passed once light from the sun can clearly be seen in the sky, and he left open whether to move to sunrise instead, "possibly we should"; the finer detail he thought "just a matter of definition".30 The test from Thailand, the lines of the palm seen by natural light at arm's length, Thanissaro takes as marking dawnrise itself, and Ajahn Brahm only as the point by which dawn has certainly passed.31

    Perception does not mitigate. Whether the monk perceives the time as wrong, is unsure, or believes it right, eating at the wrong time is the full offence, on the pattern of Pc 4.32

    Effort

    The effort is eating, and the sources do not agree on what eating is.

    Disputed — whether eating is swallowing, putting food into the mouth, or both.

    Two cases turn on it: food stuck in the teeth since the morning and swallowed after noon, and food put into the mouth after noon and spat out. Thanissaro reports the Commentary as generally defining eating as the food going down the throat, and Brahmāli notes that the Vibhaṅga everywhere defines it as swallowing.33​ 34 Thanissaro argues from the Cullavagga's allowance for a monk who ruminates that eating is "taking into the mouth", and Ajahn Brahm reads the passage the same way and draws the consequence: food in the teeth, swallowed after noon, is no offence.35​ 36 Brahmāli adds Pc 40's "should bring food to the mouth" and reads the ruminator passage his own way: it forbids "tasking food into the mouth and then swallowing it, whereas a ruminator is allowed to bring food up and then re-swallow it". He reaches a third position, that eating is "the combination of putting food in the mouth and then swallowing it", so that neither case is an offence.37​ 38 The passage was not written to define eating, and all three readings stretch it. All three agree that food is not to be put into the mouth after noon to be eaten.

    Receiving fresh or cooked food at the wrong time with the intention of eating it is an offence of wrong conduct, and every mouthful a pācittiya; Thanissaro reports the Commentary placing the wrong conduct at accepting in the wrong time, not at accepting in the morning food meant for the afternoon.39​ 40 Tonics taken as food carry their own pair of wrong-conduct offences, as at Pc 35.41

    Thanissaro finds the boundary's reason in the food rules taken together. Read with Pc 38, this rule means a monk who may neither eat before dawn nor keep food overnight has no reason to go for alms in the dark, and at MN 66 Venerable Udāyin tells the Buddha what the dark had cost. A woman washing a pot saw him by a flash of lightning and screamed that a demon was after her; told that he was a monk waiting for alms, she answered that it would be better for him "that your belly be slit open with a sharp butcher's knife than this prowling around for alms for your belly's sake in the pitch black of night."42 That, on Thanissaro's reading, is one of the things the line at dawn is for: no monk need be that figure in the dark again.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — receiving fresh or cooked food at the wrong time with the intention of eating it.43​ 44
    • Dukkaṭa — receiving post-midday, seven-day or lifetime tonics for the purpose of food, and a further dukkaṭa for every mouthful.45
    • Dukkaṭa — where it is the right time but he perceives it as the wrong time, or is unsure. Where it is the wrong time, all three perceptions carry the full pācittiya.46

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if, when there is a reason, he uses post-midday tonics, seven-day tonics, or lifetime tonics; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.47

    There is no exemption for illness, and Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm give the same reason: a sick monk already has the tonics at any hour.48​ 49 Two allowances stand outside the Vibhaṅga's list. A monk who has taken a purgative may take strained meat, rice or green-gram broth at any time, which Thanissaro extends to a monk similarly ill or worse, though "mere hunger or fatigue would not seem to count as sufficient reasons"; and loṇasovīraka may be taken in the wrong time as a medicine by a monk who is ill and, mixed with water, as a drink by one who is not. Whether that allowance reaches miso Thanissaro calls controversial and doubts.50

    Related Offences

    • Pc 38 — stored food; read with this rule it means a monk cannot go for alms before dawn.
    • Pc 35 — where fresh and cooked food are defined, and where tonics taken as food carry the same pair of offences.
    • Pc 29 — where the naming of the two food-categories is worked through.
    • Pc 40 — where "should bring food to the mouth" supplies half of Brahmāli's definition of eating.
    • NP 2 — where the offence falls at dawn; when dawn rises is discussed here.
    • NP 23 — the tonics and the illnesses that license them.
    • Pc 4 — the rule whose perception pattern this one follows.

    Related Suttas

    • DN 1 — the restraint by which people judged a renunciant, praised in the Buddha himself, "refraining from eating at improper times", which Walshe's note puts between midday and the following dawn, the rule's own bounds.51
    • MN 66 — Udāyin tells how the monks gave up first the afternoon meal and then the night meal, unhappy at it, "out of our love and respect for the Blessed One", and what alms in the dark had cost: the woman who took him for a devil by a flash of lightning.52
    • MN 70 — the monks at Kīṭāgiri refuse to give up eating at night, to which the Buddha had invited the Saṅgha with its benefit, "lightness, strength, and a comfortable abiding": the later of the two prohibitions MN 66 records, and the resistance it met.53

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; the two-question exchange between the six and the seventeen is verbatim. The Manual, the recitation, the class transcript (session 026, 31 May 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1 with his Appendix I on dawn, to which his chapter points, Ajahn Brahm, including his treatment of dawn at NP 2, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were checked. The Cullavagga passage on the ruminating monk is given in Brahmāli's translation, and the Udāyin passage was checked against the Majjhima Nikāya. Ajahn Brahm's retelling of the story adds a motive the Vibhaṅga does not give, and it is not carried into the Story. The class is cited for two points of modern practice, solar noon and the dawn kept at Brahmāli's monastery, and for his view, which no written source states, that the rule sets no number of meals; in the Indian anecdote the transcription dropped a negation, and I give only what the surrounding sentences state.
