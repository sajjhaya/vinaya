    NP 11

    Story (Vatthu)

    While the Buddha was staying at Āḷavī at the Aggāḷava Shrine, the monks from the group of six went to silk-makers and said, "Please boil a heap of silkworms and give us silk. We want to make a blanket containing silk." The silk-makers complained and criticised them, "How can the Sakyan monastics come and say such things to us? It's our misfortune that we must kill many small creatures because of our livelihoods and because of our wives and children."

    The monks heard the complaints of those silk-makers, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised those monks, "How could the monks from the group of six go to silk-makers and say such a thing?" After rebuking them in many ways, they told the Buddha. He had the Saṅgha gathered and questioned the monks: "Is it true, monks, that you did this?" "It's true, sir." The Buddha rebuked them: "Foolish men, how could you do this? This will affect people's confidence …" And he laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — the group of six ask silk-makers to boil silkworms so that they can have a silk-mixed blanket made; the silk-makers, who kill such creatures only to support their families, criticise them for asking it; the Buddha prohibits having a blanket containing silk made.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk has a blanket made that contains silk, he commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu makes a blanket containing silk, or has one made, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu have a felt (blanket/rug) made of a mixture containing silk, it is to be forfeited and confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Having a santhata made with silk is a nissaggiya pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should have a rug mixed with silk made, [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture."6

    The renderings agree on what is forbidden and part over a single word that they all must translate, santhata. Brahmāli gives "blanket", Thanissaro "felt (blanket/rug)", Ñāṇatusita "rug", and Ajahn Brahm leaves the Pāli as it stands. The difference is more than a choice of English, for it decides whether anything a monk uses today falls under this rule and the four after it; it is argued under Object. The recitation's "makes … or has one made" spells out what the Pāli's single causative, kārāpeyya, leaves to the definitions, which give both.7

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule guards a monk's harmlessness: he may not ask that beings be killed for his comfort, and the laity's confidence in him rests on it. The silk-makers in the Story did not defend their trade. They called it their misfortune, a killing of "many small creatures" forced on them by their livelihoods and their families, and what they could not understand was renunciants coming to ask for it; the Buddha's rebuke names what was at stake: "This will affect people's confidence."8 The fault, in other words, lay in the asking. The silk-makers would have boiled silkworms whether the monks came or not, and it was the monks' wanting a silk blanket enough to ask for it that the laity saw, and that drew the Buddha's rebuke.

    NP 11 opens the second chapter of the class, named the silk chapter after it, and its first five rules all govern one requisite, the santhata.9 What a santhata is, and whether anything a monk now uses is one, is therefore set out once, here, and NP 12–15 carry only what is their own. The offence has three factors: object, effort, and result.10

    Object

    The object is a santhata containing silk, intended for one's own use.11 The Vibhaṅga defines the santhata in a single line, by how it is made: "it is made by strewing, not by weaving."12 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual gives the Commentary's method, layer upon layer of thread spread on an even stretch of ground and sprinkled with rice water, and Thanissaro adds the rolling, repeated until the felt is thick and strong enough for its purpose.13​ 14 We are left with a kind of felt. Its uses are pieced together from hints across NP 11–15: a small personal rug for sitting or lying on, a rough blanket for a monk who is sick or cold, and something worn about the body, which the Commentary, the Manual notes, calls a "fourth robe".15​ 16 Ajahn Brahm concludes from these, and from the santhata's absence from the Khandhaka's chapters on robes and on furnishings, that santhata names a material rather than one particular requisite, used for sitting mats, sleeping mats, rugs and blankets, and perhaps worn like a shawl; it was thick and bulky to carry, and it could not be washed, since the rice glue would dissolve.17

    Range of application. Does anything a monk uses today count? Thanissaro reads the rules as applying "precisely to this type of blanket/rug", felt made in the old way, which is still found in parts of India.18 Brahmāli observes that if the santhata is defined strictly by that method of manufacture, NP 11–15 are defunct, since nobody now makes cloth by strewing thread and pouring rice water on it, and he would classify it by its use instead, which is why he renders it "blanket".19 Yet he does not let the use decide alone. In the Vinaya there is no limit to the thickness or material of a robe, so all woven cloth counts as robe-cloth, and the santhata is a separate category only because it is not woven.20 The Manual accordingly puts the two tests together: the object is "any type of unwoven cloth similar to felt and similar to a blanket in size".21 I take the Manual's test, since it keeps both what the Vibhaṅga defines and what a monk actually uses. And so a felt-type, unwoven blanket, mat or rug, for sitting on, lying on or wrapping around oneself, falls under NP 11–15; a woven blanket, however thick, is robe-cloth and is governed by the robe rules of NP 1–10. The non-offence clause points the same way: the same silk-mixed felt made as a canopy, a floor cover, a screen, a mattress or a pillow is no offence, so it is the felt a monk sits, lies or wraps himself in that the rule has in view.22

    Containing silk. Even a single thread of silk is enough.23

    Disputed — whether silk that enters the felt without the monk's doing it brings the offence.

    The Vibhaṅga does not mention intention or perception here, and the Commentary, in the report Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm both give, concludes that if a silk thread floats in on the breeze and lands in the felt while it is being made, the monk commits the offence all the same.24 Brahmāli reads the rule's own words the other way: "after mixing in" is the monk's act, and the rule's phrasing, "getting to make a santhata containing silk", means that "the silk has to be deliberately added at the monk's request."25 Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm give the Commentary's reading without contesting it, and both suggest that it may be why monks stopped using felt, Thanissaro adding, "for there is no way of knowing whether there are any stray silk filaments in them."26 On the Commentary's reading, then, a monk cannot be sure of any felt he has had made; on Brahmāli's he is safe so long as he has silk put in none. I find Brahmāli's reading the closer to the rule's wording, but it is one translator's reading against the Commentary's, and the Vibhaṅga's silence on intention leaves both standing.

    Effort

    Making the blanket oneself or having it made, and the Vibhaṅga carries the full offence through every way of finishing it: finishing what one began oneself, having others finish it, finishing oneself what others began, and having others finish what others began.27 "For the effort there is an act of wrong conduct."28 The Vibhaṅga does not say when the effort begins. Thanissaro, at NP 26, carries the Commentary's reading of the causative across this family of getting-things-made rules: no offence for the request itself, but wrong conduct once the request persuades the maker to act on it.29

    Result

    The offence is incurred when he gets the blanket: on finishing it, if he makes it himself, or on receiving it, if another makes it for him.30​ 31 It is then relinquished to a Saṅgha, a group or an individual, by the procedure of NP 1, and given back to him.32

    Special Cases

    Suppose someone buys a ready-made felt blanket with silk in it and offers it to a monk. He has not had it made, so the full offence is not met, but the Vibhaṅga gives a separate act of wrong conduct for using a silk-mixed blanket that someone else has made, as it does for making or having one made for someone else.33 Ajahn Brahm reasons from those two penalties that a santhata containing silk is unallowable for any monk by its very nature: even the offender who has relinquished his, confessed, and received it back still may not use it, and would have to give it to a layperson, throw it away, or, more likely, shred it and use the material for something else.34 The Vibhaṅga itself never states the conclusion; it follows from asking what is left once every route to the blanket carries a penalty. He draws the same conclusion for the all-black and mostly-black felts of NP 12 and NP 13, and the opposite for those of NP 14 and NP 15, which once given back may be used as the monk pleases, since in themselves they are not unallowable.35 Shredding fits the non-offence clause too, since the same felt made into a floor cover or a pillow is allowed.36

    Silk itself is not forbidden. Ajahn Brahm notes that it is an allowable material for robes and other requisites, though a robe wholly of silk would strike many as extravagant and sit ill with "the very ethos of a frugal and a compassionate monk"; a monk who, like the group of six, told silk-farmers to kill silkworms for him would commit an offence under Pc 61 for the killing.37 He suggests, too, treating silk as the meat allowances treat meat, and refusing it where a monk has seen, heard or suspected that silkworms were killed for him.38 That suggestion is his own and no rule; it carries the Story's concern to cases the Vibhaṅga does not reach.

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The exemptions speak of making such things; the texts do not say that having one made is also exempt, but Thanissaro observes that it apparently is.39

    The silk-makers did not ask monks to give up silk. They asked why monks should want them to kill for it, and a monk who keeps that question in mind will know what to do with the silk the rule never mentions.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — for the effort of making a silk-mixed santhata, or having it made, before it is got.40
    • Dukkaṭa — making one, or having one made, for someone else.41
    • Dukkaṭa — using one that someone else has made.42​ 43

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he makes a canopy, a floor cover, a cloth screen, a mattress, or a pillow; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.44

    Related Offences

    • NP 12 — a santhata made entirely of black wool.
    • NP 13 — a santhata more than half black wool.
    • NP 14 — making a new santhata within six years of the last.
    • NP 15 — a sitting santhata made without a piece of an old one.
    • NP 16–17 — the chapter's rules on raw wool, the santhata's material.
    • NP 26 — the causative reading that governs when the wrong conduct for having a thing made begins.
    • Pc 61 — intentionally killing an animal: the offence for instigating the killing of silkworms.
    • NP 1 — the relinquishment procedure.

    Related Suttas

    • DN 1 §1.15 — among the "high and wide beds" the ascetic Gotama is praised for refraining from, "fleecy or variegated coverlets … silk coverlets"; the comfort he set aside is what the monks of the Story wanted enough to ask for silkworms to be boiled.45

The Commentary is cited as the Manual, Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm report it. The Concise BMC restates BMC1 and is not cited, and the class on this rule is read but not cited.
