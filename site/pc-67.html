    Pc 67

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time a monk who was travelling through the Kosalan country on his way to Sāvatthī walked through the gateway of a certain village. A woman who had had an argument with her husband walked through the same gateway. When she saw that monk, she asked him, "Venerable, where are you going?"1

    "I'm going to Sāvatthī."

    "May I go with you?"

    "Sure."2

    Soon afterwards that woman's husband also left that village. He asked around, "Have you seen such-and-such a woman?"

    "She's walking along with a monastic."3

    He then followed after them, caught that monk, and gave him a beating. The monk sat down fuming at the foot of a tree.4

    And the woman said to her husband, "This monk didn't make me go; I was the one who went with him. He's innocent. Go and ask his forgiveness." And he did so.5

    That monk then went to Sāvatthī, where he told the monks what had happened. The monks of few desires complained and criticised him: "How could a monk travel by arrangement with a woman?" They told the Buddha, who questioned him and rebuked him: "Foolish man, how could you do this? This will affect people's confidence." A monk who travels by arrangement with a woman, even just to the next inhabited area, commits an offence entailing confession.6

    Synopsis — A monk agreed to let a woman walk with him to Sāvatthī, her husband caught up with them and beat him, and she had to explain that the monk had done nothing. The rule that follows is the last of the four travelling rules and, unlike Pc 66 beside it, has no perception factor at all.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk travels by arrangement with a woman, even just to the next inhabited area, he commits an offense entailing confession."7

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu travels on a country road by arrangement with a woman, he incurs a pācittiya."8

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu, by arrangement, travel together with a woman, even for the interval between one village and the next, it is to be confessed."9

    Brahm

    "By arrangement, going on a journey with a woman is an offence of Pācittiya."10

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu, having made an arrangement, should travel together with a woman on the same main road, even [if] just the distance between villages, [this is a case] involving expiation."11

    The renderings agree, and Ñāṇatusita's whole comment says why: "Identical with preceding rule except: mātugāmena: with a woman."12 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual likewise resolves the rule's phrases by pointing at Pc 27, and its "woman" at Sg 3; the recitation drops antamaso gāmantarampi, as it does at Pc 66.13​ 14 One difference in the Pāli is easy to miss and does change what the rule requires. The rule line is Pc 66's with one word changed and one word gone: there is no jānaṁ, "knowingly".15 What follows from that is taken up below, under Perception.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule protects a monk's celibate life, and the trust of the people who see whom he walks with. A woman leaving her village after a quarrel with her husband asked a monk on the road whether she might go with him to Sāvatthī, and he said "Sure"; when the husband caught up with them he beat the monk, and she had to tell him that the monk had not made her go.16 The husband's suspicion is the one anyone would have, and the Buddha's rebuke names that cost: "This will affect people's confidence." But Brahmāli places the rule beside Pc 44 and Pc 45, the rules against being alone with a woman, which he calls "surprisingly important to sustain the monastic life", and that is the deeper reason: the road shared by arrangement is the same intimacy, on the move.17

    This is a lean mirror, and the anchor is Pc 27. The specimen dialogues that define an arrangement, the requirement that both parties speak, the dukkaṭa attached to the arrangement itself, the travel as arranged, and the count by inhabited areas or, in the wilderness, by distance are all set out there.18​ 19 What differs is the object and the perception, and one practical question the other members of the family do not raise: what counts as company.

    Object

    The woman is defined as at Sg 3: a human female able to tell decent speech from indecent — in Ajahn Brahm's words, "one old enough to understand flirtatious speech".20​ 21 Travelling by arrangement with a female spirit, a female ghost, a paṇḍaka or an animal in the form of a woman is an offence of wrong conduct.22 Thanissaro names this rule as one of four where it matters whether a paṇḍaka who may validly ordain belongs in that list; his answer, argued at Sg 2, is that such a person counts as a man under all rules.23

    Perception

    Here the missing word makes its difference. Pc 66 gives the full offence only where the party is perceived as thieving, and no offence where it is not. This rule's grid gives the full offence whether the monk perceives the woman as a woman, is unsure, or does not perceive her as one at all.24 Thanissaro states it in a sentence: "Perception as to whether the person is actually a woman is not a mitigating factor here."25 A monk who agrees to travel with someone he takes for a man has the offence if she is a woman and they travel.

    Effort

    The arrangement is Pc 27's, and the origin story shows how little it takes. In the Pāli the woman does not so much ask as announce — "I will go with the venerable" — and it was the monk's assent that made the arrangement.26 Had he said nothing, the second non-offence clause would have covered him: "if the woman has made an arrangement, but he has not expressed his agreement."27

    Thanissaro finds the rule's modern force in public transport, where prior arrangement is still what separates travelling together from happening to share a bus or a plane.28 Brahmāli turned the same point into a procedure. The rule, he said, is "actually quite easy to break": a laywoman offers a lift, the monk agrees, and it is done. The remedy is to let someone else make the arrangement — in his monastery, the monk who handles travel — though a second man is still wanted in the car for the sake of Pc 44.29 The same test settles the case of a monk whose parents would drive him to hospital. There is no exemption for relatives, but the factor is who the arrangement is with: a monk who arranges with his father, and agrees when his father asks whether his mother may come, has made no arrangement with her.30

    Disputed — whether travelling in company with others alters the offence.

    The Vibhaṅga's dialogues have one monk and one woman and do not ask.31 Thanissaro reads "woman" as including "women", so that there is an offence for every woman in the arrangement, but calls the inclusion of men "a controversial issue at present". He reports two Community practices — that cars fall under Pc 44 rather than this rule, so that a monk may ride with a woman "as long as a knowledgeable man is present", and that the arrangement is made implicitly with the driver — and takes neither side, asking only that a monk follow his Community and never arrange verbally with a woman.32​ 33 Ajahn Brahm reaches the strict count from the origin stories of Pc 27 and Pc 28, and then, since the strict standard is hard to keep and the question uncertain, allows a journey with women "as long as there is at least one male accompanying the monk and the journey is not long".34 I would teach that the offence falls on the arrangement, which company does not undo, since that is what the rule's own wording supports, and Thanissaro's one fixed condition says the same from the other side. Brahmāli, in class, put it plainly: the presence of other travellers "is actually irrelevant".35 This is a judgement between teachers and not a text; Ajahn Brahm's relaxation is offered openly as a response to uncertainty, and a Community that follows it is not ignoring the rule.

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The four clauses are Pc 66's, and Ajahn Brahm gives the first two in everyday form: she asks, he refuses, and she follows anyway, or they happen to be going the same way.36

    The woman's defence of the monk was true — he had not made her go — and it changed nothing, for the arrangement was his single word of assent. A monk on the road cannot choose who walks beside him. He can choose what he answers when he is asked.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — for the arrangement itself, before any travelling is done, as at Pc 27.37
    • Dukkaṭa — where the monk makes the arrangement and the woman does not express her agreement.38
    • Dukkaṭa — travelling by arrangement with a female spirit, a female ghost, a paṇḍaka or an animal in the form of a woman.39​ 40
    • Dukkaṭa — where it is not a woman but the monk perceives one, or is unsure.41
    • Dukkaṭa (Parivāra) — for the effort, while travelling; the pācittiya follows when the travelling is done.42

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence "if he goes, but not by arrangement; if the woman has made an arrangement, but he has not expressed his agreement; if he goes, but not according to the arrangement; if there is an emergency; if he is insane; if he is the first offender."43

    Related Offences

    • Pc 27 — travelling by arrangement on a country road with a nun: the family's anchor, to which Thanissaro and the Manual refer this rule's effort.44​ 45
    • Pc 28, 66 — travelling by arrangement in a boat with a nun, and with a group of thieves: the family's other two members. Pc 66 carries the knowledge factor this rule lacks.
    • Pc 44, 45 — sitting alone with a woman in a secluded place, and in a private one: the rules a monk is likelier to break in a car he has lawfully arranged. Brahmāli's introduction groups this rule with them.46
    • Pc 4 — where Thanissaro sets out the pattern of a perception that does not mitigate.47
    • Sg 3 — speaking lewdly to a woman, whose definition of a woman this rule takes over.48

    Related Suttas

    • AN 5.55 — "because they often saw one another, a bond was formed": the intimacy that grows on the road, where this rule applies; Brahmāli groups it with Pc 44 and Pc 45 as rules "surprisingly important to sustain the monastic life".49​ 50
    • SN 35.127 — the reflection by which young monks keep the celibate life among women, regarding them as mothers, sisters and daughters: the guard a monk brings to any road a woman shares (as at Pc 44).51

    37. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 67, final ruling and definitions. "If a monk travels by arrangement with a woman, even just to the next inhabited area, he commits an offense entailing confession." "A woman: a human female, not a female spirit, not a female ghost, not a female animal. She understands and is capable of discerning good and bad speech, what is decent and what is indecent." "By arrangement: if he makes an arrangement like this: he says, 'Let's go,' and she replies, 'Yes, let's go, venerable;' or she says, 'Let's go, venerable,' and he replies, 'Yes, let's go;' or he says, 'Let's go today,' 'Let's go tomorrow,' or 'Let's go the day after tomorrow,' then he commits an offense of wrong conduct."↩︎
