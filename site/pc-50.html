    Pc 50

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. The monks from the group of six were staying with the army for two or three nights. They went to see battles, troop reviews, the massing of the army, and troop inspections.

    One of the monks who went to a battle was struck by an arrow. People teased him, "We hope you had a good battle, venerable. How many targets did you hit?" And because they teased him, he felt humiliated.

    People complained and criticised those monks, "How can the Sakyan monastics go to see a battle? It's our misfortune that we must go to battles for the sake of our livelihoods and because of our wives and children."

    The monks heard the complaints of those people, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised those monks. The Buddha questioned them, established that it was true, rebuked them, and stated the rule.1

    Synopsis — Monks who are lawfully in the camp spend their three nights going to watch whatever the army is doing, and one of them is shot. People ask him how many targets he hit, and he is humiliated. The rule that follows binds a monk who is lawfully in the camp and tells him what he may not go and look at while he is there.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk who is staying with an army for two or three nights goes to a battle, a troop review, a massing of the army, or a troop inspection, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu, while staying with an army, goes to see the army in action, he incurs a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "If a bhikkhu staying two or three nights with an army should go to a battlefield, a roll call, the troops in battle formation, or to see a review of the (battle) units, it is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "While staying with the military, going intending to see a battle, roll-call, training session or review is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If a bhikkhu who is staying two nights or three nights with an army should go to a battle-field, or a review, or a massing of the army, or an inspection of units, [this is a case] involving expiation."6

    Four Pāli words carry the rule, uyyodhika, balagga, senābyūha and anīkadassana, and no two translators render all four alike; Ñāṇatusita prefaces them, "The meanings of the following terms are not completely clear."7 "Review" moves: it renders balagga in Brahmāli and Ñāṇatusita and anīkadassana in Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm, so a reader comparing them will take the second item of one list for the fourth of the other. The Vibhaṅga's glosses settle two of the words. Balagga is "so many elephants, so many horses, so many chariots, so much infantry", a count, and senābyūha is an order for each arm to set out from its place, a deployment.8 On those glosses Thanissaro's "roll call" and "troops in battle formation" seem the closer English, and "a massing of the army" renders the word's etymology rather than the Vibhaṅga's sense. Brahmāli's recitation line collapses all four into "goes to see the army in action", the phrase it uses for Pc 48, so that in the recitation the two rules differ by "while staying with an army" and by the loss of Pc 48's "except with an appropriate reason".9 What a monk must do is the same whichever words are chosen.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    Being lawfully in a camp is no licence to watch what the army does there, and this rule keeps a monk who has a reason to be among soldiers to that reason, so that the war he is close to never becomes his spectacle. The group of six were staying with the army within the limit of Pc 49, and spent the nights going to see its battles and parades; one of them was struck by an arrow, and the people's teasing, "How many targets did you hit?", humiliated him.10

    This is a lean mirror of Pc 48, and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual says so, referring "army", "goes to see" and the penalty there.11 The step-by-step grading, the renewed seeing and the meaning of army are Pc 48's. Four things differ.

    Circumstance

    The first difference is the reason the rule exists: the monk is lawfully in the camp. The rule opens by quoting Pc 49's permission, a monk "staying with an army for two or three nights", and then tells a monk who has done nothing wrong what he may not do while he is there.12 Ajahn Brahm's gloss gives the working shape of it: what he sees unintentionally "in the course of doing one's business" is no offence, and what he goes out to see is.13

    Object

    The second difference is that size drops away. At Pc 48 the object is a fourfold army. Here it is an activity, and the Vibhaṅga fixes each troop's floor, not its scale: three elephants, three horses, three chariots, four armed men, each "the smallest troop".14 Thanissaro draws the consequence: a monk staying with the army incurs the full penalty for watching these activities even if they involve only a small part of a single division, where a monk not staying with it would come under Pc 48 and incur only a dukkaṭa for a part.15

    Perception

    The third difference is that the Vibhaṅga gives no perception series here. The Concise BMC notices the gap and answers with a hedge: Pc 48's clauses "arguably apply here".16 It seems reasonable to me to read the silence as abbreviation, on the continuation principle Thanissaro applies at Pc 49, but the text does not say so, and a monk who treats perception here as at Pc 48 is safe on either reading.

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The fourth difference is small and real. Pc 48's fourth exemption is a suitable reason. This rule's exempts the monk who goes because something needs doing and then sees it, and its second clause adds that the army comes to where the monk is and he sees fighting.17 And so the two lists differ where the two rules need them to. A suitable reason is what brings a monk to the army in the first place, and it is already spent by the time this rule applies to him; what he needs here is an exemption for the errand that happens to take him past a parade. That reading rests on the Vibhaṅga's own wording; Thanissaro calls the two lists identical.18

    Thanissaro extends the rule by the Great Standards, since DN 1 lists these four activities among entertainments: any show the armed forces put on for the public, such as a parade or an air show, falls under it.19​ 20

    Of these three stories, only the arrow's has a consequence and not a complaint alone. The chapter's own summary verse names its rules in a word or two and remembers this one by the wound, "struck".21 The chapter that opened with food handed to a naked ascetic ends with a monk who went to watch and was shot.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — on the way to see one of the four activities; the pācittiya falls where he stands and sees, and again at each renewed seeing.22
    • Dukkaṭa — on the way, on seeing and on each renewed seeing, where what he goes to see is one division of a fourfold army.23

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he sees it while standing in a monastery; if the army comes to where the monk is standing, sitting, or lying down, and he then sees fighting; if he sees it while walking in the opposite direction; if he goes because there is something to be done, and he then sees it; if there is an emergency; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.24

    Related Offences

    • Pc 48 — going to see a mobilised army: the anchor, which governs the monk who is not staying with the army.
    • Pc 49 — staying more than three nights with an army: the permission this rule's opening clause quotes.
    • Kd 15:2.6 — going to see dancing, singing and music: the general rule against shows, treated at Pc 48.

    Related Suttas

    • DN 1 §1.13 — the shows the Buddha refrains from attending, which end with this rule's four terms in its order (see Pc 48).25

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; the people's taunt and their complaint are verbatim. Ajahn Brahm's telling has soldiers mock the monk, who went to see the soldiers training and was wounded by a stray arrow, and the Story follows the Vibhaṅga; the Synopsis's "lawfully" reads Pc 49 forward into the story. Brahmāli's recitation, the Manual and the class transcript (session 028, 12 June 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1, Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were checked; the class is not cited, since Ajahn Brahm and Thanissaro carry each point it makes. DN 1 is quoted from Walshe's translation.
