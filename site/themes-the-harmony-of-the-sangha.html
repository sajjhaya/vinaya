    The Harmony of the Saṅgha

    The quarrel at Kosambī began with one offence and two opinions of it. A monk had committed an offence and did not regard it as one; other monks did, and they ejected him for not recognising it. He was "learned, a master of the tradition", and he told his friends, "This isn't an offense, and so I haven't committed any. And I haven't been ejected, for the legal procedure was illegitimate, reversible, and unfit to stand." He was able to form a faction.1 The Buddha went to each side in turn. He told the monks who had ejected him that if they understood "the gravity of schism, they shouldn't eject that monk", and he told the monks siding with him, "If you've committed an offense, don't refuse to make amends for it just because you think that you haven't committed it."2 Thanissaro counts the second of these among several places in the Vinaya where, "for the sake of the harmony of the Community in cases that threaten to be divisive, the Buddha advises bhikkhus to abandon controversial behavior and to yield to the mandate of the Community even if it seems unjust."3

    Neither side yielded. When the quarrel reached the Saṅgha's meetings and the Buddha asked them to stop, a monk answered him: "Wait, sir, you're the Lord of the Teaching. Be at ease and enjoy the happiness of meditation. We'll face the consequences of this quarreling and disputing."4 The Buddha spoke verses in the midst of the Saṅgha, "When many voices shout at once, / No one thinks they are a fool. / Even as the Sangha splits, / They do not think it through", and left Kosambī.5 On the way he visited Anuruddha, Nandiya and Kimila, and asked whether they were living "in peace and harmony, blending like milk and water". Anuruddha told him how: he asked himself, "Why don't I set aside what I wish to do and instead do what these venerables wish?", and did it. "We're separate in body, but it might seem as if we're one in mind."6 Then, alone in a forest grove at Pālileyyaka, the Buddha reflected, "Previously, when I was surrounded by those quarreling monks at Kosambī, I wasn't at ease. But now that I'm alone, away from those monks, I'm happy and at ease."7

    It was the lay followers of Kosambī who forced the monks to settle the quarrel. "These venerable monks at Kosambī have caused us much misfortune. The Buddha himself left because he was troubled by them," they said, and they stopped honouring and feeding them: "Then, they'll either leave, disrobe, or reconcile with the Buddha." The monks went to Sāvatthī. There the ejected monk reflected on the Teaching and the Monastic Law and concluded, "This is an offense and I've committed it", and he was readmitted. The Saṅgha then met, everyone present, and unified itself by a motion and one announcement: "The schism in the Sangha has come to an end." The Buddha's next instruction was, "The observance-day ceremony, the recitation of the Monastic Code, should be done straightaway."8 The sign that harmony had returned was a Saṅgha able to recite the Pātimokkha together.

    Sg 10 states that harmony in the words the monks are to say to a monk who pursues schism: "Stay with the Sangha, for a united Sangha—in concord, in harmony, having a joint recitation—is at ease."9 This rule and Sg 11 were laid down against Devadatta and the four who backed him, whose story belongs to Characters of the Vinaya. Thanissaro reminds the reader how grave a schism is: achieved in a dishonest way, it ranks with killing one's mother, one's father or an arahant, or wounding a Buddha, "as one of the five most heinous crimes a person can commit".10 Kosambī shows that the danger need not arrive in Devadatta's form. There the faction was led by a conscientious and learned monk, and the monks on both sides believed the Teaching was with them.

    The Pācittiyas guard the harmony of the Saṅgha at the point where it is made, in its meetings and decisions. At Pc 3 the group of six carried tales between monks who were arguing, "in order to create division between them. In this way they started new quarrels and made existing quarrels worse."11 At Pc 63 they reopened a legal issue they knew had been legitimately settled. At Pc 79 they gave their consent to a meeting and complained of its decision afterwards, and at Pc 80 one of them, suspecting the procedure was meant for one of his group, got up and left without giving his consent while the Saṅgha was in the middle of it. At Pc 81 they gave a robe to Dabba "as part of a unanimous Sangha" and then said the monks were "diverting the Sangha's material support according to friendship".12 Each rule leaves a monk free to act in good faith. A monk who reopens or criticises a procedure "because he knows that the legal procedure was illegitimate, done by an incomplete assembly, or done against one who did not deserve it" commits no offence, and a monk may leave a meeting "because he thinks there will be a fracture or schism in the Sangha".13 What these rules ask of a monk who took part in a lawful decision is that he stand by it.

    Harmony, as these rules guard it, is a matter of acting as one body: meeting together, deciding together, keeping a decision once it is made and reciting the Pātimokkha with a single voice. Opinions may differ, and the exemptions protect the monk who differs in good faith. The Buddha at Pālileyyaka and the rule at Sg 10 use the same word: the ease the quarrel cost him is the ease the rule promises a united Saṅgha. The way to it that a single monk can practise is Anuruddha's, and it costs him only his own preference: "Why don't I set aside what I wish to do and instead do what these venerables wish?"

    8. Brahmāli, Kd 10 (Kosambakkhandhaka): "These venerable monks at Kosambī have caused us much misfortune.     9. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Sg 10, final ruling: "Stay with the Sangha, for a united Sangha—in concord, in harmony, having a joint recitation—is at ease."↩︎

    11. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 3, origin story: "After hearing something on one side they reported it to the other side, and vice versa, in order to create division between them. In this way they started new quarrels and made existing quarrels worse."↩︎

    12. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 63, origin story: the group of six "were reopening a legal issue that they knew had been legitimately settled"; Pc 79, origin story: "How could the monks from the group of six give their consent to legitimate legal procedures and then criticize them afterwards?"; Pc 80, origin story: "When the Sangha is in the middle of a discussion, how could that monk get up from his seat and leave without giving his consent?"; Pc 81, origin story: "The monks are diverting the Sangha's material support according to friendship"; "How could the monks from the group of six give out a robe as part of a unanimous Sangha and then criticize it afterwards?"↩︎

    13. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 63 and Pc 79, non-offenses: "because he knows that the legal procedure was illegitimate, done by an incomplete assembly, or done against one who did not deserve it"; Pc 80, non-offenses: "if he leaves because he thinks there will be a fracture or schism in the Sangha".↩︎
