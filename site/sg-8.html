    Sg 8

    Story (Vatthu)

    At one time when the Buddha was staying at Rājagaha in the Bamboo Grove, Venerable Dabba the Mallian realized perfection at the age of seven. Having nothing further to do, he wondered how he could be of service to the Saṅgha, and he went to the Buddha and proposed to assign the dwellings and designate the meals. The Buddha approved, and the Saṅgha appointed him by a formal motion.

    Dabba assigned dwellings according to character. He put the experts on the discourses together, thinking, "They'll recite the discourses to one another"; the experts on the Monastic Law, so they could discuss the law; the expounders of the Teaching, so they could discuss the Teaching; the meditators, thinking, "They won't disturb one another"; and the gossips and the bodybuilders, thinking, "In this way even these venerables will be happy." When monks arrived at night, he entered the fire element and, with his finger glowing, walked ahead of them to their dwellings. Monks even arrived late on purpose, hoping to see the marvel, and asked to stay somewhere far away: on the Vulture Peak, at Robbers' Cliff, in Jīvaka's Mango Grove.

    The monks Mettiya and Bhūmajaka were newly ordained and had little merit, and they got inferior dwellings and meals: where the senior monks were given ghee, oil and special curries, they got ordinary food of porridge and broken rice. A householder gave a regular meal of fine food to four monks, serving it with his wives and children, and one day the next day's meal was designated to Mettiya and Bhūmajaka. That householder happened to visit Dabba, asked who was to come, and was disappointed: "Why should bad monks eat in our house?" At home he told a female slave to prepare seats for them in the gatehouse and to serve them broken rice and porridge. The two monks, expecting rice, bean curry, oil and special curries, were so excited that they did not sleep properly that night. In the morning they were seated in the gatehouse and served broken rice and porridge. "Yesterday this householder came to the monastery and spoke with Dabba," they said to each other. "Dabba must be responsible for this split between the householder and us." They were too dejected to eat as much as they had intended, and back at the monastery they squatted outside the gatehouse, silent and humiliated, with shoulders drooping and heads down, glum and speechless.

    The nun Mettiyā came and greeted them three times, and they did not respond. "Have I done something wrong?" "It's because we've been badly treated by Dabba the Mallian, and you're not taking an interest." "But what can I do?" "If you like, you could make the Buddha expel Dabba." They told her what to say: "Sir, this isn't proper or appropriate. There's fear, distress, and oppression in this district, where none of these should exist. It's windy where it should be calm. It's as if water is burning. Venerable Dabba the Mallian has raped me." She went to the Buddha and repeated it.

    The Buddha had the Saṅgha gathered and asked Dabba whether he remembered doing as the nun said. "Sir, you know what I'm like." He asked a second and a third time and got the same answer. "Dabba, the Dabbas don't give such evasive answers. If it was done by you, say so; if it wasn't, then say that." "Since I was born, sir, I don't recall having sexual intercourse even in a dream, let alone when awake." "Well then, monks, expel the nun Mettiyā, and call these monks to account." The Buddha then got up and entered his dwelling.

    When the monks had expelled the nun Mettiyā, Mettiya and Bhūmajaka said to them, "Don't expel the nun Mettiyā; she hasn't done anything wrong. She was urged on by us. We were angry and displeased, and trying to make Dabba give up the monastic life." "But did you groundlessly charge Venerable Dabba with an offense entailing expulsion?" "Yes." The monks of few desires complained and told the Buddha, who questioned the two monks, rebuked them, and laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — Resentful over a poor meal, the monks Mettiya and Bhūmajaka have the nun Mettiyā falsely accuse Venerable Dabba, an arahant, of rape; when the scheme unravels, the Buddha lays down the rule against groundlessly accusing a monk of an offence entailing expulsion.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk who is angry and displeased groundlessly charges a monk with an offense entailing expulsion, aiming to make him leave the monastic life, and then after some time, whether he is questioned or not, it is clear that the legal issue is groundless, and he admits to his ill will, he commits an offense entailing suspension."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu, motivated by anger, falsely accuses a bhikkhu of a pārājika, aiming to make him disrobe, he incurs a saṅghādisesa."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu—corrupt, aversive, disgruntled—charge a bhikkhu with an unfounded case entailing defeat, (thinking), 'Perhaps I may bring about his fall from this celibate life,' then regardless of whether or not he is cross-examined on a later occasion, if the issue is unfounded and the bhikkhu confesses his aversion, it entails initial and subsequent meetings of the Community."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Should any monk, malicious, angry, displeased, charge a monk with an unfounded case involving defeat (thinking) 'surely with this I may bring about his fall from the holy life', then regardless of whether or not he is cross-examined on a later occasion, if the issue is unfounded and the monk is motivated by anger it entails initial and subsequent meetings of the community."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu, corrupted by malice [and] upset, should accuse a bhikkhu with a groundless case involving disqualification [thinking]: 'If only I could make him fall away from this holy life!,' [and] then, on another occasion, [whether] being interrogated or not being interrogated, if that legal issue is really groundless, and if the bhikkhu stands firm in malice: [this is a case] involving the community in the beginning and in the rest."6

    The five renderings agree on the offence and part on two points. The first is the motive, "angry and displeased" for Brahmāli, "corrupt, aversive, disgruntled" for Thanissaro, "corrupted by malice [and] upset" for Ñāṇatusita; whether that anger is a factor of its own is taken up under Intention. The second is the closing clause, which the recitation leaves out. Brahmāli's monk "admits to his ill will" and Thanissaro's "confesses his aversion", both following the Vibhaṅga's gloss of the clause as an admission; Ñāṇatusita's "stands firm in malice" takes the verb in its ordinary sense; and Ajahn Brahm's version, otherwise close to Thanissaro's wording, ends "motivated by anger". The reading changes what a Community must establish, and is discussed below, under Effort.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule protects the way the Saṅgha settles a charge against one of its own, and two things in the Story bear on it. The first is the Buddha's way with Dabba. Asked three times whether he remembered doing what Mettiyā said, Dabba answered only, "Sir, you know what I'm like", and the Buddha would not take that for an answer: "If it was done by you, say so; if it wasn't, then say that."7 Dabba denied it, and the Buddha exonerated him, Brahmāli writes, "on the basis of his perfect memory", which is how resolution through recollection, sativinaya, came to be established among the principles for settling legal issues.8 Later, Thanissaro notes, the Buddha put the Saṅgha in charge of judging accusations of this kind and gave it a definite pattern to follow, so that its judgments would be as fair and accurate as possible.9 The penalty is heavy even for a charge that convinces no one: whether anyone believes it is not a factor.10 The second thing in the Story is Mettiyā's expulsion, which raises a question of its own, since she had said only what two monks told her to say, and her conduct, as Brahmāli puts it, was at most grounds for a saṅghādisesa. He gives Anālayo's suggestion that the Mahāsāṅghika Vinaya supplies the answer, for there the nun was already pregnant by someone else.11 Thanissaro credits the Commentary with a different defence: treated less harshly, the monks behind her would never have come forward, and some would have stayed convinced of an arahant's guilt, to their own long harm.12 In the Story itself, it was once she had been expelled that the two spoke up for her, and the truth came out.13

    The rule sits inside the procedure the Vinaya lays down for every accusation. Before admonishing another, a monk should know himself pure in body and speech, motivated by goodwill, learned in the Teaching and versed in both Pātimokkhas in detail; he resolves to speak at the right time, truthfully, gently, to the point and from goodwill; and he keeps in mind compassion, concern for the other's welfare, sympathy, a wish to see him rehabilitated, and esteem for the Vinaya.14​ 15 He then asks the other's leave to speak, at a fitting time and place: to accuse him without asking is a dukkaṭa, and so is asking leave with no grounds at all.16 The one asked may assess the asker and refuse an unqualified or abusive one, but should willingly give leave to one who is neither.17 A monk who knows of another's offence and does not feel qualified to raise it should inform one who is, since to tell no one may be a pācittiya under Pc 64, and a monk who reports genuine grounds to the senior monk from that duty, and not from malice, has no offence here.18​ 19 A matter not settled between the two goes to a meeting of the Community. This rule covers the abuse of that system, the charge with no ground at all.

    The offence has four factors in Thanissaro's analysis: object, perception, intention and effort.20 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual divides the same ground into seven points, two of which Thanissaro does not accept: anger as a factor in its own right, and the accused's immediate understanding of the charge.21 Thanissaro's four are the frame here, and the two he does not accept come up where the sources divide over them.

    Object

    The accused must be a monk, which Thanissaro reports the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī as taking to mean one regarded as ordained: a monk who has secretly committed a pārājika and goes on living as a monk is still the object of this rule if another monk later charges him groundlessly with a different one.22 The charge is of one of the four pārājikas, and the Commentary extends it to the equivalent pārājikas and to the persons who could never be validly ordained at all: to accuse a monk without ground of having killed his father or mother before his ordination is the full offence. The Vibhaṅga does not mention these; Thanissaro finds them justified by the Great Standards.23​ 24

    Perception

    The accuser must perceive the accused as innocent of what he charges him with, and whether the accused is in fact innocent makes no difference; to accuse him on the assumption or suspicion that he is not innocent is no offence.25 The Vibhaṅga sets this out in a grid of four, a monk pure or impure, viewed as pure or impure: to view him as pure and speak aiming to make him leave is the offence whether he is pure or not, and to view him as impure is no offence here, whether or not he is. Asking his leave first removes only the dukkaṭa that rides alongside, and a monk who speaks to abuse him, not to expel him, commits the offence for abusive speech instead.26 A charge with no ground, Ñāṇatusita notes, is still groundless if the accused has in fact, by chance, committed a pārājika.27

    Ajahn Brahm gives the pair of cases that teaches the point. A teacher, angry with his student and wanting him gone, seizes on the disappearance of a valuable bowl to accuse the student of stealing it; to the teacher's surprise the student admits it and disrobes, and the teacher still undergoes the penance for a saṅghādisesa, for his charge had no ground when he made it. The same teacher, seeing the student take the bowl and hide it, honestly reports what he saw, hoping it will rid him of the student; at the inquiry the bowl proves to have been taken on trust, the student is cleared, and the teacher has no offence.28 What decides the case is what the accuser knew, whatever the fact; and even a wish to be rid of him does not make an honest report this offence.

    Intention

    The accuser must aim to make the accused leave the monastic life, by disrobing or by expulsion.29 An aim only to insult him falls under Pc 2, and both aims together incur both offences.30 A monk who, angry with another, accuses him without seeing that the charge could cost him his robes has no offence here, though he may have one under Pc 76 or Pc 77.31

    Disputed — whether anger is a factor in its own right, or folded into the aim.

    The rule names it at both ends, "angry and displeased" at the start and the admission of ill will at the close. Thanissaro holds that the Vibhaṅga consistently folds the impulse into the motive, so that all this factor needs is the wish to see the accused expelled; the malice behind it need not be severe, and wanting him gone "just for the fun of it" would fulfil it.32 The Manual answers him by name: the Vibhaṅga gives no clear decision either way, and anger is so plainly part of the rule that this "must take precedence".33 Ñāṇatusita reads the rule's closing conditions as a groundless case and a monk who has gone wrong through ill will, which he calls the factor of intention, and Ajahn Brahm requires malice, though of any degree.34​ 35 They part only on a charge with the aim and no ill will behind it. The Vibhaṅga gives no case of one. In practice the gap is narrow, since the stricter reading asks for ill will of any degree and a wish to see a fellow monk expelled is hard to hold without some; that is an observation about monks, not a reading of the text.

    The joke, with no aim to expel, falls outside this rule on both readings. Thanissaro thinks, with a hedge, that it comes under Pc 1; Ajahn Brahm, who does not raise Pc 1, finds no offence here, though such speech is unsuitable for any monk.36​ 37

    Effort

    The charge may be made by the monk himself or through another, as in the origin story, at the same penalty; and if the one who passes it on is a monk who knows it to be unfounded, he incurs the full penalty too.38 Groundless means not seen, not heard and not suspected,39 and the Commentary counts what is seen or heard through clairvoyance or clairaudience.40 A charge with some ground is still groundless if its status is changed. The Vibhaṅga works through every exchange of "I've seen", "I've heard" and "I suspect", so that a monk who heard and charges it as seen, or suspected and charges it as heard, commits the offence for each statement; and the same holds for one who did see but is unsure of what he saw, does not believe it, does not remember it or is confused about it, and charges it as seen.41 In the Commentary's examples, a monk and a woman seen leaving the same grove become a monk seen having sex, and a night thief who looks vaguely like a certain monk becomes that monk, definitely seen.42

    A charge must say outright what was done: name the act, name the pārājika, or declare that he is no longer a true monk. To withhold the respect due to his seniority may imply it, and is not yet a charge.43 By the Commentary's account it may be made by gesture, as by pointing when asked who did it, but not in writing, which Thanissaro suspects was then thought too cowardly to take seriously.44 A monk giving evidence in the Community's investigation of another's charge, whose statement amounts to a groundless charge of pārājika aimed at the accused's expulsion, commits the offence as well.45

    Disputed — whether the charge must be made in the accused's presence, and whether he must understand it at the time; two questions, on which the sources line up differently.

    On presence, the Commentary concludes from the Vibhaṅga's examples, all spoken to the accused's face, that a charge behind his back is only a dukkaṭa; Thanissaro takes presence into his factor of effort; and the Manual reaches it from the same Vibhaṅga passage and from reading the rule's verbs as admonishing, which is done face to face.46​ 47 Ajahn Brahm calls the requirement an "unreasonable judgement" that neither the rule nor the Vibhaṅga supports, and holds that a charge made to anyone who understands it is enough.48 On understanding, the line-up changes. The Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī, which the Manual cites for it, requires that the accused understand at the time; Brahmāli agrees, since an admonition is accomplished only when understood, and the requirement separates a real charge from something muttered to let off steam; and Ajahn Brahm, extending the Samantapāsādikā's pattern from Pj 1, gives a thullaccaya where the charge is heard but not immediately understood and a dukkaṭa where it is not heard at all.49​ 50 Thanissaro holds that nothing in the Vibhaṅga supports this added factor.51 To the objection that the presence requirement lets a character assassination behind a monk's back off lightly, Thanissaro notes that such a charge may still be a pācittiya for deliberate lying, and answers that a monk spreading it should be told to take it up with the accused or with those who would call a meeting, and if he refuses, should not be listened to.52 The texts settle neither question; a Community facing such a case must choose, and should know it.

    Understanding at the time is not this rule's requirement alone. Ajahn Brahm traces it to a pattern the Commentary first sets out at Pj 1, on renouncing the training, and applies at Pj 4, Sg 3 and Sg 4 as well; the requirement is disputed at Pj 4 and Sg 4, as it is here.53

    Disputed — what the rule's closing clause asks for: the accuser's admission, or ill will shown to be there.

    Both readings put the offence at the charge: the Vibhaṅga counts one "for each statement", and the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī places the saṅghādisesa "at the very moment of accusation".54​ 55 Thanissaro gives the Commentary's reading, on which the closing clause marks when the rest of the Community knows it. The Vibhaṅga glosses it as an admission, "What I said was empty," "What I said was false," "I said it without knowing," and Brahmāli's and Thanissaro's renderings follow the gloss.56​ 57 Ñāṇatusita reads the verb, patiṭṭhāti, in its usual sense, "stands firm in": it takes a locative everywhere else in the Canon and in the other schools' Prātimokṣasūtras, and cannot take "fault" as an object, as an admission would need. On his reading the clause restates the offence's two conditions, a groundless case and a monk acting from ill will, and "as in the Aniyata rules, the bhikkhu's admission is not necessary", though he must still admit it to undergo the penance.58 The readings part on what a Community needs to find the offence: his admission, or ill will shown by the evidence. The tradition largely follows the gloss, as I do, and Ñāṇatusita himself allows a causative reading that would support it; but the grammatical case is real, and the tradition's following is no answer to it.

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence in charging a monk honestly on what one has seen, heard or suspected, viewing him as impure, whether or not he proves to be; even so, to make the charge without asking his leave is a dukkaṭa, and to make it so as to insult him is a pācittiya.59

    When he is himself accused, a monk is to keep two qualities in mind: truth and staying unprovoked.60 It is what Dabba did.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — accusing without first asking leave, alongside the saṅghādisesa where the charge is groundless and alone where the accuser views the accused as impure; asking leave with no grounds; by the Commentary, a groundless charge made behind the accused's back.61​ 62​ 63
    • Thullaccaya, dukkaṭa — by Ajahn Brahm's extension of the Samantapāsādikā's pattern from Pj 1, a charge heard but not immediately understood, and one not heard at all (disputed above).64
    • Pācittiya — speaking with the aim of abusing the accused, not expelling him (Pc 2), with a dukkaṭa besides if leave was not asked; a groundless charge behind his back, as deliberate lying (Pc 1), by Thanissaro's reading.65​ 66

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he views a pure person as impure; if he views an impure person as impure; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.67

    Related Offences

    • Sg 9 — the same charge built on a pretext, some point taken from an unrelated matter.
    • Pc 76 — a groundless charge of a saṅghādisesa.
    • Pc 2 — abusive speech, where the aim is to insult.
    • Pc 64 — concealing another monk's serious offence, the duty behind an honest report.
    • Sg 12, Pc 12, Pc 54, Pc 71 — how the one admonished must conduct himself.
    • Pj 1, Pj 4, Sg 3, Sg 4 — the rules whose pattern, as Ajahn Brahm reports the Commentary, ties the offence to the hearer's understanding at the time.

    Related Suttas

    • AN 5.167 — Sāriputta's five things to establish in oneself before reproving another, and the remorse to be stirred in one who reproves falsely, "so that another bhikkhu would not think of reproving falsely"; and the two in which the one reproved should be established, "in truth and non-anger"; Dabba, asked to say plainly whether he had done it, met both.68
    • Snp 3.10 — Kokālika, who would not stop accusing Sāriputta and Moggallāna of evil desires, and the harm a false accuser does himself: "When a person has taken birth / an axe is born inside his mouth / with which the fool cuts himself / by uttering wrongful speech."69
    • MN 58 — "such speech as the Tathāgata knows to be untrue, incorrect, and unbeneficial … such speech the Tathāgata does not utter"; a groundless charge is all three.70

Ñāṇatusita, Analysis of the Bhikkhu Pātimokkha, Sd 8, printed pp. 65–68: "patiṭṭhāti: stands firm in, establishes, bases on, persists"; quoting Kkh 72: "for that bhikkhu there is a saṅghādisesa offence at the very moment of accusation"; "Patiṭṭhāti is an intransitive verb and cannot take dosaṃ as a patient, therefore the meanings 'confirms,' or 'admits' or 'confesses' don't make sense. Elsewhere in the Pali Canon patiṭṭhāti only takes a locative … and it is significant that the Prātimokṣasūtras … all have a locative form here"; and "In the initial part the two factors of dosa and amūlaka are introduced, then, in the last part, the two are again stated as defining factors. Therefore, as in the Aniyata rules, the bhikkhu's admission is not necessary to make it a Sd offence. In this case a misunderstanding about this rule would have occured before the Padabhājana was established"; and "The point is that the bhikkhu commits the Sd offence as soon as he makes the false accusation. He has to admit though that he has made a false accusation under the influence of anger in order to undergo the mānatta and parivāsa."↩︎

Thanissaro, BMC1, Sg 8, Effort: "There is nothing in the Vibhaṅga to indicate that the Commentary is wrong here, aside from the consideration that—because the charge is unfounded—it could entail a pācittiya for deliberate lying. Some people, however, have objected to the Commentary's position here, saying that a dukkaṭa or even a pācittiya is a very light penalty for backhanded character assassination. Nevertheless, we should remember that the correct procedures for making an accusation require that an earnest charge be made in the presence of the accused. If a bhikkhu spreads gossip about another bhikkhu, accusing him of having committed a pārājika, he should be asked whether he has taken up the matter with the accused. If he hasn't, he should be told to speak to the accused before he speaks to anyone else. If he says that he doesn't feel qualified or that he fears the accused will retaliate, he should be told to take the matter up with the bhikkhus who will be responsible for calling a meeting of the Community. If he refuses to do that, he shouldn't be listened to."↩︎
