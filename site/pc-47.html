    Pc 47

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying in the Sakyan country in the Banyan Tree Monastery at Kapilavatthu. Mahānāma the Sakyan had an abundance of tonics. He went to the Buddha, bowed, sat down, and said, "Sir, I wish to invite the Sangha to ask for tonics for four months."

    "Good, good, Mahānāma. Please do so."

    But the monks were afraid of wrongdoing and did not accept. They told the Buddha, and he allowed them to accept an invitation to ask for tonics for four months. Yet the monks only asked Mahānāma for a small amount, and so he still had an abundance. A second time he came and asked to invite the Saṅgha for a further four months; the monks again did not accept; the Buddha allowed a further invitation. A third time he came and asked to invite the Saṅgha for life; the monks again did not accept; the Buddha allowed a permanent invitation.

    At that time the monks from the group of six were shabbily dressed and improper in appearance. Mahānāma criticised them, "Venerables, why are you shabbily dressed and improper in appearance? Shouldn't one who has gone forth be suitably dressed and proper in appearance?"

    The monks from the group of six developed a grudge against Mahānāma. Thinking of ways to humiliate him, it occurred to them, "Mahānāma has invited the Sangha to ask for tonics. Let's ask him for ghee." They went to him and said, "We need a doṇa measure of ghee."1

    "Please wait until tomorrow. People have gone to the cow-pen to get ghee. You may come and get it in the morning."

    A second time and a third time they said the same thing, and Mahānāma replied as before. They then said, "Why do you give an invitation if you don't wish to give?"

    Mahānāma complained and criticised them, "How can they not wait for one day when asked?" The monks of few desires complained and criticised those monks, the Buddha questioned them, established that it was true, rebuked them, and stated the rule.2​ 3

    Synopsis — A layman with an abundance of tonics has to ask three times, and be granted three separate allowances, before monks too scrupulous to accept will take what he is trying to give. Then six monks he has politely corrected about their robes come back at him with a demand for a doṇa of ghee on the spot — an invitation used as a weapon — and will not wait the one day he asks for. The rule is the answer to the second half of the story, and the whole point of the first half is what it has to protect.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "A monk who is not sick may accept an invitation to ask for requisites for four months. If he accepts one beyond that limit, except if it is a further invitation or a permanent invitation, he commits an offense entailing confession."4

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a healthy bhikkhu who has been invited to ask for tonics or medicines goes beyond the terms of the invitation, he incurs a pācittiya."5

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "A bhikkhu who is not ill may accept (make use of) a four-month invitation to ask for requisites. If he should accept (make use of) it beyond that — unless the invitation is renewed or is permanent — it is to be confessed."6

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Exceeding the terms of a Sangha-pavāranā for medicines is an offence of Pacittiya."7

    Ñāṇatusita

    "By a bhikkhu who is not ill a four-month invitation for requisites can be accepted; except with a repeated invitation, except with a permanent invitation; if he should accept more than that, [this is a case] involving expiation."8

    The renderings divide on one word and compress the rule in different ways. The word is paccaya, in the rule's compound for a four-month invitation for requisites. Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation, Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita render it "requisites"; Brahmāli's recitation says "tonics or medicines", and Ajahn Brahm says "medicines".9​ 10​ 11 Two lines also replace the rule's four months, further invitation and permanent invitation with "the terms of the invitation", which is the Vibhaṅga's later schema rather than the rule's words: the recitation says "goes beyond the terms", and Ajahn Brahm's says "exceeding the terms" and makes the invitation the Saṅgha's. Whether the rule is about medicine or about requisites of every kind is a real question, and Object carries it.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    An invitation to ask is a donor's trust laid open, and this rule holds a monk to moderation in using it, so that a generous layman is never made to regret his generosity and the confidence of the givers is kept. The story has two halves. A layman with an abundance of tonics has to ask three times before monks too scrupulous to accept will take what he offers. Then six monks he has corrected for their shabby robes demand a doṇa of ghee on the spot and will not wait the one day he asks for: "Why do you give an invitation if you don't wish to give?"12​ 13 The rule answers the second half, and the first half shows what it protects. Thanissaro describes the arrangement: the donor was expected to provide what he offered, and the monks to be reasonable in what they asked.14 The rule guards the second expectation.

    Thanissaro gives two factors, object and effort.15

    Object

    The object is what a donor has invited the Saṅgha to ask for, and two questions arise about it: what kind of thing, and whose invitation.

    Disputed — whether paccaya, the rule's word, means medicine or any requisite.

    The Vibhaṅga's whole analysis is about tonics, and its story is about ghee.16 Brahmāli's endnote argues that the rule is broader than its analysis. Used alone, paccaya never means medicine in the Vinaya or the four main Nikāyas; it means support, and since the rules existed before the Vibhaṅga, "the meaning of the rule is independent of it."17 The sutta he cites, AN 4.79, bears him out, since there a donor "invites him to ask for what he needs", and nothing narrows it to medicine.18 Ñāṇatusita reads the word the same way, as one of the four requisites.19 On the other side, Thanissaro holds that the Vibhaṅga's discussion assumes the rule applies to medicine, and rejects the Vinaya-mukha's extension to any requisite as without support (below); Ajahn Brahm and Brahmāli's own recitation line likewise read the rule as about medicine.20​ 21​ 22 The offence is committed in the same way on either reading. What changes is the reach: whether an invitation to ask for robes or lodgings falls under this rule, or only under the general restraint of NP 6, NP 7 and Sg 6. I follow the Vibhaṅga for the offence, and find Brahmāli's argument the better account of the rule's words; a monk who keeps to the terms of any invitation, for robes or lodgings as much as tonics, and asks only for what he needs, is safe on both.

    Whose invitation the rule covers the Vibhaṅga does not say. Thanissaro reports the Commentary restricting it to an invitation made to the Saṅgha, reading the non-offence clause "those who have given an invitation" as personal invitations, which a monk may use according to their terms. Thanissaro finds this the best way to make sense of the clause and gives the reason the penalty requires it. Asking for a tonic uninvited when not ill is only a dukkaṭa under Pc 39, so there is no sense in a heavier penalty for asking after a personal invitation has run out; for an invitation to the whole Community, the heavier penalty protects the donor from its less conscientious members.23 Ajahn Brahm and the Concise BMC take the same reading.24​ 25 The Vinaya-mukha, in his account, extends the rule to every invitation, personal or communal and for any sort of requisite, and assumes four months where no limit is stated. Thanissaro rejects this as without support in the Vibhaṅga, though he suggests the four-month default as a personal policy for a monk teaching himself moderation.26

    Effort

    The effort is asking outside the terms of the invitation when not ill. The rule names three forms of invitation, for four months, renewed and permanent, and Thanissaro suggests four months because each of India's seasons lasts that long.27 The Vibhaṅga then sets this aside and works out a schema for any invitation: one may limit the tonics, the time, both or neither. Asking outside a stated limit is a pācittiya, and where neither is stated "there is no offense."28 Two further offences concern honesty, not limits. Asking for tonics when one has no need of them is a pācittiya, and so is asking for one tonic when one needs another, as a monk whose illness calls for a ghee concoction who asks for honey. Both are committed in the asking, whether or not anything is received.29​ 30 Perception does not mitigate, the pattern of Pc 4.31

    Not ill the Vibhaṅga leaves undefined. The Commentary's test, which Thanissaro gives, is practical: a monk who can get by on "mixed" food, food gathered at random on almsround, has no need of medicine.32 Ajahn Brahm adds that although sickness can mean little more than discomfort, a frugal monk begs only in dire need, but having been invited he is more likely to make good use of medicines in less extreme circumstances.33

    On Thanissaro's reading, the sick monk's exemption has an unexpected shape. It does not say "no offence if he is ill"; it gives him words to say: "The time period during which you invited me to ask has passed, but I need tonics."34 Thanissaro calls it a point of etiquette. By naming the lapse, the monk acknowledges that the donor is no longer bound and gives him a courteous way out, the least consideration owed to someone generous enough to invite the Community.35

    The real difficulty is that most invitations state no terms at all. Brahmāli's counsel is to be sensitive to the donor. "If you ever need anything, please ask me" may well be in doubt a year later, and the remedy is to ask whether it still stands, to which the answer will very likely be yes.36 Ajahn Brahm's examples run the other way, with the letter kept and the spirit broken: the group of six's ghee; Upananda at NP 6 asking a young man for the robes he was wearing; and a Western Buddhist in Thailand who offered four pilgrim monks the next leg of their journey and was asked for four expensive plane tickets to the other end of the country. His warning is the thing to carry away from the rule: "it is not uncommon for insensitive monks to exploit the time, energy and material resources of a generous layperson to the point where they lose their faith in monks and maybe even in Buddhism."37

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — where the request is not beyond the limit but he perceives that it is, or is unsure of it. Where it is beyond the limit, all three perceptions carry the full pācittiya.38
    • Pācittiya — asking for tonics with no need of them, or for a tonic other than the one needed, incurred in the asking.39

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he asks for those tonics for which he was invited to ask; if he asks during the time period for which he was invited to ask; if he asks by informing, "You have invited me to ask for these tonics, but I need such-and-such a tonic"; if he asks by informing, "The time period during which you invited me to ask has passed, but I need tonics"; if it is from relatives; if it is from those who have given an invitation; if it is for the benefit of someone else; if it is by means of his own property; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.40

    Thanissaro gives the Commentary's reading of "from those who have given an invitation" as a personal invitation, which this rule does not govern.41 The time-period clause he takes to be for the sick monk, and the Great Standards, he suggests, could extend it to an invitation that names requisites.42​ 43 Relatives are reckoned through seven generations, as at NP 4.44

    Related Offences

    • Pc 39 — asking for the five tonics when not ill and not invited: a dukkaṭa, and the comparison on which Thanissaro's Saṅgha reading rests.
    • NP 6, NP 7 — asking a non-relative for robe-cloth, and accepting more than two robes when invited to take many: the same abuse of generosity, for robes.
    • NP 22, Pc 86 — invitations to ask for a bowl and a needle case: Ajahn Brahm's evidence that invitations for other requisites exist.
    • Sg 6 — the rule on a hut built by begging, whose origin story turns on immoderate asking.

    Related Suttas

    • AN 4.79 — the donor who "invites him to ask for what he needs" and then does not give it, and whose business in a later life ends in failure: the rule asks the matching restraint of the monk, so that an invitation stays one the donor can keep.45
