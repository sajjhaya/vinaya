    Sg 13

    Story (Vatthu)

    At one time the Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time the bad and shameless monks Assaji and Punabbasuka were staying at Kīṭāgiri, and they were misbehaving in many ways. They planted flowering trees, watered and plucked them, and tied the flowers together; they made garlands, flower arrangements, wreaths and ornaments for the head, the ears and the chest, and had others do the same, and they took these, or sent them, to the women, the daughters, the girls, the daughters-in-law and the female slaves of respectable families. They ate from the same plates as these women and drank from the same vessels; they sat on the same seats and lay down on the same beds, on the same sheets and under the same covers. They ate at the wrong time, drank alcohol, and wore garlands, perfumes and cosmetics. They danced, sang, played instruments and performed, and when the women danced, sang, played and performed, so did they. They played games of every kind, checkers, hopscotch, pick-up-sticks, dice, leaf flutes, toy plows, somersaults, pinwheels, guessing games and mimicking deformities. They trained in elephant riding, horsemanship, carriage riding, archery and swordsmanship; they ran in front of elephants, horses and carriages, and ran backward and forward; they whistled, clapped their hands, wrestled and boxed. They spread their outer robes on a stage and said to the dancing girls, "Dance here, sister," and made gestures of approval.

    Just then a monk who had completed the rainy-season residence in Kāsi was on his way to visit the Buddha at Sāvatthī when he arrived at Kīṭāgiri. In the morning he entered the town for almsfood. He was pleasing in his conduct, in going out and coming back, in looking ahead and aside, in bending and stretching his arms; his eyes were lowered, and he was perfect in deportment. When people saw him, they said, "Who's this, acting like a moron and always frowning? Who's gonna give almsfood to him? Almsfood should be given to our Venerables Assaji and Punabbasuka, for they're gentle, congenial, pleasant to speak with, greeting one with a smile, welcoming, friendly, open, the first to speak."

    A certain lay follower saw that monk walking for alms, took him to his house and gave him a meal. Learning that he was going to see the Buddha, he asked him to pay respect at the Buddha's feet in his name and say, "Sir, the monastery at Kīṭāgiri has been corrupted. The bad and shameless monks Assaji and Punabbasuka are staying there. And they're misbehaving in many ways. … Those who previously had faith and confidence have now lost it, and there's no longer any support for the Sangha. The good monks have left and the bad monks are staying on. Sir, please send monks to stay at the monastery at Kīṭāgiri."

    The monk went to Sāvatthī and told the Buddha all that had happened. The Buddha had the Saṅgha gathered, questioned the monks and rebuked Assaji and Punabbasuka. Then he addressed Sāriputta and Moggallāna: "Go to Kīṭāgiri and do a legal procedure of banishment from Kīṭāgiri against the monks Assaji and Punabbasuka. They're your students." "Sir, how can we do a procedure of banishment against these monks? They're temperamental and harsh." "Well then, take many monks." The Buddha set out how it was to be done, and a sangha of monks headed by Sāriputta and Moggallāna went to Kīṭāgiri and banished them.

    Even so, Assaji and Punabbasuka did not conduct themselves properly so as to deserve to be released, nor did they ask the monks for forgiveness. Instead they abused and reviled them, and they slandered them as acting from favoritism, ill will, confusion, and fear. And they left and they disrobed. The monks of few desires complained and criticised them: "How can these monks act like this when the Sangha has done a legal procedure of banishment against them?" They told the Buddha, who questioned the monks, rebuked them and laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — Assaji and Punabbasuka win over the families of Kīṭāgiri by flattery and frivolity until the people turn away a well-conducted monk, and when the Saṅgha banishes them they accuse it of bias; the Buddha lays down the rule that a monk who criticises his banishment is to be corrected, and offends only if he will not stop.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk who lives supported by a village or town is a corrupter of families and badly behaved, and his bad behavior has been seen and heard about, and the families corrupted by him have been seen and heard about, then the monks should correct him like this: 'Venerable, you're a corrupter of families and badly behaved. Your bad behavior has been seen and heard about, and the families corrupted by you have been seen and heard about. Leave this monastery; you've stayed here long enough.' If he replies, 'You're acting out of favoritism, ill will, confusion, and fear. Because of this sort of offense, you only banish some, but not others,' then the monks should correct him like this: 'No, venerable, the monks are not acting out of favoritism, ill will, confusion, and fear. Venerable, you're a corrupter of families and badly behaved. Your bad behavior has been seen and heard about, and the families corrupted by you have been seen and heard about. Leave this monastery; you've stayed here long enough.' If that monk continues as before, the monks should press him up to three times to make him stop. If he then stops, all is well. If he does not stop, he commits an offense entailing suspension."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu has corrupted the householders in a certain place, the Saṅgha should officially banish him from that place. If he criticises this act of banishment, he should be admonished up to three times to make him stop. If he does not stop, he incurs a saṅghādisesa."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "In case a bhikkhu living in dependence on a certain village or town is a corrupter of families, a man of depraved conduct—whose depraved conduct is both seen and heard about, and the families he has corrupted are both seen and heard about—the bhikkhus are to admonish him thus: 'You, venerable sir, are a corrupter of families, a man of depraved conduct. Your depraved conduct is both seen and heard about, and the families you have corrupted are both seen and heard about. Leave this monastery, venerable sir. Enough of your staying here.' And should that bhikkhu, thus admonished by the bhikkhus, say about the bhikkhus, 'The bhikkhus are biased through desire, biased through aversion, biased through delusion, biased through fear, in that for this sort of offense they banish some and do not banish others,' the bhikkhus are to admonish him thus: 'Do not say that, venerable sir. The bhikkhus are not biased through desire, are not biased through aversion, are not biased through delusion, are not biased through fear. You, venerable sir, are a corrupter of families, a man of depraved conduct. Your depraved conduct is both seen and heard about, and the families you have corrupted are both seen and heard about. Leave this monastery, venerable sir. Enough of your staying here.' And should that bhikkhu, thus admonished by the bhikkhus, persist as before, the bhikkhus are to rebuke him up to three times for the sake of relinquishing that. If while being rebuked up to three times he relinquishes that, that is good. If he does not relinquish (that), it entails initial and subsequent meetings of the Community."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "In case a monk living in dependence on a certain village or town is a corrupter of families, a man of depraved conduct -- whose depraved conduct is both seen and heard about -- he is to be admonished thus by the monks: 'You, Venerable Sir, are a corrupter of families, a man of depraved conduct. Your depraved conduct is both seen and heard about, the families corrupted by you are both seen and heard about. Leave this monastery, Venerable Sir. Enough of your staying here'. And should that monk, thus admonished by the monks, say about the monks, 'The monks are prejudiced by favouritism, prejudiced by aversion, prejudiced by delusion, prejudiced by fear, in that for this sort of offence they banish some and do not banish others', he is to be admonished thus by the monks: 'Do not say that Venerable Sir, the monks are not prejudiced by favouritism, are not prejudiced by aversion, are not prejudiced by delusion, are not prejudiced by fear. You, Venerable Sir, are a corrupter of families, a man of depraved conduct. Your depraved conduct is both seen and heard about, and the families corrupted by you are both seen and heard about. Leave this monastery, Venerable Sir. Enough of your staying here'. And should that monk, thus admonished by the monks, persist as before, he is to be formally admonished up to three times so as to desist. If, being formally admonished three times by the monks, he should desist, that is good. If he should not desist, it entails initial and subsequent meetings of the community."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "Now, a bhikkhu lives dependent upon a certain village or town who is a spoiler of families, who is of bad behaviour. His bad behaviour is seen and is heard about, and the families spoilt by him are seen and heard about. That bhikkhu is to be spoken to thus by the bhikkhus: 'The venerable one is a spoiler of families, one who is of bad behaviour. The bad behaviour of the venerable one is seen and is heard about, and the families spoilt by the venerable one are seen and are heard about. Let the venerable one depart from this dwelling-place! Enough of you dwelling here!' and [if] that bhikkhu being spoken to thus by the bhikkhus should say thus to those bhikkhus: 'The bhikkhus are driven by desire; the bhikkhus are driven by anger; the bhikkhus are driven by delusion; the bhikkhus are driven by fear. They banish one because of this kind of offence, [but another] one they do not banish.' [Then] that bhikkhu is to be spoken to thus by the bhikkhus: 'Venerable, one not speak thus! The bhikkhus are not driven by desire; and the bhikkhus are not driven by anger; and the bhikkhus are not driven by delusion; and the bhikkhus are not driven by fear. The venerable one is a spoiler of families, one who is of bad behaviour. The bad behaviour of the venerable one is seen and is heard about, and the families spoilt by the venerable one are seen and are heard about. Let the venerable one depart from this dwelling-place! Enough of you dwelling here!' and [if] that bhikkhu being spoken to thus by the bhikkhus should persist in the same way [as before], [then] that bhikkhu is to be argued with up to three times by the bhikkhus for the relinquishing of that [course], [and if that bhikkhu,] being argued with up to three times, should relinquish that [course], then this is good, [but] if he should not relinquish [it]: [this is a case] involving the community in the beginning and in the rest."6

    The full renderings agree, and Ajahn Brahm's, otherwise close to Thanissaro's, drops the families from the first description of the monk. The recitation recasts the rule in a way that matters. The rule names the banishment only in the corrupter's accusation, the monks simply telling him to leave; the recitation has "the Saṅgha should officially banish him", writing into the rule the formal act that the Vibhaṅga's own motion presupposes, a monk "who has had a legal procedure of banishment done against him". The offence is thus plainly the criticism of a banishment already done, and that act is the object of the offence.7

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule protects the Saṅgha's remedy for a corrupter of families, and with it the faith that remedy is meant to win back. The Story measures the harm by its effect. A monk perfect in deportment walks for alms and is mocked, while the two who flatter and amuse the families are praised. The lay follower's message names the loss: those who had faith have lost it, the Saṅgha has no support, and "the good monks have left and the bad monks are staying on."8 It reads as the reverse of the Vinaya's own reasons for a training rule, among them "to give rise to confidence in those without it, to increase the confidence of those who have it" and "for the ease of good monks".9 The Saṅgha's remedy is to banish the corrupter from the place he has corrupted. A banished monk who answers it by accusing the Saṅgha of favouritism, ill will, confusion and fear attacks the means by which the Saṅgha restores the laity's faith, and the rule meets him, as Sg 10 meets the schismatic, with correction before any penalty.

    Two acts of the Saṅgha are involved, and the offence lies in the second. The first is the banishment, which belongs to the Khandhakas; the second is the pressing, which is Sg 10's procedure, offences, validity grid and annulment.10​ 11 Thanissaro's analysis has one factor, effort: criticising a valid act of banishment imposed on oneself, or those who imposed it, even when rebuked three times in a properly performed act.12 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual sets the corruption and the banishment before it as factors of their own, and adds that the criticism is made "without good reason."13 I take Thanissaro's single factor here, since the corruption is not itself this offence, and treat the banishment it presupposes as the object.

    Object

    The object is a valid act of banishment for corrupting families. The Vibhaṅga lists the means of corruption: gifts of flowers, fruit, bath powder, soap, tooth cleaners and bamboo, medical treatment, and taking messages on foot; bad behaviour it defines as planting, watering and plucking flowering trees and tying the flowers, oneself or through others.14 Thanissaro reports that the Commentary reads this as shorthand for the Story's whole catalogue, and allows a monk to carry messages connected with the religious life, such as an invitation to a meal or a layperson's respects to a senior monk.15 It also distinguishes, on his report, three purposes in watering a plant: to make gifts of its flowers or fruit is a dukkaṭa, even through others; for one's own or the Community's use of them, a monk may not water it himself, though he may prompt another in allowable language; and for its shade or as part of a garden or forest, he may water it himself, provided the water has no living beings in it.16 Medical treatment has a like limit. The Commentary, as Thanissaro reports it, lets a monk treat only certain lay people: his parents and those who care for them, his blood relatives, his preceptor's and teacher's parents and other blood relatives, applicants for ordination, his own steward, and travellers who arrive ill at the monastery or people who fall ill there; even for them he may expect no material reward. His fellow monks are another matter: Thanissaro adds that a monk is expected to know enough medicine to care for their illnesses.17 Any one of these acts of corruption carries only a light penalty, a dukkaṭa or a pācittiya; habitual, and "seen and heard about", they are grounds for banishment.18

    The families are corrupted when they have lost their faith and confidence because of him.19 Thanissaro describes the corrupter as one who ingratiates himself until the laity withdraw their support from earnest monks and give it to those more ingratiating, which is what the Story shows; the Manual keeps to the Vibhaṅga, and it is the loss of faith that the rule requires.20​ 21

    The banishment itself is a formal act of one motion and three announcements, which the Cullavagga sets out from this same story. It allows a Community to banish a monk for nine kinds of fault, among them making strife in the Community and living in unbecoming association with householders, and, last, wrong livelihood: running errands for kings and ministers, pursuing gain with gain, and the worldly arts, fortune-telling, astrology, charms, any art "external and unconnected with the goal."22 Thanissaro gives the one modern instance, and it shows how the old list is to be read: running errands for the powerful today would include taking part in political campaigns.23 A banished monk must keep the observances the Cullavagga sets and mend his ways so that the Saṅgha will lift the act. By the Commentary he leaves the neighbourhood as well as the monastery, and even after the act is lifted he refuses gifts from the families he corrupted, unless they explain that they give because he has reformed.24​ 25

    Effort

    Two of the banished monk's duties are that he not criticise the act of banishment or those who performed it, and it is by breaking either, and not stopping, that he comes under this rule.26 The charge in the Story and the rule is a specific one: that the monks act out of favouritism, ill will, confusion and fear, banishing some for the offence and not others. The edge is the validity of the banishment. Thanissaro's factor names "a valid act of banishment", and the Manual a criticism "without good reason"; a monk banished by an act that was not properly done is not the monk this rule describes.27​ 28

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    A monk who will not see the offence or undergo the penalty can, as under the three rules before this one, be suspended.29

    After all it says of Assaji and Punabbasuka, the lay follower's message closes on a request. He had seen a well-conducted monk turned away, gave him a meal, and, learning that he was going to see the Buddha, asked him to carry it: "Sir, please send monks to stay at the monastery at Kīṭāgiri." The rule exists so that the next monk who walks for alms with his eyes lowered is not mocked, and so that a lay follower who wants good monks near him can have them.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — for a monk who hears of the criticism and says nothing; for the banished monk who does not stop after the private corrections, after the corrections in the Saṅgha, or after the motion; and for one who does not stop when the procedure pressing him is illegitimate.30​ 31
    • Thullaccaya — after each of the first two announcements of a legitimate procedure; with the dukkaṭa after the motion, annulled when the saṅghādisesa falls.32

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he has not been pressed; if he stops; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.33

    Related Offences

    • Sg 10 — the procedure, set out in full.
    • Sg 11, Sg 12, Pc 68 — the same pressing, for other refusals.
    • Pc 37, Pc 51 — eating at the wrong time and drinking alcohol, two of the acts that, habitual and known, ground a banishment.
    • Pc 20 — water with living beings, which the watering allowance above presupposes.

    Related Suttas

    • SN 17.8 — the old jackal with mange: "a bhikkhu here whose mind is overcome and obsessed with gain, honour, and praise does not feel at ease"; the gain the two monks' flattery won them, and its cost.34
    • MN 3 — "Bhikkhus, be my heirs in Dhamma, not my heirs in material things"; the corrupter of families seeks the second inheritance.35
