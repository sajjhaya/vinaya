    Pc 91

    Story (Vatthu)

    At one time the Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. He had allowed rainy-season robes for the monks.1

    Knowing this, the monks from the group of six wore rainy-season robes that were inappropriate in size. As they were walking about, they were dragging them along, both in front and behind. The monks of few desires complained and criticised those monks: "How can the monks from the group of six wear such rainy-season robes?"

    The Buddha asked those monks whether it was true, and they said that it was. He rebuked them: "Foolish men, how can you do this? This will affect people's confidence." After rebuking them, he laid down the rule.2

    Synopsis — the group of six wear rains cloths so long that the ends drag as they walk, and the rule that follows caps the cloth at six standard handspans by two and a half.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk is having a rainy-season robe made, it should be made the right size. This is the right size: six standard handspans long and two-and-a-half wide. If it exceeds that, it is to be cut down, and he commits an offense entailing confession."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu makes a rainy-season robe larger than six by two-and-a-half standard hand-spans, or has one made, it should be cut down to size and he incurs a pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "When a bhikkhu is having a rains-bathing cloth made, it is to be made to the standard measurement. Here the standard is this: six spans—using the sugata span—in length, two and a half spans in width. In excess of that, it is to be cut down and confessed."5

    Brahm

    "Having a rainy-season-bathing-cloth (vassikasātikam) made beyond the maximum allowable size is an offence of Pācittiya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "By a bhikkhu who is having a rains bathing-cloth made, [a bathing-cloth] which has the [proper] measure is to be made. This measure here is: six spans of the sugata-span in length, two-and-a-half across. For one who lets it exceed [the measure], [this is a case] involving expiation with cutting [off the cloth]."7

    Every rendering that gives a measure gives the same one; Ajahn Brahm's rule line, as at Pc 89 and Pc 90, leaves the measure to the paragraph beneath it. The renderings differ only in what they call the thing. Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation and his recitation call it a "rainy-season robe", which is a literal rendering of vassikasāṭikā and could suggest a robe worn through the rains in place of the ordinary ones — the reading Ñāṇatusita records Horner as having taken, and rejects.8 Thanissaro, Ajahn Brahm and Ñāṇatusita all put its purpose into the name, "rains-bathing cloth", which is what NP 24 and the Khandhakas make it. Nothing the monk must do turns on the choice, since the size is the same whatever the cloth is called; what the size is for is taken up below, under Object.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A rains cloth is for a monk's modesty, and the rule keeps it the size modesty needs, so that what he wears to stay decent in the rain does not become something he drags about in front of others. The cloth was given so that monks need not bathe naked in the rain, which Visākhā, asking to give it, called "gross, disgusting, and repulsive"; the group of six then wore theirs so long that the ends trailed in front and behind as they walked, and the Buddha rebuked them with "This will affect people's confidence" before setting a size.9​ 10

    This rule is Pc 89 again with a third cloth and a third measure, and it borrows everything else. Its machinery is Pc 86's: making the cloth or having it made is wrong conduct for the effort, the full offence falls when the monk receives it, it is cut down before he confesses, the four combinations of his own hands and another's all carry the full offence, and making one for someone else, or using one another person made, is wrong conduct.11​ 12 Two further questions are settled elsewhere: how long a standard handspan is, at Pc 87, and the cloth itself, when it may be sought, worn and determined, at NP 24, where the whole calendar is laid out. This rule adds one thing only, how big the cloth may be.

    Object

    A rains cloth longer than six standard handspans or wider than two and a half. The Vibhaṅga's own definition gives only the season, "for use during the four months of the rainy season", and the purpose comes from elsewhere.13 At NP 24 the group of six, their rains cloths worn out, bathed naked in the rain, and to do so while having one is wrong conduct; Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual and the Concise BMC both define the cloth by that use.14​ 15 At Thanissaro's reckoning of the handspan the limit comes to 1.5 metres by 62.5 centimetres, which he judges "enough to cover one from the waist to the knees".16 The group of six did not wear their cloths at the wrong time or for the wrong purpose; they wore them so long that they trailed in the dirt, and the rule answers by giving the cloth a length.

    Effort and intention

    As at Pc 86.17 Why the Vibhaṅga treats the two ways of coming by an oversize cloth so differently, the full offence for having one made and wrong conduct for using one given ready-made, Brahmāli explains from the rule's point: "it is actually the asking which is part of the problem here, because you are being luxurious", and a monk who is simply handed a cloth has asked for nothing.18

    The cloth itself has outlived the custom, as NP 24 records, and the bathing cloths in use today are a different article under a different allowance. What remains is the measure of a covering: enough to be decent, and not so much that it trails behind the man it covers.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • An offence of wrong conduct for the effort of making an oversize rains cloth or having one made, the full offence falling on receiving it, at which point it is to be cut down before the offence is confessed.19
    • An offence of wrong conduct where a monk makes one, or has one made, for the benefit of someone else.20
    • An offence of wrong conduct where a monk obtains one made by someone else and uses it without first cutting it down.21

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    As at Pc 89 and Pc 90, and in the same words: there is no offence if he makes it the right size; if he makes it smaller; if he gets one made by another that exceeds the size and cuts it down before using it; if he makes a canopy, a floor cover, a cloth screen, a mattress or a pillow; if he is insane; if he is the first offender.22

    Related Offences

    • Pc 89 — an oversize sitting mat; the anchor for this rule's measure, penalty and non-offence clause.
    • NP 24 — seeking or wearing the rains cloth at the wrong time; the home of the cloth itself, its origin, its calendar and its determination.
    • Pc 90 — an oversize itch-covering cloth; the other occasional cloth, determined while its owner is sick and assigned to another otherwise.
    • Pc 87 — where the length of a standard handspan is argued.
    • Pc 92 — an oversize robe; the last of the measured cloths.
    • Bi Pc 22 — the nuns' corresponding rule, on a bathing cloth of four standard handspans by two. Brahmāli's introduction to the nuns' rules counts it as "the same as" this one and tells it with the same complaint against a group of six; the nuns' Pātimokkha carries no rains cloth.23

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; Ajahn Brahm's retelling summarises the same account. The Manual, the recitation, the class (session 032, 30 July 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1 and its Appendix II, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were all checked, and the Khandhaka's account of Visākhā was read in Brahmāli's translation. I treat it as a lean mirror of Pc 89: the machinery belongs to Pc 86, the unit to Pc 87 and the cloth to NP 24, and each is named and declined, not retold.
