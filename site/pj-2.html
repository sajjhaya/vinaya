    Pj 2

    Story (Vatthu)

    Venerable Dhaniya had been a potter before he went forth. He kept the rains residence with a group of companions in grass huts on the slope of Mount Isigili near Rājagaha, and when the others left at the end of the rains he stayed on through the winter and the summer.

    Three times, while he was away on almsround, women gathering grass and firewood took his hut apart and carried off the grass and sticks, and three times he rebuilt it. After the third time, drawing on his old trade, he kneaded mud and built himself a hut entirely of clay, then gathered grass, sticks, and cow dung and fired it. The hut was a pretty thing — red like a scarlet rain-mite, and when struck it sounded just like a bell.

    Coming down from the Vulture Peak with a number of monks, the Buddha saw the red hut and asked what it was. When he heard, he rebuked Dhaniya: "Doesn't he have any consideration, compassion, and mercy for living beings?" He had the monks demolish it, so that later generations would not follow the example, and laid down that a monk should not make a hut entirely of clay.

    Dhaniya then thought of timber. The caretaker of the royal woodyard was a friend, but the timber there belonged to the king, set aside for repairing the town in an emergency, and could be released only if the king gave it away. Dhaniya told the caretaker, "Actually, it's been given by the king," and the caretaker, trusting the integrity of the Sakyan monastics, let him take it. Dhaniya built a wooden hut.

    Vassakāra, the chief minister of Magadha, inspecting the public works, found the timber gone, and the caretaker said the king had given it to Dhaniya. The minister had the caretaker bound and taken by force. Seeing this, Dhaniya went with him.

    Before King Bimbisāra, Dhaniya reminded the king that at his anointing he had declared grass, sticks, and water given for monastics and brahmins to enjoy. The king answered that he had spoken of conscientious monastics and of what was ownerless in the wilderness, and that Dhaniya had used his words as a pretext to take timber that had not been given. But he could not beat, imprison, or banish a monastic living in his own kingdom. "Go, you're free because of your status," he said, "but don't do such a thing again."

    People complained that the Sakyan monastics were liars who "even deceive the king, never mind other people." The monks of few desires told the Buddha, who questioned and rebuked Dhaniya. Then he turned to a former judge who had gone forth among the monks and asked him for stealing how much King Bimbisāra would beat, imprison, or banish a thief. "For stealing a pāda coin, sir, or the value of a pāda." At that time in Rājagaha a pāda was worth five māsaka. The Buddha laid down the rule on that threshold: a monk who, intending to steal, took what had not been given was expelled.

    A second incident extended it. Some of the group of six monks stole the dyers' stock of cloth and shared it out. When the other monks objected, they answered that the rule concerned inhabited areas, not the wilderness. "But that's just the same," the monks said. The Buddha extended the rule to cover an inhabited area and the wilderness alike.1

    Synopsis — Ven. Dhaniya, after his clay hut is demolished, obtains the king's reserved timber from a caretaker by claiming the king had given it; the caretaker is arrested, and the Buddha lays down the rule against theft, set at the kingdom's own legal threshold, then extends it to the wilderness after a second group of monks exploits the gap.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk, intending to steal, takes from an inhabited area or from the wilderness what has not been given to him — the sort of stealing for which kings, having caught a thief, would beat, imprison, or banish him, saying, 'You're a bandit, you're a fool, you've gone astray, you're a thief' — he too is expelled and excluded from the community."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu commits theft punishable by imprisonment, he incurs a pārājika."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu, in what is reckoned a theft, take what is not given from an inhabited area or from the wilderness — just as when, in the taking of what is not given, kings arresting the criminal would flog, imprison, or banish him, saying, 'You are a robber, you are a fool, you are benighted, you are a thief' — a bhikkhu in the same way taking what is not given also is defeated and no longer in affiliation."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1, reproducing Thanissaro's rendering in a form that differs from the current BMC1, apparently an earlier printing)

    "Should any monk, in the manner of stealing, take what is not given from an inhabited area or from the wilderness – just as when there is the taking of what is not given, kings having arrested the criminal would flog, imprison or banish him saying, 'You are a robber, you are a fool, you are benighted, you are a thief' – a monk in the same manner taking what is not given is defeated and no longer in communion."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should take what has not been given from a village or wilderness, which is reckoned as theft, [and] the taking of what has not been given is of the kind [that] on account of it kings, having caught the robber, would physically punish or imprison or banish [him, saying]: 'You are a robber! You are a fool! You are insane! You are a thief!,' a bhikkhu taking what has not been given of such a kind, is also disqualified, not in communion."6

    The four full renderings track the Pāli closely, and their differences of wording ("beat" or "flog", "bandit" or "robber") change nothing. Brahmāli's recitation line is another matter. It drops the distinction between inhabited area and wilderness, since "theft" covers both; it reduces the three punishments to imprisonment, the only one, he notes, still used in Australia, with the caveat that where physical punishment persists "this rule may have to be phrased differently"; and it drops the fourfold rebuke as redundant.7 It drops the kings as well, and the Vibhaṅga allows for that, since it defines "kings" as anyone with the power to punish — "judges, government officials, or whoever metes out physical punishment" — though Ñāṇatusita, noting that the plural means any of those who have the authority to punish and that the commentaries gloss it "king's men", keeps "kings" because the origin story names one, allowing that both senses may be meant.8 The compression also builds Brahmāli's own side of the rule's main dispute into the rule line: "punishable by imprisonment" is his standard for the threshold, a question discussed below, under Value.

    The full renderings part over a single word, theyyasaṅkhātaṃ. Brahmāli's "intending to steal" follows the Vibhaṅga's own gloss, "the thought of theft"; Thanissaro's "in what is reckoned a theft" and Ñāṇatusita's "which is reckoned as theft" keep the compound's literal sense. Ñāṇatusita grants that it can be read either as the manner of taking or as the value of what is taken, and on the strength of the origin story and the word-analysis inclines to value.9 Since the full offence needs both an intention to steal and an object of sufficient value, nothing a monk must do depends on which of them this word names.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule keeps a monk's hands off what is not his, and the Story shows both what a monastic's theft costs the Saṅgha and where the rule draws its line. People complained that the Sakyan monastics were liars who "even deceive the king, never mind other people", and the caretaker who had trusted a monk's word was bound and taken away.10 Dhaniya, strictly speaking, did not steal the timber: the caretaker handed it over, deceived by a lie, and Brahmāli observes in his introduction to the Vibhaṅga that the origin story therefore does not quite fit the rule it produced, though Dhaniya's actions "were tantamount to stealing".11 What the story does supply is the rule's measure. The Buddha did not fix a value of his own: he asked a former judge what the king's courts punished, and set the threshold there. At its origin, the theft that expels a monk was the theft the king's courts punished.

    The rule is, in the working out of its details, the most complex in the Pātimokkha, in Thanissaro's judgement, "not because stealing is a concept especially hard to understand, but because it can take so many forms", which the Vibhaṅga meets case by case.12 The sources also divide the ground differently. Ajahn Brahm uses three factors: the intention to steal, an object of value, and the act of taking possession.13 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual arranges its factors under three heads, "steals", "something" and "punishable by imprisonment".14 Thanissaro gives four — object, perception, intention and effort — with the value of the object as a fifth that decides not whether there is an offence but how heavy it is.15 Thanissaro's is followed here, since it is the division the Vibhaṅga's own closing summaries follow: the possession of another, perceived as such, worth enough, and taken with the intention to steal.16

    Object

    What can be stolen is "what has not been given", which the word-analysis spells out as "what has not been let go of, what has not been relinquished; what is guarded, what is protected, what is regarded as 'mine', what belongs to someone else."17 In every case the Vibhaṅga treats, that someone else is a human being or a group of human beings. What belongs to an animal or a ghost falls outside the rule: the monks who came down from the Vulture Peak and cooked the remains of a lion's kill committed no offence.18 Thanissaro adds that peta, as the Canon uses it, covers human corpses as well as hungry ghosts.19

    What has been discarded is not "not given", so rags from a refuse heap and unclaimed things in the wilderness may be taken freely.20 The qualification that matters is how completely the owner has let go. An owner who has given a thing up for lost but still regards it as his has not abandoned it; only a complete relinquishment counts.21 Ajahn Brahm gives the Commentary's leading case. A visiting monk lost a costly roll of cloth in a festival crowd and gave it up; a resident monk found it and hid it, meaning to steal it, and soon confessed a pārājika to his teacher. The teacher would not let him disrobe until the owner had been traced to a monastery in the south, and it was established that he had given the cloth up before it was taken. There had been no owner, and so no pārājika — only a dukkaṭa for the attempt.22

    The Saṅgha is an owner, and the case studies leave no doubt that its property can be stolen: monks who took the Saṅgha's water, clay or grass, a bed, a door, a window or a rafter, intending to steal it, were expelled, and so was a monk who disregarded the draw when the Saṅgha's robe-cloth was shared out and took a portion meaning to steal it.23 A monk who gives the Saṅgha's heavy property away, believing it his to give, incurs a thullaccaya whatever its value.24 The Commentary adds, and Thanissaro accepts, that offerings to a shrine or a Buddha image which people guard on its behalf are owned for the purposes of this rule.25

    Perception

    The monk must also perceive the thing as belonging to someone else. A monk who takes something believing it his own, or discarded, or his to take on a friend's trust or from common stores, commits no offence even when the belief proves wrong.26 When Venerable Ānanda put on another monk's sarong in the sauna, thinking it was his own, the Buddha said: "There's no offense for one who perceives it as his own."27 The reverse case carries a lesser offence. A monk who takes an ownerless thing that he perceives as owned, intending to steal it, incurs a dukkaṭa at each stage, as did the monk who came back at night for goods he had seen by day and took his own instead.28

    The perception the rule asks about is ownership, not price. The Vibhaṅga's summaries list the object's worth among the facts of the case and nowhere among what the monk perceives, so a monk who steals a thing he takes to be cheap is, on my reading, judged by what it is actually worth.29 The texts do not discuss a monk who is unsure whether a thing has an owner. Thanissaro suggests he would lack the intention the rule requires, but is clear about the wise course: in doubt, do not take the thing for one's own, or at most take it on loan.30

    Intention

    The intention is "the thought of theft, the thought of stealing".31 A thought alone is no offence: a monk who saw a valuable cloth at the dyers' and wanted to steal it was told, "There's no offense for the arising of a thought."32 Nor is taking without that intention — borrowing, taking on trust, putting a thing somewhere safe for its owner. The Vibhaṅga pairs its cases to show the same act made innocent or a pārājika by intention alone. A monk who caught a wrap garment blown up by a whirlwind, meaning to return it, committed no offence; one who caught a turban to keep it before the owners found out was expelled. So with the pig, the deer and the fish released from a snare: freed out of compassion, no offence; released to be taken, expulsion.33 Taking on trust has strict conditions. Thanissaro gives the Mahāvagga's five — the owner is an acquaintance, is an intimate, has spoken of the matter, is still alive, and one knows he will be pleased — of which the Commentary requires the last two and any one of the first three.34

    The Commentary works through one case of conditional intent that is worth having. A monk in a dark storeroom picks up a sack thinking, "If the sack contains cloth, I'll steal it; if it contains just thread, I won't." If it does contain cloth, the theft was complete when he moved the sack. Where he picks it up meaning to steal whatever is inside, the Commentary would postpone the theft until he finds out and lifts it again; Thanissaro disagrees, since that monk set no condition at all and stole the sack when he first lifted it.35

    Effort

    For most things, taking means moving the object from its base. The Vibhaṅga goes through goods underground, on the ground, in the air, in water, in a boat or a vehicle, carried as a load, in a park, a dwelling, a field or the wilderness, and the pattern is the same in each: setting out or looking for a companion is a dukkaṭa, touching the object a dukkaṭa, making it stir a thullaccaya, and moving it from its base the full offence.36 What counts as moving it depends on the thing: a load shifted from the head to the shoulder, a boat moved a hair's breadth across the water, a four-footed animal made to move its fourth foot, a tree at the last blow of the axe, ghee worth five māsaka drunk in a single action.37 The case studies show loopholes failing. A monk who moved a bench rather than lift a bag from it, and one who stayed inside his dwelling with a stolen robe, thinking he would be expelled only when he came out, were both expelled.38

    Land, buildings and goods left in a monk's keeping are taken differently. Claiming another's park, field or dwelling is a dukkaṭa, and raising doubt in the owner's mind a thullaccaya; the theft is complete when the owner gives up the effort to recover it, or when the monk defeats him in a lawsuit — by bribery and false witness, the Commentary specifies. Goods deposited with a monk follow the same course: refusing to return them is a dukkaṭa, and the pārājika falls when the owner gives up.39 Ajahn Brahm adds a second point at which a custodian's theft is complete: when he takes the thing beyond the limits of his custody. A storeman who hides the Saṅgha's bowl inside the storeroom incurs a dukkaṭa; once he carries it out of the storeroom to keep, it is the pārājika. An attendant who hides his teacher's robe among his own things on a journey incurs a dukkaṭa; hiding it under a rock by the road, running off with it, or carrying it on past the agreed destination is the pārājika.40

    Destroying another's property is not taking it. The Vibhaṅga makes breaking, burning or ruining it a dukkaṭa, and the monk who set fire to the Saṅgha's grass meaning to steal it incurred only that.41 The Commentary would go further, with its concept of bhaṇḍadeyya, "compensation owed": a monk who destroys another's property, or loses through negligence something he borrowed or agreed to look after, owes its price, and incurs a pārājika if he fails to pay. Thanissaro rejects it. The Canon never mentions the concept, assigns a dukkaṭa or no offence to the very cases the Commentary would make pārājika, and gives a monk no lawful means of raising money to pay a layperson; the one responsibility it places on a monk who causes a layperson loss is that the Community may require him to apologise.42 Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes, which set the concept out at length, now carry an editor's note that he too has rejected it.43

    Nor is it theft to obtain something by a lie from someone who then hands it over, for the thing is given. The monk who asked at a distribution for a portion "for one more", and the pupil of the nun Thullanandā who asked her teacher's supporters for food in her teacher's name and ate it herself, each incurred a pācittiya for lying, not a pārājika.44 Dhaniya's lie to the caretaker is of this kind. At the edges the sources part. Disputed — Ajahn Brahm holds that a pupil handed medicine with the words "This is for your teacher" steals it the moment he takes it for himself, and, citing the Samantapāsādikā, that a monk who passes off an old thing as new in a barter and clears a pāda on the deal commits the pārājika;45 Thanissaro treats such cases as deceit — counterfeit, in his words, is "a form of a lie" — and gives a monk carrying an item who decides to keep it only the dukkaṭa for a broken promise.46 Neither reasoning is weak, and to choose between them here would be to rule where the Vibhaṅga has not.

    A monk can also steal through someone else, and the Manual lists inciting another to steal among the ways of stealing.47 Telling another to steal is a dukkaṭa. If the other steals as instructed, both are expelled; if he steals something else, or at another time than the one arranged, the instigator is free of the theft and the thief alone is expelled. An instigator who regrets his order but does not withdraw it is expelled with the thief; one who says, "Don't steal them," is free even if the thief goes ahead. Where several agree on a theft and only one carries it out, all are expelled, and so are monks who stole goods together and divided them into shares worth less than five māsaka each.48 Thanissaro adds that the instigator's offence becomes a thullaccaya when the accomplice agrees, and, from the Commentary, that only a clear command counts, to which he adds a sign meant as one: telling another where something lies and how easy it would be to take is no offence even if he takes it.49 The same reasoning reaches a stolen credit card, since handing it to a clerk is a gesture telling the clerk to transfer the funds.50 Copyright, by contrast, is outside the rule on his analysis: making a copy takes nothing from the owner's possession, so infringement is at most a dukkaṭa under the general rule against misbehaviour.51

    The Vibhaṅga's case of tax is the customs station, a place where a king's duty is owed. A monk who carries dutiable goods through it intending not to pay is expelled when his second foot crosses the boundary, the value counted as the duty owed, while a monk who avoids the customs station altogether incurs only a dukkaṭa.52 A monk who did not know a dutiable gem was in his bag committed no offence; one who carried a gem through for a man who had bribed him with food was expelled.53

    Disputed — whether evading any other tax is theft under this rule.

    Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes hold that it is, though their editor flags the principle as disputed: wherever a monk knows a tax is owed and deliberately does not pay it, whether on earnings, on land or as a sales-tax exemption he is not entitled to, a tax worth a pāda or more makes a pārājika;54 and the Manual names evading "tax or custom duty" among the ways of stealing.55 Thanissaro reads the customs passage the other way. He takes goods that have entered the customs area as impounded, so that paying the duty is recovering them, and the Vibhaṅga's dukkaṭa for going around the customs station as showing that evading a tax on goods never impounded is not theft; other tax evasion is a dukkaṭa, unless an item impounded on government property is taken out without paying the fee.56 Thanissaro's reading has the Vibhaṅga's own dukkaṭa behind it, which the wider reading must explain; but "impounded" is his extrapolation from an ancient customs post to a modern tax system, and it is fair to say that the texts themselves do not settle how far the customs case reaches. A monk who pays what he owes has nothing to decide.

    Value

    Every theft is an offence; value decides only how heavy. Goods worth five māsaka or more make the pārājika, goods worth more than one māsaka and less than five a thullaccaya, and goods worth one māsaka or less a dukkaṭa.57 This is the former judge's answer turned into the rule. Stolen goods are valued, in Thanissaro's words, at "the price they would have fetched at the time and place of the theft".58 Separate thefts are added together only when they come from one plan. The monk who ate a pot of ghee little by little incurred a dukkaṭa, and Thanissaro gives the Commentary's reading: each spoonful was a fresh decision, whereas a monk who resolves to gather enough lumber for a hut, and steals a plank here and a rafter there over many days, commits one offence on the value of the whole.59

    What five māsaka are worth now is the hard question, and it matters, because it decides what a community treats as the line of expulsion.

    Disputed — what modern value marks the pārājika threshold.

    Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm take the figure of a sub-commentary which both attribute to the Vimativinodanī: one māsaka is four rice grains' weight of gold, so the threshold is twenty grains, about 1/24 troy ounce.60​ 61 Thanissaro grants that some things the case studies treat as pārājika, a pillow or a handful of rice in a famine, look cheaper than that, and answers that they may have been dear at the time. He defends the figure because it sets the bar high, so that a theft above it leaves no doubt and a doubtful case gets the benefit of the doubt, and because it is precise, where the sentencing of a modern court would bring in the judge's mood and much else the Buddha never let into the reckoning;62 Ajahn Brahm adds that it is the traditional reckoning of most Theravāda countries.63 The Manual rejects the method. The figure comes, he shows, from the Uttaravinicchaya-ṭīkā, quoting an obscure old Sinhala commentary on the training of novices, and the Mūlasikkhā-ṭīkā says of it that it "is not in the canon, nor in the commentary; it is to be investigated". The benefit of the doubt runs the other way where the law is more lenient: in Western Australia thefts below $1,000 are punished by a fine, against about $70 for the gold figure in November 2011. A clear figure is not thereby a reasonable one; no one can know that a rice grain of the ṭīkā's day matches a modern grain of weight; and the ṭīkā was most likely reckoning from what its own society punished. A sutta also shows an ordinary person imprisoned in the Buddha's day for stealing half a kahāpaṇa, ten māsaka (AN I 251), close enough to the Vibhaṅga's five to suggest that an imprisonable theft is the standard the rule had in view.64 On his reading the measure is the law of the place where the theft happens, and in class he drew the consequence for a monk abroad: in a country where far smaller thefts are imprisonable, a monk who slips a hundred dollars' duty on medicine past its customs has probably committed the pārājika there.65 My own judgment leans to Brahmāli's case, since his correction of the source can be checked and his point that the gold figure is one era's local answer seems to me a real flaw; but it is a judgment on a question the texts leave open, and a community's settled convention deserves weight.

    Neither standard makes a smaller theft acceptable. Every monk is told at his ordination never to take what is not given, "even if it is only a blade of grass", and the lesser offences exist because any theft is wrong.66 What the threshold settles is only when a monk is lost to the Saṅgha, and the Vibhaṅga's closing cases show how much care that judgment deserves. Of two friends, Paṇḍaka and Kapila, one was crossing a river when a butcher's lump of fat stuck to his foot; he grabbed it meaning to return it, was told by its owners that he was no longer a monastic, believed it, and accepted a cowherd woman's invitation to intercourse: "There's no offense entailing expulsion for stealing, but there's an offense entailing expulsion for having sexual intercourse."67 The last case runs the other way. A student of Venerable Daḷhika, plagued by lust, stole a turban from a shopkeeper and told his teacher he was no longer a monk and would disrobe. Daḷhika had the turban brought and valued. It was worth less than five māsaka; he gave a teaching, "And that monk was delighted."68 Brahmāli draws the lesson: it is the duty of a teacher, "and presumably any fellow monastic, to go to some length to help a co-monastic get out of trouble, if at all possible."69

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — each step toward a theft and touching the object; every stage of a theft of goods worth one māsaka or less; taking an ownerless thing perceived as owned; destroying another's property; going around a customs station; claiming another's land or refusing to return a deposit; telling another to steal.70
    • Dukkaṭa — offering or asking to carry a message to a supporting family and keeping what is sent back; taking rags from a fresh corpse; using a lay follower's equipment where it does not belong.71
    • Thullaccaya — making the object stir; taking goods worth more than one māsaka and less than five; each foot but the last of an animal led away, and the last blow but one on a tree; raising doubt in the owner of land or a deposit, or losing a fraudulent lawsuit; an accomplice's agreement to a theft worth five māsaka.72
    • Thullaccaya — giving away the Saṅgha's heavy property as though it were one's own, whatever its value.73
    • Pācittiya — a lie that obtains a thing which is then handed over (Pc 1).74

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if the monk perceives the thing as his own, takes it on trust or borrows it; if it belongs to a ghost or an animal; if he perceives it as discarded; or if he is insane, deranged, overwhelmed by pain, or the first offender.75 The last four are the standing exemptions, discussed under Pj 1.

    The case studies add further cases of no offence: words that were "just a way of speaking", as when a monk who had put away another's robe for safekeeping answered "I've taken it"; visiting monks who ate the Saṅgha's fruit, thinking it was meant for eating; a gift from the keeper of an orchard, who had authority to guard it but not to give it away; carrying a dutiable item without knowing; Venerable Ajjuka, who assigned a supporter's property to the heir the supporter had asked for; and Venerable Pilindavaccha, who brought back two kidnapped children by his supernormal powers.76 When monks, afraid of wrongdoing, would not bring anything to the observance-day hall and sat on the bare ground, the Buddha said, "I allow you to borrow."77 From the orchard keeper's case Thanissaro infers that receiving stolen goods, even knowingly, is no offence under this rule, though it leaves a monk exposed to the civil law.78

    Related Offences

    • Pj 1 — the monk who wrongly believed he had fallen by a theft, and then fell in fact by intercourse.
    • Pc 1 — the lie that obtains something which is then handed over: deceit, not theft.
    • Pc 84 — picking up a valuable left in a monastery or a house, to keep it for its owner.
    • NP 30, Pc 82 — diverting to oneself or to another individual what was intended for the Saṅgha, where the Vibhaṅga also treats offerings dedicated to a shrine.
    • Sg 10–13 — the passage from which the commentaries derive the principle that a completed offence absorbs the lesser offences leading to it; Thanissaro reads it as meant only for those four rules. Under this rule the question does not arise, since a monk who completes the pārājika is no longer a monk.79

    Related Suttas

    • Dhp 246–247 — the one who "steals" is among those who "right here in this world / destroys their own foundation"; for a monk the rule makes that loss literal, since the theft ends his life in the Saṅgha.80
