    Pc 78

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. The monks from the group of six were arguing with the good monks. The good monks said, "These monks from the group of six are shameless; it is not possible to argue with them."1

    And the monks from the group of six said, "Why are you slandering us by calling us shameless?"2

    "How did you know?"3

    "We were eavesdropping on you."4

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised them, "How could the monks from the group of six eavesdrop on monks they are arguing and disputing with?" The Buddha questioned them, established that it was true, rebuked them, and stated the rule.5

    Synopsis — The group of six give themselves away by complaining. The rule that follows looks not at what they heard but at why they went to hear it.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk eavesdrops on monks who are arguing and disputing, thinking, 'I'll hear what they say,' and he does so only for this reason and no other, he commits an offense entailing confession."6

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu eavesdrops on bhikkhus who are disputing about Dhamma or Vinaya, intending to use what he hears against them, he incurs a pācittiya."7

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu stand eavesdropping on bhikkhus when they are arguing, quarreling, and disputing, thinking, 'I will overhear what they say' — doing it for just that reason and no other — it is to be confessed."8

    Brahm

    "Eavesdropping on bhikkhus during a dispute, intending to use any knowledge gained against those bhikkhus, is an offence of Pācittiya."9

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should stand overhearing bhikkhus who are arguing, who are quarrelling, who are engaged in dispute [thinking], 'I shall hear what these ones will say,' having made just this the reason, [and] not another, [this is a case] involving expiation."10

    The renderings agree on the act, and the one that differs does so openly. The rule names three states of a quarrel, bhaṇḍanajātānaṁ kalahajātānaṁ vivādāpannānaṁ; Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita keep all three, Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga has two, and the recitation and Ajahn Brahm reduce them to one, which the Vibhaṅga licenses, since its word-analysis glosses all three with the single phrase "engaged in a legal issue".11​ 12 The recitation adds two clauses, and neither — "about Dhamma or Vinaya" and "intending to use what he hears against them" — is in the rule itself. The second comes from the word-analysis and is taken up under Intention; the first narrows the object, and whether it should is the question disputed under Object.13

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    Fairness is hardest for a monk to keep when he is a party to a quarrel, and this rule holds him to it there, and with it the Saṅgha's means of settling one. A dispute among monks is to be settled in the open, by procedures the whole community can see; a monk who creeps within earshot of the other side to collect words he can use against them has stepped outside that, whatever the merits of his case. The Story is a small comedy of self-betrayal: the group of six, quarrelling with the good monks, overheard themselves called shameless and gave themselves away by complaining of it. Nor would a Saṅgha spying on itself keep the people's confidence, which is the rebuke's concern.

    Thanissaro analyses the offence into three factors: object, effort and intention.14

    Object

    The object is monks engaged in a legal issue, and not monks in conversation. The word-analysis defines the three words of the quarrel as "engaged in a legal issue", and Ajahn Brahm, reporting the Commentary and citing the Khandhaka, describes the issue as monks disagreeing over what is and is not the Dhamma and Vinaya.15​ 16 Where there is no legal issue, there is no offence. Ajahn Brahm's example is overhearing two learned monks discussing a point of Dhamma and staying quiet so as not to interrupt; Thanissaro's is sharper: there is no penalty for listening in on a monk sitting in private with a woman, to hear what they say to each other.17​ 18 Within a legal issue the object is wide. Thanissaro notes that it makes no difference whether the two sides are confronting each other or, as in the origin story, one side is talking in private; nor does it matter whether one is already a party to the dispute oneself, as the group of six were.19 And because the Vibhaṅga moves between singular and plural, even a single monk in an argument with someone unordained is grounds for the full offence.20 Perception of the monks' ordination works as under Pc 42; the unordained are grounds for an offence of wrong conduct.21​ 22

    Disputed — whether an accusation, and not only a dispute, is grounds for the offence.

    Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm give the Commentary as confining the object to a dispute, and Ajahn Brahm follows it without remark; Brahmāli's recitation ("about Dhamma or Vinaya") and his Pātimokkha Manual ("in relation to Vinaya issues") both leave the question open.23​ 24​ 25​ 26 Thanissaro departs from it tentatively, on the word-analysis's unqualified "legal issue": "accusations would appear to fit here as well".27 The case where the readings part is a monk listening in on the two parties to a charge. There is a second argument to be had from the rule itself. The hostile purposes of the word-analysis — to accuse, to remind, to counter-accuse, to counter-remind, to humiliate — are four terms of the formal accusation procedure and one of plain malice, as Thanissaro's own intention factor recognises, and an intention framed in the vocabulary of accusation sits oddly with an object that excludes it.28​ 29 That argument is mine and not his, and it rests on vocabulary; the texts do not settle the question. I would have a monk treat a charge as covered, since nothing is lost by keeping out of earshot.

    Effort

    The effort is to put oneself where the words reach. Brahmāli observed in class that the word need not mean listening in secret; it can mean "just listening in to a conversation which really is none of your business".30 Ajahn Brahm defines the word the other way, as "secretly listening".31 On Brahmāli's reading a monk need not hide to be inside this rule; he need only place himself within earshot on purpose.

    The word-analysis allots the offences step by step.32 Going in order to listen is an offence of wrong conduct, and so is speeding up behind the other party or slowing down in front of them; wherever he then stands to listen, the pācittiya. Ajahn Brahm puts the pācittiya at the hearing: the effort is the dukkaṭa, and "when one succeeds in overhearing, it is an offence of Pācittiya".33 The efforts the Vibhaṅga names are going, hurrying, lagging and standing within earshot, with the silence of the fourth case beside them, and the Parivāra classes the offence as arising from body and mind, or from body, speech and mind, never from mind alone.34 And so a monk who stays in his seat and merely turns his attention to a quarrel across the hall has made none of the efforts the Vibhaṅga names (Ajahn Brahm's stricter view, below, would reach him if he stayed unnoticed to gather evidence), though Thanissaro suggests that the effort would extend, at present, to "surreptitiously reading another person's mail".35

    There is a fourth case, and it is broken by saying nothing. A monk who comes to where another is speaking privately must clear his throat or otherwise make his presence known; if he does not, the pācittiya falls with no lesser offence beneath it.36 The Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī, Thanissaro reports, suggests saying "I'm here".37 Presumably this is where speech enters the Parivāra's account: the silence is part of the act.

    Intention

    The monk must go in order to hear, and for that reason alone.38 The word-analysis fills the reason in with its five hostile purposes, and the recitation writes them into the rule as the intention to use what he hears against them; Thanissaro's factor is the same, "with the purpose of using what they say against them".39​ 40​ 41 The Vibhaṅga ties the purpose to the going: he goes "thinking, 'After hearing what they say, I'll accuse them'".42 A monk who happens to overhear, does not stay on to listen, and only afterwards wishes he could use what he heard, has not gone for that reason, and the steps the rule counts were never taken with it. Ajahn Brahm holds the chance listener to more: he must make his presence known at once, and if he deliberately stays unnoticed to overhear something to his advantage in the dispute, that too is a pācittiya.43

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    The exemption turns the hostile purposes round: the same steps, taken to hold back, abstain, resolve the quarrel or free himself, are no offence at all, which is the rule's clearest statement that it reads the purpose and not the feet. Outside a legal issue the cough is courtesy, not a rule, and Ajahn Brahm commends it anyway. A monk approaching people in private conversation does well to make himself known, for a cough "could prevent a layman being embarrassed when candidly confiding in his teacher".44

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — going in order to overhear; the pācittiya where he then stands to listen.45
    • Dukkaṭa — speeding up behind the other party, or slowing down in front of them, in order to overhear.46
    • Dukkaṭa — overhearing those not fully ordained who are engaged in a legal issue, however they are perceived.47

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he goes thinking, "After hearing what they say, I'll hold back," "… I'll abstain," "… I'll resolve it," or "… I'll free myself"; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.48 Nor is there an offence where no legal issue is in progress, since the object is missing.49​ 50

    Related Offences

    • Pc 63 — reopening a settled legal issue: where Thanissaro points for what a legal issue is.51
    • Pc 3 — malicious tale-bearing: Ajahn Brahm points to its origin story as the pattern of a monk carrying what he has heard to the party it will damage.52
    • Pc 76, 77 — the two rules before, each laid down at Sāvatthī on account of the group of six's dealings with a fellow monk.53
    • Pc 16, 42, 77 — with this rule, the four pācittiyas whose rule carries "just this the reason and no other", as Brahmāli's endnote to the Parivāra groups them.54
    • Pc 66 — travelling with a caravan of thieves: the rule whose name the Parivāra gives to the class of offences, this one among them, that arise from body and mind or from body, speech and mind.55

    Related Suttas

    • MN 103 — monks who differ are approached openly, one by one, so that they do not "fall into a dispute": the open way of settling a quarrel, which the monk who creeps within earshot of the other side abandons.56

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register, with the exchange quoted in the monks' own words and the complaint, interrogation and rebuke in the standing summary form. Brahmāli's endnote records that he renders the complaint "monks they are arguing and disputing with" rather than the literal "monks who are arguing and disputing", since the group of six were parties to the quarrel; the Story follows him. The Manual, the recitation, the class (session 031, 23 July 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were all checked; the Concise BMC condenses BMC1 here. Ajahn Brahm's example of the learned monks, which he cites to the Vinaya-mukha beside the Vibhaṅga, is left out. The Commentary and the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī are given as Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm report them. MN 103 is quoted from Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation.
