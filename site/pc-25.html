    Pc 25

    Story (Vatthu)

    First sub-story. The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. A certain monk was walking for alms along a street in Sāvatthī, and so was a certain nun. The monk said to her, "Go to such-and-such a place, sister, and you'll get alms," and she said the same to him. Because they met frequently, they became friends.

    Just then robe-cloth belonging to the Saṅgha was being distributed. After going to the instruction, that nun went to the monk and bowed, and he said to her, "Sister, will you accept my share of the robe-cloth?" — "Yes, venerable, my robes are worn." He gave her his robe-cloth, and as a consequence his own robes became worn too. Other monks asked him why he did not make himself a robe, and he told them what had happened.

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised him: "How could a monk give robe-cloth to a nun?" They told the Buddha, who questioned him, established that the nun was not a relative of his, and rebuked him — "Foolish man, a man and a woman who are unrelated don't know what's appropriate and inappropriate, what's good and bad, in dealing with each other. And still you did this. This will affect people's confidence" — and laid down the rule: a monk who gives robe-cloth to an unrelated nun commits an offence entailing confession.1

    Second sub-story. After that, being afraid of wrongdoing, the monks did not give robe-cloth to the nuns even in exchange. The nuns complained: "How can they not give us robe-cloth in exchange?" The monks told the Buddha, who gave a teaching and then declared, "Monks, I allow you to give things in exchange to five kinds of people: monks, nuns, trainee nuns, novice monks, and novice nuns." And he amended the rule to its final form, adding the words "except in exchange."2

    Synopsis — A monk gives a nun he has befriended on almsround his own share of a Saṅgha distribution of cloth, and goes ragged himself; the Buddha forbids giving robe-cloth to an unrelated nun, then amends the rule to permit giving it in exchange.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk gives robe-cloth to an unrelated nun, except in exchange, he commits an offense entailing confession."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu gives robe-cloth to an unrelated bhikkhunī, except if she gives something in return, he incurs a pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu give robe-cloth to a bhikkhunī unrelated to him, except in exchange, it is to be confessed."5

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Giving cloth to a bhikkhunī who is not a relation, except in exchange for something else, is an offence of Pācittiya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should give a robe [-cloth] to an unrelated bhikkhunī, except in an exchange, [this is a case] involving expiation."7

    The renderings agree, and nothing that divides them changes the rule. The recitation spells out "except in exchange" as "except if she gives something in return", naming the nun as the one who gives back, which the Pāli leaves to the context.8 The exception is NP 5's word for word, and Ñāṇatusita refers it there.9 Cīvara alone has work to do. Brahmāli's "robe-cloth", Ñāṇatusita's bracketed "[-cloth]" and Ajahn Brahm's plain "cloth" all follow the Vibhaṅga, which defines the article here as any cloth large enough to be assigned to another, not only a finished robe. That is taken up under Object.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule protects the reserve a monk keeps with the nuns, for his own sake and so that lay people have no cause to doubt the two Saṅghas. The Buddha's rebuke states it: "a man and a woman who are unrelated don't know what's appropriate and inappropriate, what's good and bad, in dealing with each other … This will affect people's confidence."10 The Story shows how such a bond forms. A monk and a nun met often on almsround and became friends, and when a Saṅgha distribution of cloth came round, he gave her his own share, and went ragged himself.

    Here the chapter changes subject. Pc 21 to Pc 24 concern the formal instruction of the nuns, and this rule concerns cloth. What it extends is NP 5, from outside the chapter, which forbids a monk to receive robe-cloth from an unrelated nun except in exchange; this rule forbids him to give it, on the same object and with the same exception. Thanissaro calls it NP 5's counterpart, and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual refers its object and its exception there in a single line.11​ 12 "Unrelated" and "nun" carry the definitions worked out at NP 4. The offence has two factors, object and effort.13

    Object

    The object is robe-cloth of any of the six allowable kinds, linen, cotton, silk, wool, sunn hemp and hemp, no smaller than a piece that may be assigned to another, which the Khandhaka sets at eight standard fingerbreadths by four.14​ 15 Any other requisite falls outside the rule altogether, so a monk may give an unrelated nun a bowl or a medicine without touching it.16

    Effort

    The effort is giving the cloth to an unrelated nun and receiving nothing in return. The nun is one fully ordained by both Saṅghas; one ordained on the nuns' side only is wrong conduct, and a trainee nun or a novice nun is no offence at all.17​ 18​ 19 The Vinaya's "unrelated" reaches back eight generations, and Brahmāli's counsel was practical: "if you have no sense of relation to somebody, that probably is enough to count as unrelated normally."20 Perception does not mitigate. If she is unrelated, the full offence falls whether he takes her as unrelated, is unsure, or takes her to be related; this is Pc 4's six-fold series, to which Thanissaro refers the reader.21​ 22

    The giving need not be hand to hand. By Thanissaro's report of the Commentary, placing the cloth near her counts as giving it, if she accepts it as given.23 It does not include her borrowing the cloth, or taking it on trust, the arrangement between close friends by which one may take the other's belongings.24​ 25

    The exception is read generously. The exchange need not be equal: the Vibhaṅga allows much for little or little for much, and Thanissaro reports Buddhaghosa quoting the ancient Mahā Paccarī, for which even a piece of yellow myrobalan, one of the cheapest things in India, is enough in return for a robe.26​ 27 Behind the clause stands the permission the second sub-story records, that the five kinds of ordained may exchange things among themselves.28 It does not reach laypeople, with whom trade falls under NP 20.29

    The cloth in view is the monk's own. Cloth handed to the nuns from a monastery store is another matter: in Brahmāli's view that is the Saṅgha giving, and it is usually acceptable because it "doesn't create the same intimacy".30 The origin story fits his line. What the monk there gave away was his own share of a Saṅgha distribution, and his own robes went ragged for it.31

    What the rule leaves open is as telling as what it closes. A monk may still support the nuns, since every requisite but robe-cloth falls outside it and robe-cloth itself passes on the smallest token in return. What it stops is the unreciprocated private gift of cloth from a monk to a nun. The monk of the story was generous, giving away his own share and wearing rags for it, and the rule was laid down all the same. Generosity, it seems, is not the test.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • A dukkaṭa where the cloth is given, otherwise than in exchange, to a nun ordained on one side only, in the nuns' Saṅgha but not the monks'.32​ 33
    • A dukkaṭa where the nun is in fact related but the monk perceives her as unrelated, or is unsure whether she is related.34

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if the nun is related; if the exchange is unequal, much for little or little for much; if she takes the cloth on trust; if she borrows it; if he gives her any requisite other than robe-cloth; if she is a trainee nun; if she is a novice nun; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.35 There is likewise no offence where the nun is in fact related and the monk perceives her as related.36

    Related Offences

    • NP 5 — receiving robe-cloth from an unrelated nun, except in exchange: this rule's converse and the source of its object and its exception, word for word. The generosity runs one way here and the other way there, and the Buddha forbade both.
    • NP 4 — having an unrelated nun wash, dye or beat a used robe; the anchor of the whole family, carrying the definitions of "unrelated" and "nun" that this rule uses without restating.
    • Pc 26 — sewing a robe, or having one sewn, for an unrelated nun; this rule's pair. Cloth here, labour there, and the object shifts with it, from robe-cloth to a robe.
    • NP 20 — trading, which bounds the permission of the second sub-story: requisites may be exchanged among the five kinds of ordained, not traded with laypeople.
    • Pc 4 — where the perception series this rule follows is set out: a monk who believes the nun to be a relative is no better off for believing it.

    Related Suttas

    • AN 5.55 — a monk and a nun, son and mother, who "often saw one another": "a bond was formed; because a bond formed, intimacy arose", the course the origin story's friendship had begun.37

Ajahn Brahm's retelling has the monk given new cloth to replace his own robe, where the Vibhaṅga has a Saṅgha distribution of which he gave away his share, and it is not carried. If a bhikkhu simply places the cloth near a bhikkhunī as his way of giving it to her, and she accepts it as given, this factor is fulfilled… Buddhaghosa quotes the Mahā Paccarī, one of the ancient commentaries, as saying that even if, in return for the cloth, the bhikkhunī gives the bhikkhu a piece of yellow myrobalan—a medicinal fruit, one of the cheapest things imaginable in India—he escapes the penalty under this rule."↩︎
