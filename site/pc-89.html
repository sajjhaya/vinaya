    Pc 89

    Story (Vatthu)

    At one time the Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. He had allowed sitting mats for the monks.1

    Knowing this, the monks from the group of six used sitting mats that were inappropriate in size. The mats hung down from beds and benches, both in front and behind. The monks of few desires complained and criticised those monks: "How can the monks from the group of six use such sitting mats?"

    The Buddha asked those monks whether it was true, and they said that it was. He rebuked them: "Foolish men, how can you do this? This will affect people's confidence." After rebuking them, he laid down a rule setting the size at two standard handspans long and one and a half wide.

    At that time there was a big monk called Venerable Udāyī. Putting out his sitting mat in front of the Buddha, he stretched it on all sides before sitting down. The Buddha asked him, "Udāyī, why are you stretching the sitting mat on all sides, as if an old hide?"

    "Because, sir, the sitting mat you have allowed for the monks is too small."

    Soon afterwards the Buddha gave a teaching and addressed the monks: "Monks, I allow a border of one handspan for the sitting mat." He then laid down the rule in its final form.2

    Synopsis — the group of six use sitting mats that hang off the furniture and are given a size; Udāyī, who is a big man, has to stretch his on all sides, "as if an old hide", and the Buddha adds a border.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk is having a sitting mat made, it should be made the right size. This is the right size: two standard handspans long and one-and-a-half wide, and a border of one handspan. If it exceeds that, it is to be cut down, and he commits an offense entailing confession."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu makes a sitting-cloth larger than three by three-and-a-half standard hand-spans, or has one made, it should be cut down to size and he incurs a pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "When a bhikkhu is having a sitting cloth made, it is to be made to the standard measurement. Here the standard is this: two spans—using the sugata span—in length, one and a half spans in width, the border a span. In excess of that, it is to be cut down and confessed."5

    Brahm

    "Having a sitting cloth (nisīdana) made beyond the maximum allowed size is an offence of Pācittiya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "By a bhikkhu who is having a sitting-cloth made, [a sitting-cloth] which has the [proper] measure is to be made. This measure here is: two spans of the sugata-span in length, one and a half across, [and] the border is a span. For one who lets it exceed [the measure], [this is a case] involving expiation with cutting [off the cloth]."7

    The five renderings divide three ways on the one question the rule exists to answer, which is how big the thing may be. Three of them — the full Vibhaṅga translation, Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita — give the three components and leave them unadded: two handspans, one and a half, and a border of one. The condensed recitation alone gives a finished total, three by three and a half handspans.8 Ajahn Brahm gives no measure in the rule line at all and sends the reader to his commentary. The addition is not arithmetic but interpretation, and it is taken up below, under Object.

    One further divergence changes the picture a reader forms. Only Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation calls the thing a sitting mat; his own recitation joins the other three in calling it a cloth. His appendix gives the reason: nisīdana is rendered "sitting cloth" because of how the article is used now, almost always indoors and largely ceremonially, whereas the Suttas show it used outdoors as an underlay on the ground, and NP 15's nisīdanasanthata points to thick material.9

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    The rule keeps a monk's sitting mat the size of the use it was allowed for, so that a requisite meant to protect his robes and the furniture does not grow into something worn for show. The group of six used mats so large that they hung down from the beds and benches in front and behind, and the Buddha, rebuking them with "This will affect people's confidence", set a size; then Udāyī, a big monk, was seen stretching his on all sides "as if an old hide", and the Buddha met that by adding a border, not by removing the limit.10 The mat had been allowed "to protect the body, the robes, and the furniture", and Ajahn Brahm's counsel for a rule whose measure cannot be fixed is to remember that purpose and make the mat "just large enough to sit on".11​ 12

    The machinery is Pc 86's and is stated here in one line: making the mat or having it made is wrong conduct for the effort, the full offence falls when the monk receives it, the four combinations of his own hands and another's all carry it, and making one for someone else, or using one another person made, is wrong conduct.13​ 14 How long a standard handspan is, is the question at Pc 87, and every centimetre figure below rests on the smaller of the two figures Brahmāli's works support.15 Thanissaro's factors are again object, effort and intention.16

    Object

    A sitting mat larger than the right size. The Vibhaṅga defines sitting mat as "one with a border", so the border the Buddha added for Udāyī became part of the definition of the thing, and Thanissaro adds that this holds "regardless of whether it is made of felted or woven material".17​ 18 Whether NP 15's felted sitting rug therefore counts as a sitting mat under this rule too is a question the Commentary answers yes and the Sub-commentary no, on Thanissaro's report, and he takes the Commentary's side, since the Vibhaṅga defines both by their border.19 The measure is two standard handspans long, one and a half wide, and a border of one handspan.

    Disputed — how many sides carry the border, and so how large a sitting mat may be. The rule gives a border of one handspan without saying where it goes, and the answer enlarges the cloth by about a third.

    On the commentarial account, as Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual and Ajahn Brahm both report it, there are three borders, made, in Ajahn Brahm's rendering, "by cutting in two places on one side"; Thanissaro, reporting the same commentarial sentence, has the three pieces cut at one end and does not say whether they are sewn back.20​ 21​ 22 If each is a full handspan and, as the Manual sets them, they make the cloth as square as possible, the mat comes to 87.5 by 75 cm, the Manual's figure, and the one Ajahn Brahm thinks "more probable"; setting the commentary aside, a single border on each of the four sides, dasā being singular, the Manual reaches a metre by 87.5 cm. In class Brahmāli names these as the two he would follow: "either one of those I think is acceptable to follow".23 Thanissaro gives no total at all, since no text says how many sides carry a border or how the borders are patterned, and Ajahn Brahm, having stated his preference, calls it "little more than speculation" and leaves the size "a mystery".24​ 25 The texts do not settle it.

    What a monk can do with a measure the texts leave open, Thanissaro takes from the story the measure came from: make the mat large enough to sit cross-legged on without soiling the robes or the furniture, and not so large that it extends out on any side.26 That is the fault the group of six committed and the one Udāyī did not, and it is a test a monk can apply without first settling the arithmetic.

    Effort and intention

    As at Pc 86.27

    Two obligations attach to the mat but not to this rule. A monk may not be without one for four months, which the Khandhakas make wrong conduct, and Pc 60 names it among the requisites a monk may not hide from another; and he is to determine it, not assign it to another.28​ 29 How much the first makes of the mat, the sources weigh differently. Ajahn Brahm calls it a necessary requisite, noting that its older use, to sleep on, fell away once the Buddha allowed a sheet as large as a monk liked.30​ 31 Brahmāli reads the four-month limit the other way, as a sign that a requisite used mostly in the wilderness "would not have been regarded as a particularly important" one.32

    The rule's two rulings come from the two ends of one problem: monks whose mats hung off the furniture, and a large monk who could not fit on what the first ruling allowed. Between them the Buddha kept the limit and widened it, and the measure that resulted is uncertain to the centimetre and clear in its purpose. A monk who has to stretch his mat like an old hide has one that is too small, and one whose mat hangs down in front and behind has one that is too large.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • An offence of wrong conduct for the effort of making an oversize sitting mat or having one made, the full offence falling on receiving it, at which point it is to be cut down before the offence is confessed.33
    • An offence of wrong conduct where a monk makes one, or has one made, for the benefit of someone else.34
    • An offence of wrong conduct where a monk obtains one made by someone else and uses it without first cutting it down.35
    • An offence of wrong conduct, in the Khandhakas, for being without a sitting mat for four months; it belongs to the mat, not to this rule.36

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence if he makes it the right size; if he makes it smaller than the right size; if he gets one made by another that exceeds the right size and then cuts it down before using it; if he makes a canopy, a floor cover, a cloth screen, a mattress or a pillow; if he is insane; if he is the first offender.37 Thanissaro reads the fourth of these as meaning that an oversize mat may be put to one of those uses instead of being cut.38

    Related Offences

    • Pc 90 — an oversize itch-covering cloth; the same rule in every respect but the article and the measure, and the next in the chapter.
    • Pc 91, Pc 92 — the rains cloth and the robe, the remaining measured requisites of this chapter.
    • Pc 87 — a bed or bench with legs over eight standard fingerbreadths; where the length of the standard measure is argued.
    • NP 15 — a sitting blanket made without a piece of the old one; the felted form of the same article.
    • Pc 60 — hiding a monk's requisites; the sitting mat is named there among them.

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register, and both substories are given because the rule's final measure depends on the second; Ajahn Brahm's telling adds the Mahāvagga's account of how the sitting mat was first allowed, which belongs to the Khandhaka and not to this rule's narrative, and is not woven in. The Manual, the recitation, the class (session 032, 30 July 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC, Ñāṇatusita, Brahmāli's appendix of technical terms and his introduction to the nuns' rules were all checked. The commentarial account of the borders is given as the Manual and Ajahn Brahm report it, and the Commentary and Sub-commentary on the felted rug as Thanissaro reports them. The centimetre figures are the Manual's and rest on a handspan of 25 cm; the other figure Brahmāli's works support is argued at Pc 87.
