    Pc 24

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in the Jeta Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. At that time the senior monks who were instructing the nuns received robe-cloth, almsfood, dwellings, and medicinal supplies. The monks from the group of six said this about them: "The senior monks aren't instructing the nuns as a service, but for the sake of worldly gain."

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised them: "How can the monks from the group of six say that the senior monks aren't instructing the nuns to render a service, but for the sake of worldly gain?" They told the Buddha, who questioned the monks from the group of six and confirmed that it was so. He rebuked them — "Foolish men, how can you say this? This will affect people's confidence" — and laid down the training rule.1

    Synopsis — Seeing that the senior monks who instruct the nuns are well supplied, the group of six put it about that they instruct not as a service but for what they get out of it; the Buddha forbids saying so.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk says that the senior monks are instructing the nuns for the sake of worldly gain, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu says that bhikkhus are giving the ovāda to the bhikkhunīs for the sake of worldly gain, aiming to discredit them, he incurs a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu say that the bhikkhus exhort the bhikkhunīs for the sake of worldly gain, it is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Defaming those who exhort the bhikkhunīs, saying they do so for material gain is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should say so: 'The bhikkhus exhort bhikkhunīs for the sake of reward,' [this is a case] involving expiation."6

    The renderings agree on what the rule forbids and differ in three places, none of which changes it. Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga has the charge made against "the senior monks", where Thanissaro, the recitation and Ñāṇatusita have "the bhikkhus". Ñāṇatusita records the variant and sets it aside on the parallel Prātimokṣas and the text the commentator used, adding that seniority is already inside the appointment, which requires twenty years' standing.7 The recitation brings the intention up into the rule, "aiming to discredit them", and Ajahn Brahm does the same with "defaming"; the Vibhaṅga keeps it in the word-analysis, and without it the rule would seem to forbid a plain statement of fact, which it does not.8 The one word that matters is āmisa, gain. Ajahn Brahm's "material gain" reads narrower, though he glosses it to take in honour and respect; Brahmāli's "worldly gain" and Ñāṇatusita's "reward" keep the width in the word itself, discussed below under Object.9

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule restrains a monk's tongue at the point where a fellow monk's good name is most exposed, the teaching the Saṅgha has given him to do, for the harmony of the Saṅgha and the trust of those who support it. The senior monks who instructed the nuns were well supplied for it, and the group of six said that they taught not "as a service, but for the sake of worldly gain". The Buddha's rebuke looks past the monks to everyone who hears such talk: "This will affect people's confidence."10

    This is the fourth rule of the chapter, and the first to turn from giving the instruction to talk about the monks who give it. It takes its object from Pc 21, whose appointment procedure decides who counts as an exhorter at all, and its machinery from Pc 13, which penalises groundlessly disparaging an appointed Saṅgha official; Thanissaro marks the borrowing himself, giving this rule "the same intention as under Pc 13."11 Thanissaro gives the offence three factors, object, intention and effort, and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual has the same three under its own headings.12​ 13

    Object

    The full offence requires a monk whom the Saṅgha has validly appointed to instruct the nuns, and who is not in fact teaching for the sake of worldly gain.14 The gain is wider than the requisites. Alongside robe-cloth, almsfood, a dwelling and medicine, the Vibhaṅga counts honour, respect, deference, veneration and worship,15 so the rule forbids alleging that a monk teaches for the standing it brings him as much as for what he is given.

    Anything short of an appointed monk is wrong conduct: a monk who instructs the nuns without the Saṅgha's appointment, and anyone not fully ordained who instructs them, appointed or not.16 Perception does not soften this, because the Vibhaṅga's series runs on whether the appointing act was valid, not on what the speaker took it to be. If it was legitimate, the pācittiya falls whether he took it as legitimate, was unsure, or took it as illegitimate; if it was not, wrong conduct falls in all three cases.17 Thanissaro notes this as another place where the pattern set out at Pc 4 does not hold.18

    Intention

    The Vibhaṅga places the intention inside its definition of saying, in the same triple formula as Pc 13: the monk speaks "desiring to disparage him, desiring to give him a bad reputation, desiring to humiliate him."19 The Manual's "and/or" makes explicit that any one of the three is enough.20 Without the wish to bring the other monk down there is no offence, and that is why the rule leaves room for an honest report.

    Effort

    The effort is saying it: alleging that the monk instructs for any of the nine kinds of gain.21 The Vibhaṅga does not say to whom. At Pc 13 the penalty is graded by the listener, a pācittiya if he is ordained and wrong conduct if not, and there is no such grading here.22 Brahmāli reads the rule from its intention as reaching what is said to other monks rather than to the exhorter's face: "I think it is to other monks."23 That seems reasonable, since one does not damage a man's reputation by telling him to his face, and it matches the line Pc 13 draws, where an insult to the official's face falls under Pc 2. But it follows from the intention, not from anything the rule says. Nor is a false charge made to the monk himself thereby free. Deliberate lying is Pc 1, and Thanissaro names this rule among those whose penalty takes precedence over it, so a false charge that meets this rule's terms is punished here and one that does not falls back to Pc 1.24

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence in saying it of a monk who does instruct for the sake of gain.25 The Vibhaṅga's word is pakatiyā, which Brahmāli renders "regularly". Thanissaro's "actually" is looser, though at Pc 13, whose exemption is built the same way, he keeps the habitual sense.26​ 27 I read the exemption, then, as Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga gives it: it covers the monk of whom the charge is characteristically true, not the monk caught at it once.

    And it protects only a true report. Thanissaro warns that it does not apply where the speaker's belief is mistaken, so one must be sure of the facts before relying on it.28 Ajahn Brahm, in a paragraph of his own, holds instead that reasonable grounds to believe the charge are enough; the same split over Pc 13's parallel exemption is the Disputed passage there.29 The rule leaves the exhorter's conduct fully open to honest report, and protects his name from a charge that costs nothing to make and much to answer.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • A dukkaṭa where the monk spoken of is fully ordained and instructs the nuns, but has not been appointed by the Saṅgha to do so.30
    • A dukkaṭa where the person spoken of is not fully ordained, whether or not he has been appointed.31
    • A dukkaṭa where the appointing act was illegitimate, whether the speaker perceives it as legitimate, illegitimate, or is unsure.32

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence in saying it of a monk who regularly gives the instruction for the sake of robe-cloth, almsfood, a dwelling, medicinal supplies, honour, respect, deference, veneration or worship; nor if he is insane; nor if he is the first offender.33 The list of nine is the definition's own list run through a second time, so the exemption is exactly as wide as the charge.

    Related Offences

    • Pc 13 — groundlessly criticising an appointed Saṅgha official, from which this rule takes its intention formula, its perception ruling and its truth exemption, with the dispute over it.
    • Pc 21 — the chapter's anchor, and the source of this rule's object: only a monk appointed by the Saṅgha's formal act, with the eight qualifications, is protected by the full offence. Its origin story opens on the same scene, the senior monks well supplied for their teaching.
    • Pc 22, Pc 23 — the other two rules of the ovāda group, both about giving the instruction; this rule alone penalises speech about it.
    • Pc 1 — deliberate lying, which gives way to this rule for the false charge it names.
    • Pc 2 — insulting speech, for disparagement delivered to the monk's own face, not about him to others.
    • Pc 4 — the perception pattern this rule departs from: here the speaker's view of the appointment mitigates nothing.
    • Pc 76 — a groundless charge of a saṅghādisesa, another accusation that is an offence because of what it does to the monk accused.

    Related Suttas

    • AN 5.167 — Sāriputta's five things to establish before reproving another, the last of them, "I will speak with a mind of loving-kindness, not while harboring hatred", the reverse of the intention this rule penalises.34

When someone who is not fully ordained is an instructor of nuns, whether or not he has been appointed by the Sangha as such… he commits an offense of wrong conduct. If it is an illegitimate legal procedure, but he perceives it as legitimate, he commits an offense of wrong conduct."↩︎

    33. Brahmāli, Vibhaṅga translation, Pc 24, non-offences: "There is no offense: if he says it to one who regularly gives the instruction for the sake of robe-cloth, … for the sake of worship; if he is insane; if he is the first offender." His English reads "says it to"; the Manual, BMC1 and the Concise BMC take the clause as speech about the monk, and it is so read here.↩︎
