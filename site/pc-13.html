    Pc 13

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Rājagaha, in the Bamboo Grove, the squirrel sanctuary. At that time Venerable Dabba the Mallian was assigning the dwellings and designating the meals. The monks Mettiya and Bhūmajaka were newly ordained and had little merit, and so they received inferior dwellings and inferior meals. They complained about Dabba to other monks: "Dabba the Mallian assigns dwellings and designates meals based on favouritism."

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised them: "How can the monks Mettiya and Bhūmajaka complain about Venerable Dabba to other monks?" They told the Buddha, who asked the two whether it was true, and they admitted it was. He rebuked them and laid down a preliminary rule: "If a monk complains, he commits an offence entailing confession."

    Knowing now that complaining was prohibited, Mettiya and Bhūmajaka looked for another way to get the monks to hear their grievance, and criticised Dabba in the vicinity of other monks: "Dabba assigns dwellings and designates meals based on favouritism." Again the monks of few desires complained, again the Buddha rebuked them, and he revised the rule to its final form.

    Synopsis — The newly ordained monks Mettiya and Bhūmajaka, aggrieved at the poor dwellings and meals their low seniority earns them, first complain of favouritism against the Saṅgha's appointed steward Dabba the Mallian and then, once complaining is forbidden, voice the same charge as open criticism; the Buddha lays down a rule against groundlessly complaining about or criticising a Saṅgha official.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk complains or criticizes, he commits an offense entailing confession."1

    Condensed recitation translation — "Criticising or denouncing a saṅgha official without grounds is a pācittiya."2

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Criticizing or complaining (about a Community official) is to be confessed."3

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Groundlessly criticizing an officer of the Sangha in the presence of another monk is an offence of Pācittiya."4

    Ñāṇatusita

    "In making [another bhikkhu] find fault, in criticising, [there is a case] involving expiation."5

    The full rule names only two acts, and the recitation and Ajahn Brahm fold in what its analysis supplies: the object, a Saṅgha official, and "without grounds", which keeps in view the exemption for an official who is in fact biased.6​ 7 The two Pāli words are not synonyms, and the English does not hold them steady: Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation renders ujjhāpana "complains" and khiyyana "criticizes", while his recitation and Manual, like Thanissaro, put "criticising" first. By the Pāli, ujjhāpana is causative, "making (someone else) find fault", which is what Mettiya and Bhūmajaka set out to do, and khiyyana is criticising or decrying.8 The Commentary's distinction, which Thanissaro gives, falls the same way: the first is speaking of a person before others "so as to make them form a low opinion", the second is "simply to give vent".9 Both carry the same penalty, and which English word goes with which changes nothing a monk must do.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule keeps a monk from turning the Saṅgha against the monks who serve it, for its harmony and for the ease of those who take on its work. Mettiya and Bhūmajaka, newly ordained and of little merit, received the poorer dwellings and meals, and set about getting the other monks to share their grievance against Dabba; when complaining was forbidden, they found another way to the same end. Thanissaro names what is at stake for the Saṅgha: a steward who follows standard procedure will sometimes seem biased when he is not, and "if he cannot receive the benefit of the doubt from his fellow bhikkhus, there is no incentive for him to undertake these duties in the first place."10

    Thanissaro gives the offence three factors: object, intention and effort, noting that the Vibhaṅga folds the intention into its definition of the effort.11 Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual lists four, the official, the listener, the motive, and criticising "without a reason".12 Thanissaro's three serve here, with the want of a reason placed under Object, where the Vibhaṅga places the exemption: in the official's own conduct.

    Object

    The official is a monk the Saṅgha has appointed by a formal act to a distributive duty: assigning dwellings, designating meals, or distributing congee, fruit, fresh food or minor requisites.13 Thanissaro sets out three conditions for the full offence. The official must be ordained, properly authorised, and not one who habitually acts out of the four kinds of bias: desire, aversion, delusion or fear.14 An ordained official never authorised, or improperly authorised, and an unordained person acting as one, authorised or not, are grounds for a dukkaṭa only.15 Ajahn Brahm adds a widening for the modern monastery, where these duties are usually given by informal agreement: he judges it appropriate to treat such officers as formally appointed ones, so that groundlessly criticising them is also the pācittiya.16 Whether a bhikkhunī official counts as ordained for the full offence is the question met at Pc 2: the Commentary, Thanissaro reports, reads bhikkhus only, and the Manual takes the word at its full width, "As with pācittiya 2".17​ 18

    Perception is not a factor, which makes the rule exacting. If the official was properly authorised, the pācittiya stands whether the critic took his authorisation to be proper, improper or doubtful; as Thanissaro puts it, the pattern set out under Pc 4 does not hold.19 Where the bias is real, a complaint of it is in effect a charge that the official's appointment was invalid, which the Saṅgha is then bound to examine.20

    Disputed — whether a monk who sincerely believes the official biased is covered by the exemption.

    The Vibhaṅga exempts criticising an official who regularly acts out of favouritism, ill will, confusion or fear, and its perception grid reaches only the appointing procedure.21​ 22 Thanissaro carries the perception ruling to this condition as well: disappointment and anger colour what a monk sees, and one thoroughly convinced of bias is still guilty of an offence if the official's behaviour was in fact fair.23 Ajahn Brahm holds the opposite: "If one believes that there are valid grounds for finding fault with someone, then it is no offence to mention this to another", though even then a monk should weigh his motive, and whether he is "genuinely concerned for their progress and welfare."24 The Vibhaṅga does not say which it means, and on Thanissaro's reading a monk must be sure of the facts, not only of his conviction, before he speaks.

    Intention

    The Vibhaṅga writes the motive into the effort itself: the monk speaks "desiring to disparage him, desiring to give him a bad reputation, desiring to humiliate him".25 Thanissaro's summary is that the aim is to make the official "lose face, lose status, or feel abashed".26 A critical remark made simply to tell the truth does not fulfil it.27

    Effort

    To complain about or criticise a qualifying official to an ordained person is the pācittiya; to do so to one who is not ordained is a dukkaṭa.28 Ajahn Brahm carries the rule a step further down: groundlessly criticising a monk who holds no office, a novice or a lay person, before anyone, is a dukkaṭa, so that disparaging gossip may often be an offence.29 Thanissaro reaches the same penalty from Pc 9: a monk who uses that rule's allowance, reporting only the action or only the class of another's offence, "to discredit a fellow bhikkhu" incurs a dukkaṭa under this rule.30 The Vibhaṅga's own dukkaṭas concern only officials and unordained listeners; Ajahn Brahm cites its dukkaṭa section without saying how it reaches further, and Thanissaro names no clause. Said to the official's own face, simply to hurt him, the same words are an offence under Pc 2, whether or not his conduct was in fact biased.31

    The rule asks a monk to give the Saṅgha's stewards the benefit of the doubt, and Thanissaro closes on the reason a monk can afford to. The Buddha likened material gains to excrement, "and when excrement is shared out there is rarely any point in complaining about who gets the choicest portions."32

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • A dukkaṭa for complaining about or criticising a properly appointed, unbiased Saṅgha official to one who is not ordained.33
    • A dukkaṭa for complaining about or criticising an ordained official who was never authorised or was improperly authorised, or an unordained person acting as an official, to anyone, ordained or not.34
    • A dukkaṭa, by Ajahn Brahm's reading, for groundlessly criticising a monk who holds no office, a novice or a lay person; and, by Thanissaro's, for using Pc 9's allowance to discredit a fellow monk.35​ 36

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence in complaining about or criticising an official who regularly acts out of favouritism, ill will, confusion or fear; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.37 On Thanissaro's reading the first exemption depends on the fact of the bias, not on the critic's belief in it; Ajahn Brahm's differs (the Disputed passage above, under Object).

    Related Offences

    • Pc 12 — the preceding rule, built in the same grammatical form: there, obstructing the Saṅgha's inquiry into one's own offence; here, disparaging without grounds the officials who run its daily distribution.
    • Pc 9 — using that rule's allowance to report only the action, or only the class, of another's grave offence in order to discredit him is a dukkaṭa under this rule, by Thanissaro's reading.
    • Pc 2 — criticising an official to his face, purely to hurt him, is the insult of Pc 2, whether or not his conduct was biased; this rule governs the same disparagement made about him to others.
    • Pc 24 — accusing the Saṅgha's appointed instructor of the bhikkhunīs of teaching for the sake of gain. That rule borrows this one's machinery almost entire: the disparage, bad-reputation and humiliate formula word for word, the properly appointed object, the perception ruling, and a truth exemption of the same construction; Thanissaro glosses its intention as "the same intention as under Pc 13."
    • Sg 8, Sg 9 — the same aggrieved pair, Mettiya and Bhūmajaka, and the same target, Dabba the Mallian: there they turn the grievance into a groundless charge of pārājika, a saṅghādisesa; here the lesser form of the same resentment, groundless criticism of his stewardship, is a pācittiya.

    Related Suttas

    • AN 5.196 — the bodhisatta's dream of climbing a mountain of dung without being soiled by it, a sign that he would use his requisites "without being tied to them": the gain over which Mettiya and Bhūmajaka turned on the steward.38

    A note on sourcing

    The Story, the rule, the definition of the appointed official, the permutations and the non-offence clause are from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation, and the recitation line from his Pātimokkha recitation translation. Thanissaro's BMC1 supplies the three factors and their sub-conditions, the perception ruling, the Commentary's distinction between the two words and its reading of "ordained", both given as he reports them, the caution about being sure of the facts, the Pc 9 and Pc 2 cross-references and the closing observation on the steward's thankless work. The Manual gives Brahmāli's four factors and his reading of "ordained" as including bhikkhunīs. Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes adds the informally appointed officer, the dukkaṭa for disparaging gossip and the counsel to weigh one's motive. The Concise BMC was checked; beyond BMC1 it adds only a pointer to Pc 81, which is not carried. Ñāṇatusita gives the rule translation and the causative sense of ujjhāpana. The class transcript (session 022, 3 May 2012) carries no discussion of this rule; the recording lapses after Pc 11. AN 5.196 is quoted from Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation; the two suttas the parallels list offers concern evasion under questioning and belong to Pc 12.
