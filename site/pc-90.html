    Pc 90

    Story (Vatthu)

    At one time the Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. He had allowed itch-covering cloths for the monks.1

    Knowing this, the monks from the group of six wore itch covers that were inappropriate in size. As they were walking about, they were dragging them along, both in front and behind. The monks of few desires complained and criticised those monks: "How can the monks from the group of six wear such itch covers?"

    The Buddha asked those monks whether it was true, and they said that it was. He rebuked them: "Foolish men, how can you do this? This will affect people's confidence." After rebuking them, he laid down the rule.2

    Synopsis — the group of six wear itch cloths so long that the ends drag as they walk, and the rule that follows caps the cloth at four standard handspans by two.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk is having an itch-covering cloth made, it should be made the right size. This is the right size: four standard handspans long and two wide. If it exceeds that, it is to be cut down, and he commits an offense entailing confession."3

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu makes a bandage larger than four by two standard hand-spans, or has one made, it should be cut down to size and he incurs a pācittiya."4

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "When a bhikkhu is having a skin-eruption covering cloth made, it is to be made to the standard measurement. Here the standard is this: four spans—using the sugata span—in length, two spans in width. In excess of that, it is to be cut down and confessed."5

    Brahm

    "Having an itch-cloth (kandupaticchādi) made beyond the maximum allowable size is an offence of Pācittiya."6

    Ñāṇatusita

    "By a bhikkhu who is having an itch-covering [-cloth] made, [an itch-covering] which has the [proper] measure is to be made. This measure here is: four spans of the Sugata-span in length, two spans across. For one who lets it exceed [the measure], [this is a case] involving expiation with cutting off [the cloth]."7

    Nothing here divides the renderings as the border divided them at Pc 89: the four that give a measure give the same one — Ajahn Brahm's rule line, as at Pc 89, gives none — and the only substantive difference is what they call the thing. Kaṇḍu means an itch, and Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation, Thanissaro, Ajahn Brahm and Ñāṇatusita keep it, Thanissaro in the broader "skin-eruption" — "itch-covering cloth", "skin-eruption covering cloth", "itch-cloth", "itch-covering". The condensed recitation drops it, giving "bandage".8 That is not a liberty but a reading of the Vibhaṅga, which defines the cloth by four conditions of which an itch is only the first; the point is taken up below, under Object. "Bandage" can mislead, since the article is worn rather than applied.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    A cloth allowed for sickness is to be the size sickness needs, and the rule keeps it there, so that a monk's care for his body stays a private necessity and does not become one more thing he is seen trailing behind him. The cloth was allowed so that a monk with sores below the waist would not soil his robes; the group of six wore theirs so large that the ends dragged in front and behind as they walked, and the Buddha rebuked them with "This will affect people's confidence" before setting a size.9​ 10

    This rule is Pc 89 again with a different cloth and a different number, and its machinery is Pc 86's: making the cloth or having it made is wrong conduct for the effort, the full offence falls when the monk receives it, it must be cut down before he confesses, the four combinations of his own hands and another's all carry the full offence, and making one for someone else, or using one another person made, is wrong conduct.11​ 12 How long a standard handspan is, is the question at Pc 87. Two things are this rule's own: what the cloth is for, and how it is held.

    Object

    An itch-covering cloth longer than four standard handspans or wider than two. The Vibhaṅga defines the article by its purpose and its place on the body: it is "for the purpose of covering an itch or a boil or a running sore or a carbuncle, below the navel and above the knees".13 Thanissaro draws the inference from the second half: a cloth confined to that band is meant to be worn as an inner garment, beneath the lower robe, and at his reckoning of the handspan the limit comes to a metre by 50 centimetres.14

    The name of the cloth is narrower than the thing it names. Kaṇḍu, the first word of the compound, is an itch, and three of the four conditions are not itches. The four have in common what the article is for, which Thanissaro gives as "to protect their robes".15

    This rule carries only one practical thing that Pc 89 does not. Because the cloth belongs to an illness and not to a monk's standing kit, it is not simply determined and kept: the Khandhakas have him determine it for as long as he is sick and assign it to another the rest of the time, and Thanissaro points to NP 1 for how that is done.16​ 17 It is the arrangement of the rains cloth at Pc 91, a cloth for an occasion, held under one's own determination while the occasion lasts.

    The rule is quickly stated, and it has fallen out of use; Ajahn Brahm puts this down to effective medicines.18 What has not gone is the judgement behind the size. A cloth that covers a man from navel to knee is exactly enough to keep the discharge off a robe, and one whose ends drag in the dirt as he walks is no greater kindness to himself and a smaller one to everyone watching, which is the complaint the story records and the reason the rule sets a number at all.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • An offence of wrong conduct for the effort of making an oversize itch-covering cloth or having one made, the full offence falling on receiving it, at which point it is to be cut down before the offence is confessed.19
    • An offence of wrong conduct where a monk makes one, or has one made, for the benefit of someone else.20
    • An offence of wrong conduct where a monk obtains one made by someone else and uses it without first cutting it down.21

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    As at Pc 89, and in the same words: there is no offence if he makes it the right size; if he makes it smaller; if he gets one made by another that exceeds the size and cuts it down before using it; if he makes a canopy, a floor cover, a cloth screen, a mattress or a pillow; if he is insane; if he is the first offender.22

    Related Offences

    • Pc 89 — an oversize sitting mat; the anchor for this rule's measure, penalty and non-offence clause.
    • Pc 91 — an oversize rains cloth; the third of the measured cloths, held the same way as this one.
    • Pc 87 — where the length of a standard handspan is argued.
    • NP 1 — the determination and assignment of robe-cloth, under which this cloth is held when its owner is well.
    • Bhikkhunīs' Pc 165 — the same rule for the bhikkhunīs, its wording changed only for gender. Their Pātimokkha has no counterpart to Pc 89, and Brahmāli's introduction to the nuns' rules gives the reason: the sitting mat was a requisite used mostly in the wilderness, where nuns did not live.23​ 24

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register. Ajahn Brahm prefixes the Mahāvagga's account of Venerable Belaṭṭhasīsa, whose sores were the occasion for the cloth being allowed at all; that belongs to the Khandhaka, not to this rule's narrative, and is not woven in. Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual, the recitation, the class (session 032, 30 July 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were all checked, and the nuns' rule was read in Brahmāli's translation of their Pātimokkha. I treat this rule as a lean mirror of Pc 89, carrying only what is its own.
