    Upananda the Sakyan

    No single monk stands behind more rules than Upananda. Brahmāli names his fault in a sentence, "His main defilement was an excessive greed for robes and cloth", and puts his centre of gravity where it belongs, in the Nissaggiyas: NP 6, 8, 9, 10, 18, 20, 25 and 27 are his.1 The Pācittiyas add a run of five in a row, Pc 42 to Pc 46, and four more besides.

    The Nissaggiya stories are nearly one story told eight ways, and the people in them say the same thing again and again: "These Sakyan monastics have great desires. They're not content."2 A merchant's son who had invited him to say what he needed offered to send a wrap from home, and Upananda asked three times for the one he was wearing: "What's the point of inviting me if you don't want to give?" (NP 6). Hearing from another monk that a householder meant to give him cloth, he went and told the man what cloth to buy, "For what's the point of giving robe-cloth that I won't use?" (NP 8), and then did the same to two householders at once (NP 9). Told by a steward to wait one day for his robe-cloth because the householders' association fined latecomers fifty coins, he took the man by the belt, and the man paid the fine (NP 10). Offered the price of a meal a family's son had eaten, he asked for the coin itself (NP 18). He would not undo a trade a wanderer regretted: "But didn't I ask you if you were sure?" (NP 20). And he told a weaver to make a supporter's cloth long, wide and closely woven, so that the man's wife had to give the thread a second time (NP 27).3

    Brahmāli adds that "Upananda, too, enjoyed the company of women", and the Pācittiyas bear him out.4 At Pc 43 he sat on in a friend's bedroom while the husband asked him three times to leave and the wife, who could see what her husband wanted, asked him three times to stay; at Pc 44 and Pc 45 he sat with a friend's wife in private, first on a concealed seat and then alone. The last of the five, Pc 46, is about something else: a family that had prepared a meal for him kept the other invited monks waiting through a morning while he called on other supporters.5

    One thread runs through four of his rules. Each time, the monk who bears the cost is the same: his brother's student. Upananda gave him a robe so that he would go wandering with him, and when the young monk chose to go with the Buddha instead, took it back in anger (NP 25). He took him on almsround and sent him away before he had been given anything, because he wanted to talk and sit alone, and the young monk missed his meal (Pc 42). He assigned him a robe and went on wearing it (Pc 59). And having committed the offence of intentional emission of semen, he told him and added, "Don't tell anyone" (Pc 64).6 The Vibhaṅga names him with the same phrase each time, bhātuno saddhivihārika, "his brother's student". In three of the stories it is the young monk who goes to the other monks with what has happened; in the fourth his keeping quiet is itself the offence the rule names. An offence of the same kind as Pc 64's stands behind Pc 9, where Upananda was on probation for it and sat on the last seat in the dining hall, and the group of six told the lay followers why.7

    What his rules show together is how one craving reaches into everything a monk touches. It begins with cloth and moves on to money, to the families who support him, to their wives, to his junior's meal and his junior's robe, and at last to his junior's silence. Brahmāli finds this realistic: "Strong defilements are difficult to overcome and so it is not unreasonable to think that a single person could be behind such a large number of offenses."8 His rules also show the cost falling on others. In many of his stories the complaint comes from a layperson who has been pressed, overcharged or embarrassed, and the rule protects the monk from becoming what that layperson saw.

    The story a monk is likely to remember is the shortest. At Pc 87, the rule on the height of a bed's legs, Upananda saw the Buddha coming past his dwelling and called out, "Sir, please come and see my bed." The Buddha turned around and said to the monks, "A fool, monks, can be recognized by his sleeping place," and then "spoke in dispraise of being difficult to support".9
