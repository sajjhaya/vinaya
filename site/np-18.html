    NP 18

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Rājagaha, in the Bamboo Grove, the squirrel sanctuary. Venerable Upananda was at that time associating with a family from which he received a regular meal. Whenever that family obtained food, they put aside a portion for him, and so they did one evening when they obtained some meat.

    The following morning their son got up early and cried, "Give me meat!" The man said to his wife, "Give him the venerable's portion. We'll buy something else for the venerable."

    That same morning Upananda robed up, took his bowl and robe, and went to the family, where he sat down on the prepared seat. The man of the house approached him, bowed, sat down, and said, "Yesterday evening, venerable, we obtained some meat, and we put aside a portion for you. But then in the morning our son got up early and cried, 'Give me meat!' and we gave him your portion. What can we get you for a kahāpaṇa?"

    "Are you giving up a kahāpaṇa coin for me?"

    "Yes."

    "Then just give me that kahāpaṇa."

    After giving a kahāpaṇa to Upananda, the man complained and criticised him: "The Sakyan monastics accept money just as we do."

    The monks heard the man's complaint, and the monks of few desires complained and criticised Upananda: "How could Venerable Upananda receive money?" After rebuking him in many ways, they told the Buddha. Soon afterwards he had the Saṅgha gathered, questioned Upananda, and rebuked him: "Foolish man, how could you do this? This will affect people's confidence." Then he laid down the rule.1

    Synopsis — Upananda, offered the price of a missed meal, asks for the coin itself and takes it, and the rule against a monk receiving money follows.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk takes, has someone else take, or consents to gold, silver, or money being deposited for him, he commits an offense entailing relinquishment and confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "If a bhikkhu becomes the owner of money or gold, he incurs a nissaggiya pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Should any bhikkhu accept gold and silver, or have it accepted, or consent to its being deposited (near him), it is to be forfeited and confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 1)

    "Accepting gold or money for oneself is a nissaggiya pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "If any bhikkhu should take gold and silver, or should have [it] taken, or should consent to [it] being deposited [for him], [this is a case] involving expiation with forfeiture."6

    The object, jātarūparajata, is literally "gold and silver", and Brahmāli renders it "gold, silver, or money" because the Vibhaṅga defines the silver as coins, "whatever is used in commerce."7 The full translation, Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita keep the rule's three verbs, and Ñāṇatusita gives sādiyeyya as "consent to" here, since uggaṇhāti already means accepting.8 Two renderings build in a reading. The recitation folds the three verbs into "becomes the owner", the reading Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual gives its factors; Ajahn Brahm's "for oneself" writes the Commentary's intention factor into the rule. Both readings are disputed, and Object and Effort take them up.9

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    This rule keeps a monk's renunciation of money whole; the householder in the Story looked for it in Upananda and did not find it. Offered a meal and asked for the coin instead, he complained that "the Sakyan monastics accept money just as we do"; the Buddha's rebuke was that "this will affect people's confidence."10 The suttas give the Buddha's own reason, in his answer to the headman Maṇicūḷaka: whoever agrees to gold or money also agrees to the five strands of sensual pleasure, and that "is not the way of a recluse."11 Thanissaro adds a purpose from the monk's side: the rule relieves him of the burden of owning what is given, and in giving up money he makes his renunciation of worldly pursuits real and shows others "that the struggle for wealth is not the true way to find happiness."12

    Thanissaro gives two factors, object and effort, and since the object is defined one way for the first two acts and another for the third, he treats the rule as two related offences: accepting money or having it accepted, and consenting to money intended for oneself being deposited.13 The Manual reads the acts as ways of becoming the owner of money.14 I take Thanissaro's factors, which keep the three acts apart, and give the Manual's ownership reading where it bears on the question, in the second of the disputes below.

    Object

    Gold and money. The Vibhaṅga defines gold as "that which has the color of the Teacher", and silver as the coins of the day, copper, wooden or resin, "whatever is used in commerce"; the Commentary, as Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm both report it, extends this to bone, hide, fruit and seeds used as currency, stamped or not.15​ 16 The test is function, and the Manual states it in a phrase: anything "used as a direct means of payment."17 Thanissaro gives the marks of a currency as a generally accepted medium, of recognised value, presentable by any bearer, so that coins, notes, and money orders and cashier's cheques not made out to a payee are money, while cheques, credit and debit cards, travellers' cheques, gift cards and the like are not.18 Ajahn Brahm's test comes to the same: money is what can be taken into almost any shop and exchanged as it stands, "without any further 'ink-work'", and the cheque or card is only "potential money". What a monk then does with it divides them. Ajahn Brahm puts cashing a cheque under NP 19 and buying with a card on his own account under NP 20; Thanissaro, noting "some controversy", holds that a monk who cashes a cheque, or orders someone else to, offends under this rule.19​ 20

    Two things lie outside the object. By long practice, gold images and gold given to an image, a relic or a stūpa are not taken to fulfil it, probably because none of these can be owned, and a monk who understands that he assumes no ownership may handle them.21 And the Commentary, Thanissaro reports, lists things that carry wrong conduct only when accepted, pearls and precious stones, raw grain, slaves, fields and the like; Ajahn Brahm holds that the gems among them are valuables under Pc 84, where that argument belongs.22​ 23

    Disputed — whether money received unknowingly is the offence.

    The Vibhaṅga's permutations make no exception: if it is money, the full offence falls whether he perceives it as money, is unsure, or does not perceive it as money.24 Thanissaro reads this at face value. A monk who accepts a closed envelope or a bolt of cloth not knowing money is inside commits the full offence, which may seem harsh, "but we must remember that, having received the money even unknowingly, he is now in possession of it and must dispose of it in a proper way."25 Ajahn Brahm agrees for an article accepted on purpose, while holding that money slipped into his bowl while his attention was elsewhere is no offence, the receiving being unintentional.26 The Manual dissents: to make a scrupulous monk fall for taking a parcel he believed held no money "almost makes a mockery of the rule, for clearly it is intentionally receiving money that is wrong"; he becomes the owner of the contents only when he knows what they are, and where in doubt he should ask the donor.27 The permutations sit with Thanissaro, and the Manual's reading with what the rule is for. I find the Manual's argument the more persuasive, but it reads past the permutations, and the permutations are the text. A monk who asks the donor, as the Manual advises, before accepting a parcel he cannot see into is safe on the Manual's reading; on Thanissaro's, asking protects him only if the answer is true.

    Effort

    Any of three acts.

    Accepting. Taking money as a gift or picking up ownerless money, with the body or with something connected to it, so that money in an envelope, dropped into his bowl or slipped into his bag as it hangs from his shoulder is accepted.28 Thanissaro reports the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī as requiring some movement of the money, and sees its use: a monk on almsround whose donor puts money in his bowl over his protest may simply stand until the donor or someone else removes it.29

    Having it accepted. Telling anyone to take it for him: telling the donor to give it to a steward, telling the steward to take it or to bank it. Thanissaro, from the commentaries, adds telling him to "do what he thinks appropriate"; Ajahn Brahm, of money a donor has left on a table, counts those same words as short of a command. Anything short of a command does not fulfil the factor; naming the steward, or saying where a donation box stands, is not having it accepted.30​ 31 Where the line between a hint and a command falls, and how a fund for a monk is properly set up, is the procedure of NP 10.

    Consenting to its deposit. The donor sets money down saying, "This is for you", or tells him money of his is deposited somewhere, "it is yours", and he consents.32​ 33 Consenting is not refusing, and the Commentary, as Thanissaro reports it, and Ajahn Brahm count a refusal by any of three doors: by word, saying it is not allowable; by deed, a gesture; or by thought alone, "This is not proper for me."34​ 35 The thought counts the other way too: money set beside a monk as his, which he does not refuse even in thought, he has consented to.36 The donor's intention matters here as nowhere else in the rule: if the money is meant for the Saṅgha or for another monk, a monk who consents to its being set down by him incurs wrong conduct only, by the Commentary in Thanissaro's report; Ajahn Brahm holds it no offence, since no text names it.37​ 38 Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm both advise saying the refusal aloud where the donor will take it well; Ajahn Brahm adds that his faith is likely to grow when he sees a monk caring more about the Vinaya than about money.39​ 40

    Ajahn Brahm gives the case that shows how this act works at a distance. A monk told he has inherited money held by lawyers, who neither speaks nor gestures nor resolves against it, has consented and commits the offence; if he tells the lawyers that a monk may not own money, and suggests that the inheritance be held by a steward for his benefit, he commits none, and may use the fund as NP 10 allows.41 Silence, on this act, is consent.

    Disputed — whether the full offence needs money intended for the monk himself.

    The factor comes from the commentaries: they add intention, Thanissaro reports, the full offence falling only on money taken for one's own sake.42 Ajahn Brahm gives the Samantapāsādikā's wrong conduct for money accepted for another.43 The Manual cites the Kaṅkhāvitaraṇī's factor "intended for oneself" in support of its own, becoming the owner, since no one can relinquish money that belongs to someone else.44 Ajahn Brahm holds the same factor, the offence falling "only when the monk intends that gold or money to be for his own use."45 Thanissaro rejects it: the monk's intention appears in none of the Vibhaṅga's three acts, the donor's in only the third, and the non-offence clauses allow no monk to accept money for others.46 None of Thanissaro, Ajahn Brahm or the Commentary allows a monk to take money for another or to have it taken. Ajahn Brahm makes accepting it a pācittiya under Pc 84, against the Samantapāsādikā's wrong conduct, and Thanissaro the nissaggiya pācittiya of this rule.47 What divides them is the penalty, not the permission, and the texts do not settle it: the factor is well attested in the commentaries and has no warrant in the Vibhaṅga's words. A monk asked to take money for the monastery has the same answer as one offered money for himself.

    Procedure

    Money is relinquished in the midst of the Saṅgha, not to a group or an individual, and is not given back, since there is no way a monk may own it.48​ 49 If a monastery worker or lay follower is at hand, he is told, "Look into this"; if he asks what he should get, he is not told "get this or that", but what is allowable may be pointed out, ghee, oil, honey or syrup, and whatever allowable thing he brings may be used by everyone except the monk who received the money.50 Ajahn Brahm reports how far the Samantapāsādikā takes that exception: the monk may not read by the light of a lamp burning that oil, nor sit in the shade of a building raised with that money.51 If the layman will not buy or discard it, the Saṅgha appoints a money-discarder by motion and announcement, a monk free of bias through desire, ill will, confusion or fear, who throws it away without noting where; if he notes where, he commits wrong conduct. The Commentary, as Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm both report it, has him close his eyes and throw it into a river, over a cliff or into a thicket, "as if it were excrement."52​ 53 Money already lost or stolen cannot be relinquished, and Ajahn Brahm has the monk inform the Saṅgha and confess.54

    Non-offences (Anāpatti)

    Money found within a monastery or a lodging may be put aside for its owner, which is where Pc 84 takes over.55 Ajahn Brahm adds that money a monk owned before his ordination is not accepted under this rule, though he may not buy with it; it is better made into a fund held by a steward, or given away, before he is ordained.56

    Upananda was offered a meal worth a kahāpaṇa and asked for the kahāpaṇa. The rule asks a monk to want the meal, and leaves the coin with the giver.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — accepting, or consenting to the deposit of, something that is not money while perceiving it as money or unsure.57
    • Dukkaṭa — consenting to money meant for the Saṅgha or for another monk being set down by him (Commentary; Ajahn Brahm holds it no offence).58​ 59
    • Dukkaṭa — accepting money for another, on the Samantapāsādikā's reading in Ajahn Brahm's report (he gives Pc 84's pācittiya instead); and accepting one of the things the Commentary lists as carrying wrong conduct.60​ 61
    • Dukkaṭa — the money-discarder who takes note of where he throws it.62

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if, within a monastery or a lodging, he takes it or has someone take it, and he then puts it aside with the thought, "Whoever owns it will come and get it;" if he is insane; if he is the first offender.63

    Related Offences

    • NP 10 — the steward and the fund by which a monk benefits from money he does not own, with the line between a hint and a command.
    • NP 19 — trading in money, where the money is again forfeited to the Saṅgha and not returned.
    • NP 20 — buying and selling, where a monk who uses his own money to buy falls.
    • Pc 84 — picking up a valuable, which takes over where this rule's safekeeping exemption leaves off, and under which Ajahn Brahm places money accepted for another.

    Related Suttas

    • SN 42.10 — the Buddha's answer to the headman Maṇicūḷaka: whoever agrees to gold or money also agrees to the five strands of sensual pleasure; money is not a neutral thing to a monk, for it brings the whole of sensual pleasure with it.64
    • DN 1 §1.10 — among the virtues for which the worldling praises the Tathāgata, "He avoids accepting gold and silver"; the restraint by which even a worldling knows him, and what the householder in the Story looked for in Upananda.65
    • AN 4.50 — the third of the defilements "because of which some ascetics and brahmins do not shine, blaze, and radiate": they "accept gold and silver and do not refrain from receiving gold and silver". It names the danger this rule guards as a defilement of the renunciant.66

    A note on sourcing

    The Story, definitions, permutations, relinquishment and non-offence clauses are from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation, with his appendix of technical terms for jātarūparajata; the Manual gives the ownership reading, the direct means of payment, the factor of intention and the dissent on unknowing receipt. Thanissaro's BMC1 gives the purpose, the two offences, the marks of a currency, the images, the three acts, the donor's intention and the forfeiture; Ajahn Brahm's Vinaya Notes gives potential money, the inheritance, unintentional receipt, money accepted for another, the disposal, lost money and money owned before ordination. The Commentary is cited as Thanissaro, Ajahn Brahm and the Manual report it; Ñāṇatusita for his rendering and sādiyeyya. SN 42.10 is quoted in Ajahn Brahm's translation, no permitted English being held, DN 1 in Walshe's and AN 4.50 in Bodhi's. The Concise BMC adds nothing, and the class is cited once, for consent by thought.
