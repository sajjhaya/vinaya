    Pc 52

    Story (Vatthu)

    The Buddha was staying at Sāvatthī in Anāthapiṇḍika's Monastery. The monks from the group of six tickled one of the monks from the group of seventeen to make him laugh. Not being able to catch his breath, he died.

    The monks of few desires complained and criticised them, "How could the monks from the group of six tickle a monk to make him laugh?" The Buddha questioned them, established that it was true, rebuked them, and stated the rule.1

    Synopsis — Three sentences, among the shortest origin stories in the chapter, and a death in the second of them. Nothing is said about how the tickling began or how long it lasted, and the rule that follows does not mention the death either: it forbids the tickling.

    Translation (Mātikā)

    Brahmāli

    Full Vibhaṅga translation — "If a monk tickles someone, he commits an offense entailing confession."2

    Condensed recitation translation — "Tickling a bhikkhu is a pācittiya."3

    Thanissaro (BMC1)

    "Tickling with the fingers is to be confessed."4

    Brahm (Vinaya Notes, vol. 2)

    "Tickling another monk with any part of one's body is an offence of Pācittiya."5

    Ñāṇatusita

    "In tickling with the fingers, [there is a case] involving expiation."6

    The rule's word names the fingers: aṅgulipatodaka, poking or tickling with the fingers, from the root that means to prick.7​ 8 Thanissaro and Ñāṇatusita keep them. Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation and recitation line drop them ("tickles someone", "Tickling a bhikkhu"), and Ajahn Brahm puts the opposite in their place, "with any part of one's body".9 Neither choice is a slip. The fingers are in the rule and not in the word-analysis, which defines the offence as body-to-body contact of any kind, so a rendering that keeps them follows the rule and one that drops them follows the analysis.10 I keep "tickling with the fingers", which is what a monk hears recited and keeps the rule and its analysis distinct; how far the analysis widens it is taken up under Effort.

    Explanation (Padabhājanīya)

    Play has no natural stopping place for the one on the receiving end, and this rule keeps a monk's hands from it, for the sake of his own restraint and the ease of the monks he lives among. The origin story is three sentences long, and a death is in the second: the group of six tickled one of the monks of the group of seventeen until, unable to catch his breath, he died.11 The rule that follows does not mention the death. It forbids the tickling.

    Thanissaro gives three factors, object, effort and intention, and Brahmāli's Pātimokkha Manual folds them into one: touching another monk with the intention of making him laugh.12​ 13

    Object

    A fully ordained monk is grounds for a pācittiya, and anyone else for a dukkaṭa. Perception does not mitigate, the pattern Thanissaro refers to Pc 42.14​ 15 Whether "anyone else" includes a bhikkhunī the Vibhaṅga does not say. Thanissaro reports the Commentary saying that it does, so that tickling her in play would be no more than a dukkaṭa, and he writes the line off as one of the Commentary's "occasional attempts at humor"; the Concise BMC reads the unordained person here as a male.16​ 17 Nothing on either reading permits a monk to touch a woman: contact with lust is Sg 2.

    Effort

    The word-analysis is a single sentence: the offence is body-to-body contact with another fully ordained monk, aimed at making him laugh.18 The fingers have gone, and Thanissaro takes the factor as body-to-body contact as Sg 2 defines it, any part of one's body against any part of his.19​ 20 Contact that is less than body to body draws a dukkaṭa whoever the other person is: poking with a stick, touching something connected with the other's body, or tossing or dropping things on him, in Thanissaro's examples, or tickling with a feather, in Ajahn Brahm's.21​ 22​ 23

    Intention

    Intention carries the rule, and of the three it alone can be absent innocently: there is no offence "if he is not aiming to make him laugh".24 Thanissaro and Ajahn Brahm reach for the same illustration. A monk massaging another's tired back who touches a ticklish spot by accident commits no offence.25​ 26 The offence lies in setting out to make him laugh, not in the touch and not in the laughter; and touching another monk in anger is a different rule altogether, Pc 74.27

    The same death stands among the cases of Pj 3, where it is ruled no offence entailing expulsion, since, as Ajahn Brahm explains, the monks had no intention to kill.28 What the rule forbids is the thing they did intend: the game itself.

    Derived Offences (Āpattibheda)

    • Dukkaṭa — tickling anyone who is not fully ordained, whatever the perception; a bhikkhunī is included on the Commentary's reading, which Thanissaro gives.29​ 30
    • Dukkaṭa — contact with one's body against something connected to his; with something connected to one's body against his body or against something connected to it; or by releasing something.31
    • Dukkaṭa — tickling a novice, which Thanissaro pairs with the Khandhaka's dukkaṭa for smacking a novice with a tooth cleaner: "Even playful rough-housing is forbidden."32

    Exemptions (Anāpatti)

    There is no offence: if he is not aiming to make him laugh; if he makes physical contact with him when there is a need; if he is insane; or if he is the first offender.33

    Related Offences

    • Pj 3 — killing a human being: the same event appears among its cases, ruled no offence entailing expulsion.
    • Sg 2 — lustful bodily contact with a woman: the offence that governs the same act done with lust.
    • Pc 74 — striking a monk in anger: contact distinguished from this rule's by its motive.
    • Pc 53, Pc 55 — playing in water and trying to frighten a monk: the neighbouring rules against horseplay between monks.

    Related Suttas

    • AN 8.86 — monks "laughing and playing by poking one another with the fingers" (aṅgulipatodaka, the rule's own word), and the Buddha's judgement that they do not gain at will the bliss of renunciation.34

    A note on sourcing

    The Story is from Brahmāli's Vibhaṅga translation alone, in the de-dramatised register; the complaint is verbatim. Ajahn Brahm adds that the monk fainted, and the Story follows the Vibhaṅga. The Manual, the recitation, the class transcript (session 028, 12 June 2012), Thanissaro's BMC1 and BMC2, Ajahn Brahm, the Concise BMC and Ñāṇatusita were checked, with the case in Pj 3's analysis and AN 8.86 in Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation. The class is not cited: its points on the fingers and the massage are carried by the written sources. The Commentary is known here only through Thanissaro.
